Graphics Handbook

A visual handbook that takes someone new to graphics from “what is a frame?” to reading a renderer's feature list with confidence. It is written for a game lead who will direct AI to do much of the hands-on work.

Who it is for

The reader is sharp, comfortable working with AI tools, and has never opened a renderer. They will lead or build a game where AI agents write much of the code and content. Their job is to know what to ask for, judge what comes back, and make the trade-off calls.

That shapes the whole book. It teaches recognition and judgement first: what a technique is, why it exists, what it costs, and how it fails. Hand-writing the code comes last and stays optional.

After the handbook, the reader can

  • Follow one asset from an artist's tool to a lit pixel and name every stage it passes through.
  • Explain what a draw call, vertex buffer, shader, G-buffer and render pass are, and what each one costs.
  • Decode a feature list or a settings menu: SSAO against HBAO against GTAO, SSR, SSGI, CSM, TAA, DLSS, Lumen, Nanite.
  • Look at a visual bug and name the technique most likely at fault.
  • Read a frame-time graph and a profiler capture well enough to ask the right next question.
  • Write a brief an AI agent can act on, and review what it returns.
  • Choose a lighting, shadow and anti-aliasing approach that fits a platform and a frame budget.

What fluent sounds like

Before

“The shadows look bad on the terrain. Fix them.”

After

“Unity 6 URP, Quest 3, 72 fps target. Shadow acne on the terrain when the sun is low. The main light uses 2 cascades at 1024. Propose bias and cascade changes that keep the shadow pass under 1.5 ms, and tell me how to confirm the result in the Frame Debugger.”

How every idea is taught

Every chapter follows the same eight beats in the same order, so the reader always knows where to look. Beats four, five and seven are what set this handbook apart from a programmer's textbook: they are the parts a lead uses daily.

  1. The pictureOne diagram that holds the whole idea before any prose.
  2. In plain wordsThree sentences without jargon, an everyday analogy, and the problem the technique was invented to solve.
  3. How it worksThe mechanism step by step. Where motion explains it better, an interactive replaces the static figure.
  4. What it costsMilliseconds, memory, and what makes the cost grow: resolution, light count, triangle count.
  5. How it breaksThe artifacts it produces, what each looks like, and the usual fix.
  6. In the enginesWhat Unreal, Unity and Godot call it, and where the setting lives.
  7. Say it to your AIBriefs and review questions that use the chapter's vocabulary, ready to copy and adapt.
  8. Check yourselfThree quick questions and the list of terms the chapter introduced.

Three rules that hold across the book

  • No term before its chapter. Each chapter lists what it needs, and a test flags any term used ahead of the chapter that teaches it.
  • Simplifications are labelled. Where the simple story hides something, a note says so and points to the fuller version.
  • Maths is optional. Formulas and short annotated shader snippets live in fold-outs. The pictures carry the argument.

The learning path

The book follows the route real data takes: from a team's idea, through art tools and the engine, across the CPU and into the GPU, until it leaves as a finished image. Each part owns a stretch of that route. Part 0 walks the whole route once, quickly, before any detail.

Visual system

Pictures do the teaching and prose supports them. Every chapter opens on a diagram, and no page goes more than a screen without one.

Four kinds of picture

Static diagrams

About 250 hand-drawn SVGs, written into the page so they follow the light and dark themes and stay sharp at any zoom.

Interactives

34 small Canvas pieces with one or two controls each: drag a vertex, slide a bias, toggle a pass. Most are 2D cross-sections, which explain shadow maps, AO and ray marching better than a 3D view does.

The live scene

A small real-time renderer in WebGL2 that draws one diorama and can show any of its internal buffers.

Artifact gallery

Every failure mode drawn or simulated next to its fixed version, named and linked to its cause.

One scene, all the way through

A single diorama, the Lantern Courtyard, appears from Part 4 onward: a stone arch, a few crates, a lantern, a puddle and a hanging banner. Each object is there for a reason. The arch gives corners for AO and long shadows, the crates show instancing and level of detail, the lantern is a point light that blooms, the puddle exposes what screen-space reflections miss, and the banner moves in a vertex shader.

The reader first meets it as bare wireframe, then watches it gain textures, light, shadows, occlusion, bounced light and post effects. By Part 10 they can step through every pass that draws it. The scene is generated in code, so it needs no asset files.

Colour means the same thing everywhere

  • Blue is the idea the chapter is about. It is the blue of a tangent-space normal map.
  • Teal is geometry and vertex data.
  • Amber is light and anything it carries.
  • Rose is screen-space data: depth, G-buffer, post.

Colour never carries meaning alone. Every coloured mark also has a label or a pattern.

Signature interactives

Twelve of the 34 interactives carry the hardest ideas. They get the most design time and are the ones a reader will remember.

Frame scrubber 10.1

Step through every pass of a live frame of the courtyard: shadow map, depth, G-buffer, AO, lighting, reflections, fog, post. The capstone of the book.

Vertex buffer inspector 4.2

Click a corner of a mesh and see its bytes light up. Toggle indexing and watch the memory total change.

Space stepper 3.4

One cube carried through object, world, view, clip and screen space, one click per transform.

Triangle rasterizer 5.3

Drag a triangle across a pixel grid. See which pixels it covers and how values blend between corners.

Filter floor 6.2

A checkerboard running to the horizon under nearest, bilinear, trilinear and anisotropic filtering.

Material sphere 6.5

Roughness and metallic sliders on a lit sphere, with the light free to move.

Shadow bias bench 7.4

A side view of the shadow-map depth test. Slide the bias from acne to peter-panning and find the middle.

Horizon explorer 8.3

Drag pixel P along a depth slice and watch SSAO's random samples give way to GTAO's two horizons.

Screen-space ray lab 8.4

Fire reflection and bounce rays into the depth buffer. See them hit, then see them lose the scene behind an object or past the screen edge.

TAA time machine 9.2

Scrub through frames as jittered samples build a smooth edge, then move the object and watch ghosting appear.

Budget builder 11.1

Stack render passes into a 16.6 ms bar until the frame bursts, then choose what to cut.

Symptom gallery 12.4

Pick the picture that matches what you see on screen. Get its name, its likely cause, and what to ask for.

How the site is built

The site has no build step and no server. The docs folder is the site: what is in git is what GitHub Pages serves and what opens when someone double-clicks index.html.

Rules that keep it working from a double-click

Browsers treat a page opened from disk more strictly than one served over the web. These rules come from those limits, and a test enforces each one.

  • Classic scripts only. Browsers refuse to load JavaScript modules from file://.
  • No fetch(). Navigation, glossary and search data ship as plain .js files.
  • Textures are generated in code. WebGL cannot use image files loaded from disk.
  • All pages sit flat in one folder with relative links that end in .html, so every asset is below the page that needs it.
  • Nothing from a CDN. Fonts are self-hosted with system fallbacks, so the handbook also works offline.
  • Saved state is a convenience. Theme and reading progress use local storage and the site works without it.

What readers get

  • Part and chapter navigation with reading progress and previous/next links.
  • Offline search across chapters and about 300 glossary terms.
  • A hover card on every term, linking to the chapter that teaches it.
  • Light and dark themes that every diagram follows.
  • Copy buttons on every “Say it to your AI” brief.
  • Printable cheat sheets: the pipeline, the anatomy of a frame, budgets, settings decoded.
  • Full keyboard access, reduced-motion support, and interactives that pause when off screen.

Node is used by authors only, for the tests and to regenerate the search index. Readers never need it.

Quality checks

Structure tests

Every page has all eight beats. Every link and anchor resolves. Every term used is in the glossary and taught at or before the chapter using it. No module scripts, no fetch, no absolute or remote asset URLs. The search index matches the pages.

Browser tests

Playwright opens every page from file://: no console errors, no sideways scroll at phone width, both themes, each interactive starts and responds to input, keyboard order makes sense. Chromium locally; Firefox and WebKit added in GitHub Actions.

Technical accuracy

Each chapter lists its sources: papers, conference talks, engine documentation. Engine-specific names and settings are checked against current docs when the chapter is written and stamped with the engine version, because they change quickly.

Fresh-eyes review

At the end of each phase a separate reviewer with no context reads the new chapters twice: once hunting for technical errors, once as a newcomer marking every sentence that assumes too much.

Out of scope

The test for every topic: does it help the reader brief, judge or decide graphics work? If it only helps someone hand-write an engine, it stays out.

  • Gameplay programming, networking and audio, beyond where they share the frame budget.
  • Click-by-click engine tutorials. The handbook says what a setting does and where it lives; engine docs show the buttons.
  • Writing a renderer from scratch.
  • A full mathematics course. Only the vector and matrix ideas the pictures need.
  • Photos or screenshots from commercial games. Every image is drawn or rendered for the handbook.
