12.2 Briefing AI on graphics work

An AI can only work from what it is told and what it can find. This chapter takes a brief apart: the context it cannot guess, the numbers and the budget, the symptom and how to reproduce it, the limits, what to hand back, and how the result will be checked. An interactive builds a strong brief from a weak one, and three worked briefs show the template on a bug, an optimisation and a new feature.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A brief is the written request that starts a piece of AI work. 12.1 showed that an AI sees only its context: the brief, the files it opens and the results of its tools. Whatever the brief leaves out, the AI fills in with a plausible guess, and it will not say which parts it guessed. A good brief puts the facts that are hard to guess inside that window.

An everyday comparison

Calling a plumber about a leak. "There's water somewhere" gets a visit, a look around and a guess. "Hot tap in the upstairs bathroom drips once a second, worse since the boiler was serviced, the valve is a 15 mm compression fitting" gets the right part on the van and a fix on the first visit, and you both know when it is fixed: the dripping stops.

The problem it solves

Without a brief, every AI result needs a round of corrections that each cost a review: wrong engine version, wrong platform, a change nobody asked for, no way to tell whether it worked. With one, the first result is usually close, and checking it takes minutes because the brief already said how.

Why a lead cares

Writing the brief is the part of AI work that cannot be delegated to the AI. It is also the same document a lead would give a contractor or a new hire, and the same one QA needs to verify the result. Ten to fifteen minutes on a brief often saves hours of review and rework, and it leaves a record of what was asked and why.

3 How it works

The steps follow the template: its parts, then the context, the numbers, the problem and how to reproduce it, the limits and deliverables, and how the result is checked. Then build a brief in the interactive, and see the template applied to three kinds of task.

1 The anatomy of a brief

Every brief in this book follows the same eight parts. Small tasks need a line for each; large ones need a paragraph. The order matters less than leaving nothing out, but this order reads well: what you want, where you are, how far off you are, what is wrong, what must not change, what to hand back, and how it will be checked.

2 Context: the facts an AI cannot guess

The context names the engine and its exact version, the render pipeline or rendering path, the features that are on or off, the target platforms, and the files or systems involved. These facts come first because they change the answer to almost every question that follows.

Give the full version, such as 5.8 or 6000.6, rather than "Unreal 5" or "Unity 6". Name the pipeline and path: URP or HDRP and their rendering path in Unity, deferred or forward in Unreal, the rendering method in Godot. Say which big features are on: Lumen, Nanite, virtual shadow maps, ray tracing. If your project has modified the engine, say that too.

3 Numbers: the budget and where you are

Performance briefs need three numbers: the frame budget for the target, the current measurement, and the gap. The budget comes from the target frame rate and the team's allocation per pass (11.1). The measurement comes from a capture, and its "where" and "how" matter as much as its value.

4 The problem, where it appears, and how to see it

For a bug, describe what is wrong in the vocabulary of the book, where it appears, when, and how often. Then give reproduction steps: the build, the settings, the place and camera, and the actions that make it happen every time. A visual bug described by name, such as shadow acne or ghosting, already points at a cause; 12.4 is a field guide to those names.

For an optimisation, the problem part holds the expanded measurement: which pass, which part of it, which content (11.5). For a new feature, it holds what the feature is and where it lives: which menu, which levels, which defaults per platform.

5 Constraints and deliverables

Constraints are the limits the solution must respect. In graphics work they usually cover the look (which cameras or scenes must not change), budgets (milliseconds and megabytes on each platform), scope (files and systems out of bounds), and trade-offs that need a person's decision, such as fewer shadow cascades or a lower internal resolution. Without them, an AI asked to make something faster will find the cheapest change, which is often a visible loss of quality on a platform nobody checked.

Deliverables say what to hand back and in what order. For anything non-trivial, ask for the diagnosis before the change, small steps rather than one large one, and a note of what each change trades away. Say where to stop: "then wait for review" keeps an agent from carrying on into work nobody has agreed to.

6 Acceptance criteria, verification and evidence

Acceptance criteria are the conditions the result must meet, written before work starts. A verification plan says how each one will be checked and by whom. Evidence is what goes in and comes back: captures, screenshots from fixed cameras, timings, logs and test results.

Ask for evidence back in the same form you sent it: the same capture at the same bookmark, the same screenshots, the same test. Then the review compares like with like, and the human review gate from 12.1 takes minutes instead of an afternoon.

7 Build a brief

Start from the one-line brief and add the elements one at a time. Watch the list of guesses shrink and the brief grow; then switch to the bug or the feature to see the same template applied in another engine.

8 Three worked briefs

The template does not change between kinds of task, but the weight shifts. A bug brief is mostly symptom and reproduction steps. An optimisation brief is mostly numbers. A feature brief is mostly a description of what to build and what done looks like.

Simplified

Real briefs often add links: the ticket, the design document, the relevant files and earlier attempts. Long attachments such as full logs cost context; attach a summary with the key lines and say where the full file is.

4 What it costs

A strong brief costs ten to thirty minutes of someone who knows the problem. A weak one costs more, later: each round trip needs a review, and the reviews that find a wrong version or a wrong platform are pure waste. The cost also grows with how hard the result is to check without the brief's acceptance criteria.

Worked example, illustrative. The weak brief costs 3.25 hours of people's time over two days, the strong one 1.2 hours: about 2 hours saved on one task. A team handing an agent ten such tasks a week saves around 20 hours, half a person, and gets a written record of each decision. The saving depends on the checks: without a devkit check, both briefs are cheaper and both results are unverified.

  • Missing contextEach missing fact is a potential round trip, and version mistakes are the most common.
  • Vague criteriaWithout a measurable "done", review becomes opinion and repeats.
  • Platforms the AI cannot runEvery one needs a person with a devkit, so say who in the brief.
  • Task sizeBig asks need plans and staged deliverables, or the review grows beyond what anyone can check.
  • Long attachmentsWhole logs and captures crowd the context; summaries and pointers work better.

The usual trade

Teams keep a brief template in their task tracker, require the context and verification parts for every AI task, and let the rest scale with the task. They save good briefs as examples, because a brief that worked once is the fastest way to write the next one.

5 How it breaks

Each missing part of a brief has its own failure. Most look like an AI mistake, and most trace back to a line the brief did not contain.

An answer for the wrong engine version

Cause
No version in the context, so the AI answered for one it knew. The setting it describes moved or was renamed.
Ask for
The exact version and pipeline in every brief, and links to that version's documentation in every answer.

Faster on the dev PC, slower on the console

Cause
No target platform or budget in the brief, so the AI optimised for the machine it could measure.
Ask for
Platforms and budgets in the target line, and a criterion per platform with who measures it.

The fix broke something elsewhere

Cause
No constraints and no regression checks: a shared setting changed, and another scene or material paid for it.
Ask for
Constraints that name what must not change, and screenshots from a fixed set of cameras before and after.

Nobody can say whether it worked

Cause
No acceptance criteria and no baseline: the result is "better", measured differently from the original.
Ask for
Criteria with a place, a method and a person, and the same capture before and after.

It rewrote half the renderer

Cause
No scope in the constraints and no stopping point in the deliverables, so the agent kept improving things.
Ask for
Files and systems in and out of scope, a plan before code, and "then stop for review" in the deliverables.

It cannot reproduce the bug, so it guesses

Cause
No reproduction steps: the AI fixes the most likely cause it can think of, and nobody can tell whether that was the real one.
Ask for
Steps that reproduce it every time, with build, settings and camera, and a test that fails before the fix and passes after.

7 Say it to your AI

Two briefs about briefs: one asks the AI to find what your brief is missing before it starts, the other sets out what it must hand back so the review is quick.

Check my briefUse before starting any larger task
Below is a brief for a graphics task. Do not start the task.

1. List anything you would have to guess: engine or version details, platforms, budgets, how the problem is reproduced, what must not change, what to deliver, how the result will be checked.
2. For each guess, ask me one specific question.
3. Restate the acceptance criteria as you understand them, with where and how each will be measured.
4. Say which criteria you cannot verify yourself and what evidence you will need from us.

[paste the brief here]
  • Surfacing guesses before work starts is far cheaper than finding them in review.
  • Restated criteria show whether the AI understood what "done" means.
  • Naming what it cannot verify assigns that check to a person early.
Report backAppend to any brief
When you finish, report in this order:

1. What you changed, file by file, in one line each.
2. The cause you found, with the evidence: capture locations, log lines or test output.
3. Each acceptance criterion with its measured result, where and how you measured it, and the same measurement from before the change.
4. What you did not test, including platforms you could not run.
5. Anything you changed that the brief did not ask for, and why.
  • A fixed report shape makes every review read the same way.
  • Before-and-after numbers in the same units make the claim checkable.
  • Listing what was not tested stops silence being read as success.

Questions to ask when reviewing the response to a brief

  1. Does the answer match the engine version and pipeline in the brief?Version drift is the most common and cheapest mistake to catch.
  2. Is each acceptance criterion reported with a measurement, a place and a method?A claim without them is not yet a result.
  3. Were the measurements taken the same way as the baseline?Different cameras, builds or tools make before and after incomparable.
  4. Did anything outside the constraints change?Unrequested changes carry unreviewed risk.
  5. Which criteria still need a person, and who has them?The brief named them; make sure someone actually does them.

8 Check yourself

  1. "The game stutters, fix it." Which parts of the template are missing, and which one would you add first?

    Show answer

    All seven: context, target, numbers, symptom and where with reproduction steps, constraints, deliverables and verification. Add the symptom and reproduction steps first, with a frame-time capture: stutter has many causes (11.4), and without knowing where and when it happens the AI can only guess which one.

  2. Which of these is an acceptance criterion an AI and a reviewer can both check: "shadows should look better and run faster", or "Shadow depths at most 2.0 ms on PS5 at bookmark 3, median of 300 frames, and no visible change at cameras 1 to 5, signed off by the art lead"? Why?

    Show answer

    The second. It names a measure, a threshold, a place, a method and a person for the part no number can judge. The first gives no threshold, place or method, so any result can be argued either way.

  3. An AI asked to make shadows faster cut the cascade count from 4 to 2. The pass is now under budget. What was missing from the brief, and what should it have said?

    Show answer

    Constraints. Fewer cascades lowers shadow resolution in the distance, a visible trade-off that needs a person's decision. The brief should have said "do not change the cascade count" or listed it as an option to propose but not apply, with the cameras where the look must not change.
