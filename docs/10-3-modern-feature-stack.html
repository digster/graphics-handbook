10.3 A modern feature stack

A high-end Unreal Engine 5 frame is built around a handful of large systems: Nanite geometry, virtual shadow maps, Lumen lighting, TSR upscaling and now MegaLights. This chapter takes that frame apart pass by pass, shows which older technique each system replaces, what it costs, where it falls back, and how to read a game's feature list and settings menu with that knowledge. It ends with the same ideas in Unity 6 and Godot 4.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

For twenty years a game's frame was assembled from techniques that each needed artists to prepare data by hand: level-of-detail meshes, shadow cascades tuned per level, lightmaps baked overnight, and anti-aliasing that ran at close to native resolution. A modern feature stack replaces each of those with a system that works things out per frame, from what the camera actually sees.

The systems cost more per frame than the techniques they replace, and they lean on each other. That is why games now describe their graphics as a list of named features and why a settings menu can change the cost of a frame by half with a single option. Reading that list well means knowing what each name replaces, what it costs and what happens on hardware that cannot run it.

An everyday comparison

A modern car's safety package. Adaptive cruise control, lane keeping and automatic braking share the same cameras and radar, and each was designed assuming the others are there. Buyers read a list of named features, but the list hides what they cost, what they need from the hardware, and which ones quietly switch off on the base model.

The problem it solves

Knowing the stack lets you read a feature list, a settings menu or a profiler capture and translate each name into a cost, a content rule and a fallback. That is what turns a marketing bullet into a decision you can make or review.

Why a lead cares

Each feature is a budget line and a set of content rules. Lumen at 60 fps is a 4 ms commitment; Nanite needs opaque or masked materials; hardware ray tracing changes the PC minimum spec; MegaLights needs ray-tracing hardware and does not run on phones. Choosing the stack early decides which platforms ship with which fallbacks, and how much art has to be built twice.

3 How it works

The steps follow the frame in section 1, one system at a time, then cover the settings that scale them and how to read a game's list of features. Every number from Epic is labelled with its source; the rest are marked illustrative.

1 Nanite: geometry chosen per cluster

Nanite, from 4.5 Level of detail, splits every mesh into a hierarchy of small clusters at import. Each frame the GPU culls clusters and picks a detail level per cluster, the cluster LOD idea, then rasterises what survives into a visibility buffer that records which triangle covers each pixel. A second pass runs the materials once per pixel and writes an ordinary G-buffer, so lighting after it is the deferred frame of 10.1.

Epic's 2021 deep dive gave numbers for one frame of a UE5 demo on console, at about 1400p before upscaling to 4K: about 25 million triangles rasterised where a classic renderer would have needed over a billion, about 2.5 ms to cull and rasterise the visibility buffer, and about 2 ms for the material pass. Nanite draws opaque and masked materials only, and its 5.8 documentation lists the forward renderer, MSAA and VR stereo rendering among the features it does not support.

Ray tracing needs triangles in an acceleration structure, so Unreal builds a simplified fallback mesh for every Nanite mesh and puts that in the ray tracing scene by default. Its detail is set per mesh with Fallback Relative Error; too coarse a fallback shows up as wrong shadows and reflections in anything traced against it.

2 Virtual shadow maps: pages instead of cascades

Virtual shadow maps, from 7.5 Shadows at scale, replace cascades with one very large shadow map per light: 16,384 texels square, cut into 128 × 128 pages that are allocated and drawn only where on-screen pixels need them. A directional light uses a clipmap of these, each level twice the radius of the one before; by default levels 6 to 22, from 64 cm around the camera to about 40 km.

The trick that makes them affordable is caching. A page that nothing changed keeps last frame's depth. Epic's documentation names the things that throw pages away: any movement of the light, and every frame for geometry deformed by skinning, World Position Offset or Pixel Depth Offset. New projects use virtual shadow maps by default, and Epic adds that a project that does not use Nanite is probably not a good fit for them.

3 Lumen: two ways to trace, five quality levels

Lumen, from 8.5 Real-time GI families, computes bounced light and reflections every frame. Its rays first try a screen trace through the depth buffer. Rays that miss continue in one of two tracing modes. Software ray tracing marches through signed distance fields of the scene, merged into one global distance field by default; it works on any GPU with Shader Model 6, and its tracing cost does not depend on how many instances overlap. Hardware ray tracing tests rays against triangles and is enabled by default in 5.8; Epic recommends it on consoles at both 30 and 60 fps, but its cost grows with instances, and Epic advises fewer than 100,000 in the ray tracing scene on current consoles.

Lumen's quality is set by two scalability groups, global illumination and reflections, and Epic states the budgets outright: 4 ms at 60 fps and 8 ms at 30 fps on consoles, at a 1080p internal resolution. Each level costs about half the one above. In 5.8 the Medium level became Lumen Lite, in Beta: an irradiance field of probes for GI and screen-space reflections, about twice as fast as High and aimed at Switch 2 and lower-end PCs at 60 fps.

4 TSR: fewer pixels, more frames

TSR, from 9.3 Upscaling and frame generation, is Unreal's built-in temporal upscaler. It runs on any GPU with Shader Model 5 and on PS5 and Xbox Series, with code tuned for their AMD GPUs. It is what lets every pass before it run at a lower internal resolution: Epic's Lumen budgets assume 1080p upscaled to 4K. Epic reports TSR's effective cost in Fortnite on PS5 and Xbox Series X as about 1.5 ms, with part of its work, about 0.5 ms, running on async compute.

The cost of a low internal resolution is time. TSR builds each output pixel from samples gathered over several frames, and Epic gives the formula for how long a newly revealed area takes to reach one sample per pixel: 1000 ÷ (screen percentage² × frame rate) milliseconds. Fewer pixels or fewer frames per second both make it slower to settle.

5 MegaLights: many lights at a fixed cost

MegaLights is a new direct lighting path. Instead of shading every light at every pixel and drawing a shadow map for each, each pixel traces a fixed number of rays towards the lights most likely to matter, chosen by importance sampling, and a denoiser reconstructs the result. In Epic's words, deferred shading has constant quality and a cost that grows with the number of lights, while MegaLights has constant cost and a quality that depends on how many important lights affect each pixel.

It needs hardware ray tracing, Shader Model 6 and wave operations, so it runs on PS5, Xbox Series and ray-tracing PCs, and Epic lists mobile, Switch and previous-generation consoles as unsupported; it is also incompatible with the forward renderer. Its status has moved quickly: Experimental in 5.5, Beta in 5.7, and in Epic's 5.8 release notes, now entering Production Ready status with reduced noise and performance improved to reach 60 fps. The 5.8 documentation page still carries an experimental tag.

6 What each part costs and where it falls back

Every system in the stack has a cheaper fallback, and a shipping game uses some of them on some platforms. Knowing the chain matters as much as knowing the best case: the fallback is what a Series S, a PC without ray tracing or a handheld actually shows.

7 Scalability presets: what Low and Epic change

A settings menu's Low, Medium, High and Epic are presets over scalability groups: shadows, global illumination, reflections, anti-aliasing, post-processing, textures, effects, foliage, view distance and resolution scale. Each group level sets a list of console variables from BaseScalability.ini, and a game or device profile can override any of them. Reading a menu means knowing which group an option belongs to and what its levels actually do.

8 Decoding a feature list

Store pages, trailers and console patch notes describe graphics as a list of features and modes. On consoles the list usually ends in a choice of graphics modes, such as a Performance mode at 60 fps and a Quality mode at 30 fps. 9 The same ideas in Unity 6 and Godot 4

Unity and Godot solve the same five problems with different machinery and less of it built in. Unity 6 cuts the CPU cost of drawing with the GPU Resident Drawer, which instances objects through the BatchRendererGroup API, generates LODs at import with Mesh LOD, lights with Adaptive Probe Volumes and, in HDRP, screen-space or ray-traced GI, and upscales with STP. Godot 4 has automatic mesh LOD and visibility ranges, SDFGI and VoxelGI for real-time GI in its Forward+ renderer, and FSR 2.2 as a built-in temporal upscaler.

Worked numbers. In a 60 fps mode, Lumen's 4 ms plus Epic's demo figures for Nanite, 4.5 ms, and TSR's 1.5 ms already add up to 10 ms of a 16.7 ms frame before shadows, direct lights, fog, translucency, post-processing and the UI. That is why a 60 fps mode runs Lumen at High, usually relies on dynamic resolution, and needs a close watch on virtual shadow map invalidations. The 30 fps mode doubles the frame and spends most of the difference on Lumen at Epic and a higher internal resolution.

  • Internal resolutionNanite's material pass, Lumen and most of the frame scale with pixels; TSR's cost follows the output.
  • Things that moveMoving lights, skinned meshes and World Position Offset redraw shadow pages and update the ray tracing scene every frame.
  • Ray tracing instancesHardware Lumen and MegaLights rebuild the top-level acceleration structure every frame, in proportion to instances.
  • Glossy surfacesPixels smoother than roughness 0.4 trace their own Lumen reflection rays by default.
  • Overlapping lightsMegaLights stays flat in cost, but quality drops where many important lights share a pixel.

The usual trade

Teams pick the 60 fps mode's settings first, because that is where the stack barely fits, and treat the 30 fps mode as the place to spend headroom. Then they write down each system's fallback for the weakest target and review the content in that fallback early, before lighting is final.

5 How it breaks

The stack breaks where content fights a system's assumptions: things that move, thin or overlapping geometry, too few pixels and too many lights.

Shadows cost milliseconds in a forest

Cause
Foliage animated with World Position Offset, skinned characters or a moving sun invalidate virtual shadow map pages every frame, so the cache saves nothing.
Ask for
The Cached Page visualisation in a busy shot, WPO bounds clamped, Shadow Cache Invalidation Behavior set where motion is static, and large casters as Nanite.

Light leaking through thin walls

Cause
Software ray tracing sees meshes as distance fields; walls thinner than the field can represent, or a whole room imported as one mesh, let rays through.
Ask for
The Mesh Distance Field view of the room, walls as separate meshes with enough thickness, a higher distance field resolution where needed, or hardware ray tracing.

Smears and softness in motion

Cause
A low screen percentage at a low frame rate: TSR needs four frames for one sample per pixel at 50%, and at 30 fps that is 133 ms of visible reconstruction.
Ask for
Captures of fast motion in each graphics mode, the TSR settings per mode, and a higher internal resolution or frame rate where motion matters most.

Noisy or ghosting lighting with many lamps

Cause
Too many important lights over the same pixels for MegaLights' fixed sample budget, often lights with large ranges or placed partly inside walls.
Ask for
The light complexity view, light ranges, cones and barn doors narrowed, small clusters merged into area lights, and lights moved out of geometry.

Shadows and reflections that do not match the mesh

Cause
Ray tracing sees the simplified fallback mesh, or culls distant objects from the ray tracing scene, so shadows from MegaLights and hardware Lumen reflections show a blockier or missing shape.
Ask for
The shadow caster mismatch view, Fallback Relative Error lowered on the meshes that show it, and the ray tracing culling radius checked.

Frame time jumps in dense areas

Cause
Hardware ray tracing pays per instance to rebuild the top-level acceleration structure, and slows on large overlapping meshes; a kit-bashed district can pass Epic's 100,000-instance guidance.
Ask for
Ray tracing active instances from stat SceneRendering in the worst area, ray tracing culling settings, and huge or layered meshes removed from the ray tracing scene.

7 Say it to your AI

Feature-stack briefs go well when they name the mode and its budget, the weakest target, and ask for fallbacks as well as settings.

BriefUse when planning graphics modes for a UE5 console game
Unreal Engine 5.8, Nanite, Lumen with hardware ray tracing, virtual shadow maps, TSR. Targets: PS5, Xbox Series X and Series S. Modes: Performance 60 fps, Quality 30 fps, 4K output.

1. Propose the scalability group levels (sg.*) and screen percentage range for each mode and console, as device profile entries.
2. Give a per-pass budget for each mode that adds up within the frame, using Epic's published Lumen targets and marking every other number as an estimate.
3. List each system's fallback on Series S and what changes visually.
4. Name the visualisations and stats we use to verify each budget in a capture.
  • Device profile entries are something a reviewer can read and test directly.
  • Separating published targets from estimates keeps invented numbers out of the plan.
  • Fallbacks per console are where visual surprises come from late in production.
ReviewUse when a level runs over budget with the full stack on
Unreal Engine 5.8, 60 fps Performance mode on PS5. The jungle level runs at 19 ms on the GPU. stat GPU: VSM 4.6 ms, Lumen 5.1 ms, Nanite 3.8 ms, TSR 1.5 ms, rest 4.0 ms.

1. Compare each figure with Epic's published targets and say which are over.
2. For virtual shadow maps, list the likely invalidation sources in foliage and how to confirm them with the Cached Page view.
3. For Lumen, say what to check first: instance count, glossy pixels, or scalability level.
4. Rank fixes by visual risk and say which need content changes rather than settings.
  • Per-system times let the AI reason about the stack instead of the whole frame.
  • Naming the visualisations makes its diagnosis checkable.
  • Asking settings versus content separates a quick fix from a production task.

Questions to ask when reviewing feature-stack work

  1. Which graphics mode and platform is this measured in, at what internal resolution?Lumen's budgets and TSR's convergence depend on both.
  2. What does each system fall back to on the weakest target, and has anyone looked at it?The fallback is what many players see.
  3. What moves in this scene: lights, skinned meshes, WPO foliage?Movement decides virtual shadow map and ray tracing costs.
  4. How many ray tracing instances are active in the worst area?Epic's guidance for consoles is fewer than 100,000.
  5. Is every feature in the spec sheet supported on every platform listed?MegaLights and hardware Lumen exclude some hardware outright.

8 Check yourself

  1. A game advertises a 60 fps Performance mode with Lumen. What does Epic's guidance say about Lumen's level, budget and resolution in that mode?

    Show answer

    The GI and Reflections scalability groups at High, which targets 60 fps on consoles with about 4 ms for Lumen's GI and reflections, at a 1080p internal resolution upscaled by TSR. The engine's default console target is 30 fps at Epic, about 8 ms.

  2. Turning on wind animation for the trees adds 3 ms to the shadow pass in a project with virtual shadow maps. Why?

    Show answer

    The wind uses World Position Offset, which invalidates the trees' cached shadow pages every frame. Pages that would have been reused are redrawn each frame. Clamping the WPO bounds, using a cache invalidation behaviour where motion does not change the shadow, or reducing animated casters brings it back.

  3. The team wants MegaLights for a night market and also plans a phone version of the game. What do you ask?

    Show answer

    MegaLights needs hardware ray tracing and Epic lists mobile as unsupported, so the phone version needs a fallback: MegaLights off, with r.MegaLights.Allowed 0 in its device profile, and the market lit with a small number of classic lights and baked or cheap shadows. Ask to see the market in that fallback early, and who owns lighting it twice.
