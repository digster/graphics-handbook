4.6 Moving geometry

A character's mesh is stored once, in one pose, and never changes. Every frame the GPU moves each vertex to where the animation wants it, by following a skeleton, mixing stored shapes, or reading positions baked into a texture.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A vertex buffer holds a mesh in one fixed shape. To animate a character, an engine does not store a new copy of every vertex for every frame. It gives the character a skeleton: a hierarchy of a few dozen to a few hundred bones that animators can pose. Every frame the animation system works out where each bone is, and the GPU moves every vertex to follow the bones it belongs to.

That last step is skinning, and a mesh set up for it is a skinned mesh. Faces add a second method, stored shapes that are mixed in, and crowds often use a third, positions baked into a texture.

Everyday comparison

A sleeve on your arm. The fabric near the shoulder moves with your upper arm, the fabric at the wrist moves with your forearm, and the fabric over the elbow does a bit of both. Bend your arm hard and that fabric bunches and thins on the inside.

The bones are your arm, the sleeve is the mesh, and how much each patch of fabric follows each bone is its skin weight.

The problem it solves

A 30,000-vertex character animated by storing every vertex would need about 350 KB of positions for each frame, about 21 MB for each second at 60 frames per second.

Its skeleton needs a few dozen bone transforms per frame instead, and the same animations can drive any mesh built on that skeleton.

Why a lead cares

Characters are usually the most expensive meshes per vertex, and the number of them on screen is a design decision with a direct cost. Skinning quality depends on rigging and weight painting, specialist work that is easy to under-budget, and its failures, such as pinched elbows and collapsing wrists, are among the most noticed visual bugs. Choices made early, such as the bone count, influences per vertex and how faces animate, are expensive to change later.

3 How it works

Follow one arm from the modelling tool to the screen, then two other ways to move vertices, and finally where engines run the work. There are eight steps.

1 Skeletons and bones

A bone, also called a joint, is a transform with a parent. Bones form a transform hierarchy, so rotating the upper arm carries the forearm, hand and fingers with it. Animation data mostly stores rotations per bone over time; the engine walks down the hierarchy each frame to work out every bone's final place.

2 The bind pose

The artist models and weights the mesh in one pose, often with the arms out in a T or an A. That is the bind pose. Skinning needs to know where each vertex sits relative to each of its bones in that pose, so for every bone the engine stores its inverse bind matrix, which takes a point from the model's space into that bone's own space.

Each frame, a vertex goes into the bone's space with the inverse bind matrix, then back out with the bone's current transform. Engines multiply those two into one skinning matrix per bone, once per frame, and every vertex uses the matrices of its bones.

Simplified

The figures are 2D. Real bones also carry translation and sometimes scale, and the matrices are 4 × 4 or 3 × 4. Nothing else about the idea changes in 3D.

3 Skin weights

Most vertices follow more than one bone. Each vertex stores a short list of bones and a skin weight for each, saying how strongly that bone moves it. The weights add up to 1. Riggers paint them in the modelling tool, and they decide how every joint deforms.

The list has a fixed length, the bone influences per vertex. Four is the common default and fits in two of the vertex attributes from 4.2: four bone numbers and four weights, a byte each. Engines allow 8 or more where joints need it, such as shoulders and faces. Influences above the limit are dropped on import and the rest are rescaled to add up to 1 again.

4 Linear blend skinning

The standard method is linear blend skinning. For each vertex, move it with each of its bones' skinning matrices, as if that bone owned it alone, then average the results by the weights. It is fast and runs well on GPUs, which is why nearly every game uses it.

Its flaw is geometric. Each bone carries the vertex along a circle around the joint, but the weighted average of two points on a circle lies on the straight line between them, inside the circle. The more the joint turns, the further inside it lands.

Try it below. Bend the elbow with each set of weights. Rigid weights fold the inside of the joint over itself and stretch the outside. Smooth weights keep almost all of the arm's area but still thin the joint, and fold a few triangles on the inside as the bend tightens. Too-smooth weights fold nothing but spread the thinning along the whole arm and lose far more area, which reads as rubber. Then switch to dual quaternions, and twist the forearm.

Twisting shows the worst case. Turn the forearm half a turn and the vertices weighted half and half average two opposite points of the arm's cross-section, which meet in the middle. The arm collapses to a thin waist, the candy-wrapper artefact, named after the twisted ends of a sweet wrapper.

5 Dual quaternions, briefly

Dual-quaternion skinning blends the bones' rotations, then rotates the vertex by the result, so a vertex keeps its distance from the joint. Kavan and colleagues, who introduced it in 2007, describe it as fixing linear blending's artefacts at a small extra cost per vertex. Blender's Armature modifier offers quaternion blending as its Preserve Volume option, and its manual describes the problem it fixes: without it, joints rotated far from the rest pose shrink the geometry around them, almost to nothing at 180°.

Dual quaternions describe only rotation and movement, so bones that scale need extra handling, and the method costs a little more per vertex. Many teams instead fix the worst joints in the rig: extra twist bones that share a forearm's twist along its length, and corrective shapes that switch on as a joint bends.

6 Blend shapes

Bones are good at rotating parts. They are poor at subtle surface changes such as a smile, a raised eyebrow or a muscle bulging. A blend shape, also called a morph target or shape key, stores the mesh in another shape as offsets from the original. A weight from 0 to 1 says how much of each shape to add, and any number can be mixed at once.

Blend shapes are applied before skinning, so a smiling face still follows the head bone. They cost memory per shape and work per active shape per frame, and they come with their own import settings for normals, because a moved surface needs moved normals too.

7 Moving vertices without a skeleton

Some movement needs no skeleton at all. A vertex shader can move each vertex with a formula, which 5.2 calls vertex animation: grass and flags sway with sine waves, water rolls, and the courtyard's banner below ripples in the wind. Nothing in the vertex buffer changes, and the cost is a few instructions per vertex.

For motion too complex for a formula, such as a cloth simulation, a destruction effect or a crowd member's walk, the motion can be recorded instead. A vertex animation texture, or VAT, stores the position of every vertex in every frame as the pixels of a texture: one column per vertex, one row per frame. The vertex shader reads its own column at the current row. No skeleton is evaluated at runtime, so the CPU cost is close to nothing.

VATs are a staple of crowd rendering: thousands of characters drawn with instancing, each starting the clip at a different frame, at the cost of fixed clips that cannot blend or react the way a skeleton can. Tools such as SideFX's Labs Vertex Animation Textures export them for Unreal and Unity, and Unreal ships an experimental AnimToTexture plugin that bakes a skeletal animation into one.

8 Where the work runs

Skinning used to run on the CPU, which then uploaded the moved vertices every frame into a dynamic buffer. Today engines use GPU skinning, in one of two forms. The vertex shader can skin each vertex as it draws, in every pass that draws the mesh. Or a compute shader can skin the whole mesh once per frame into a buffer that every pass then reads.

Unreal offers both, plus programmable mesh deformers; its compute path, the Skin Cache, is required for hardware ray tracing. Unity skins with compute shaders by default and can batch many meshes into one dispatch. Godot's Forward+ and Mobile renderers skin and apply blend shapes in a compute shader before drawing.

Animation data itself is compressed. A clip stores each bone's rotation, position and scale over time, and animation compression drops keys that interpolation can rebuild and stores the rest with fewer bits. It trades memory for small errors, which show first in fingers and feet.

4 What it costs

Take the character from 4.2: 30,000 vertices with four influences each, drawn in a depth pass, four shadow cascades and the main pass.

  • Per vertex, skinning with four influences blends four bone matrices and transforms the position, normal and tangent: roughly 80 multiply-adds.
  • Per pass, that is about 2.4 million multiply-adds for the character. Skinned in the vertex shader, it is repeated in all six passes; skinned once in a compute shader, it is done once and costs a buffer of about 0.92 MB at 32 bytes per vertex, enough for position, packed normal and tangent, and last frame's position for motion vectors.
  • On the CPU, every character's skeleton is animated every frame: clips sampled and blended, then each bone's transform worked out down the hierarchy. A hundred characters of 80 bones is 8,000 bone transforms a frame, before any skinning.

Blend shapes are the surprise in most budgets: the 4.0 MB face above is about five times its head mesh (15,000 vertices × 56 bytes, 0.80 MB). Compression and sharing shapes across characters bring that down, but it has to be planned. Unity's non-batched GPU mode also issues one compute dispatch per mesh and active blend shape, so many active shapes cost time as well as memory.

People-time matters as much. Rigging and weight painting a hero character, then fixing its shoulders, elbows and wrists, is skilled work that is redone whenever the mesh or the skeleton changes. Lock the skeleton early and share it across characters where you can.

What makes the costs grow

  • Characters on screenEvery visible character costs skeleton animation on the CPU and skinning on the GPU.
  • Vertices and passesVertex-shader skinning repeats per pass, so shadow cascades multiply it.
  • Influences per vertexEight influences do about twice the blending work of four and need twice the weight data.
  • Active blend shapesEach active shape adds work per frame; each stored shape adds memory.
  • Off-screen skinningCharacters skinned while not visible, for example with Unity's Update When Offscreen turned on.
  • Bones per drawUnreal splits a mesh section with more bones than the platform allows into chunks, each its own draw call; mobile allows 75 bones per section.

5 How it breaks

Skinning bugs show up in motion and at the extremes of a pose, so review characters animating, through their full range of movement, not in the bind pose.

Pinched wrists and elbows

Cause
Linear blend skinning on a joint that twists or bends far: the candy-wrapper artefact, made worse by weights spread too wide.
Ask for
Twist bones along the forearm, corrective blend shapes for extreme bends, or dual-quaternion skinning where the engine supports it.

Spikes and stretched parts

Cause
The mesh's bone numbers do not match the skeleton it is drawn with, often after a reimport or a skeleton change, or the bind pose differs from the one the weights were made in.
Ask for
A check that mesh and skeleton come from the same export, bone names and order compared, and the bind pose verified.

Characters vanish at the screen edge

Cause
The bounding volume used for culling was computed for the bind pose and does not cover a pose that reaches out of it, so the whole mesh is culled while part of it is on screen.
Ask for
Bounds that cover the animation's full range, or bounds recomputed while animating where the engine offers it, at its cost.

Stiff, creased joints on low settings

Cause
A quality setting caps influences per vertex below what the rig was weighted with, so blended joints snap to one bone.
Ask for
The influence limit per platform and quality level, and whether import already trimmed the weights.

Faces without expressions

Cause
Blend shapes were not imported, were stripped from lower LODs, or the mesh uses a path that does not support them, such as a Nanite mesh in Unreal.
Ask for
The import setting for blend shapes, which LODs keep them, and the rendering path each character uses.

Sliding feet and jittery fingers

Cause
Animation compressed too hard: errors near the root of a chain grow by the time they reach the feet and fingers at its ends.
Ask for
Lower error tolerances for the affected clips, checked on the extremities, and a review of compression settings per clip type.

To confirm skinning problems, view the skeleton and weights in the engine's mesh editor first. A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX shows whether skinning ran in a compute pass or in the vertex shader, and which buffers each draw read.

7 Say it to your AI

Skinning briefs need numbers: characters on screen, vertices, bones, influences and passes. Ask for the cost per frame and a way to see the result in motion.

BriefUse when planning a crowd
Engine: Unity 6.6, URP. Target: PlayStation 5 at 60 fps.
A stadium scene needs about 3,000 spectators visible at once. Each has 8,000 vertices and a 45-bone skeleton, and they only cheer, sit and stand.

1. Compare three approaches: Skinned Mesh Renderers, GPU instancing with vertex animation textures, and a mix (skinned near the camera, VAT beyond 30 m). For each, estimate CPU time for animation, GPU skinning work per frame, draw calls and memory.
2. For the VAT option, give the texture size per clip and the total for three clips, with positions and normals.
3. List what each approach cannot do, such as blending between clips or reacting to the player.
4. Tell us how to measure each in the Unity Profiler and a GPU capture.
  • Counts of characters, vertices and bones let the AI estimate instead of guessing.
  • Naming the clips shows that baked animation is enough here.
  • Asking for limits stops a VAT answer that quietly drops a design need.
Bug reportUse when joints deform badly
Our hero character's wrists collapse into a thin waist when the forearm twists past about 120°, and her elbows pinch when bent fully. Unreal Engine 5.8, a Skeletal Mesh with 4 influences per vertex, linear blend skinning, no twist bones.

Explain why linear blend skinning does this, then propose fixes in order of cost: weight changes, twist bones in the forearm (how many, and how the twist should be split), and corrective morph targets driven by the elbow angle. For each, say what the rigger has to change, what it costs at runtime, and how we can check the result across the full range of motion.
  • The angle and the joints narrow the cause to the candy-wrapper artefact.
  • Stating 4 influences and no twist bones tells the AI what the rig already has.
  • Asking for runtime cost and a range-of-motion check keeps the fix testable.

Questions to ask when reviewing skinning work

  1. Where does skinning run for this character, and how many passes read the result?Vertex-shader skinning repeats in every pass; compute skinning costs memory.
  2. How many influences per vertex does the rig use, and what does each platform and quality level allow?A lower cap changes how joints bend on some machines only.
  3. Which joints have twist bones or corrective shapes, and were they tested through the full range of motion?Linear blend skinning fails at the extremes, not in the bind pose.
  4. How many blend shapes are stored and how many are active at once?Stored shapes cost memory; active shapes cost time every frame.
  5. Do the bounds cover every pose the animations reach?Bounds that are too small make characters vanish at the screen edge.

8 Check yourself

  1. A vertex has weights 0.7 for the upper arm and 0.3 for the forearm. The upper arm alone would put it at (1.0, 0.2) and the forearm alone at (0.8, 0.0). Where does linear blend skinning put it?

    Show answer

    0.7 × (1.0, 0.2) + 0.3 × (0.8, 0.0) = (0.94, 0.14): on the straight line between the two, 30% of the way toward the forearm's position.

  2. A 30,000-vertex character is drawn in a depth pass, four shadow cascades and the main pass. How many vertices are skinned per frame if each pass skins in its vertex shader, and if a compute shader skins once?

    Show answer

    180,000 in the vertex shader, six passes of 30,000. 30,000 with compute skinning, plus a cached buffer of about 0.92 MB at 32 bytes per vertex.

  3. A character's wrist shrinks to a thin waist when the forearm twists half a turn. What causes it, and name two fixes.

    Show answer

    The candy-wrapper artefact of linear blend skinning: vertices weighted between the two bones average opposite sides of the arm. Fixes: twist bones that share the twist along the forearm, corrective blend shapes, or dual-quaternion skinning where available.
