5.5 Depth, stencil and blending

Once a pixel shader has coloured a pixel, three small fixed units decide whether that colour is kept and how it mixes with what is already there. They are why near things hide far ones, how outlines and portals are made, and why glass is one of the hardest things in a frame to get right.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The GPU draws objects in whatever order the engine hands them over. Something has to make sure a house drawn first is not painted over by a mountain drawn later that is really behind it. That something is the depth buffer: a screen-sized image that stores, for every pixel, how far away the nearest surface drawn there so far is. A new surface is kept only where it is nearer.

Next to it sits a second small image, the stencil buffer, which holds a number per pixel that effects can write and test, like a cut-out mask. And at the very end, blending decides how a new colour combines with the colour already in the framebuffer: replace it, mix with it for glass and smoke, add to it for fire and glow, or multiply it.

The depth buffer makes solid objects come out right in any order. See-through objects break that guarantee. Each layer of glass has to be mixed over what is behind it, so what is behind it has to be drawn first. That one fact explains most transparency bugs in games.

Everyday comparison

A sign-painter is painting a street scene from a pile of jobs that arrive in random order. On every patch of wall they keep a sticky note saying how far away the thing already painted there is meant to be. A job that is further away than the note gets skipped on that patch. A nearer one gets painted, and the note is updated. A cardboard stencil taped to the wall can stop them touching some patches at all.

Glass is the awkward job. It has to be painted as a thin tint over whatever is already on the wall, so it only looks right if everything behind it was painted first.

The problem it solves

Without a per-pixel record of distance, whatever was drawn last would cover everything, and the engine would have to sort every triangle in the scene from back to front each frame. That is slow, and impossible to get right when triangles cross each other.

The depth buffer removes the need for solid objects. For transparent ones nothing removes it cheaply, so engines sort them and live with the cases sorting cannot fix.

Why a lead cares

Flickering distant roads, glass that hides what is behind it, smoke that pops in front of a character as the camera turns, foliage that thins out with distance: these are among the most reported visual bugs in any game, and all of them live here. So do some of the costliest effects on a GPU, because every layer of smoke or glass is blended over the whole area it covers. Knowing which setting causes which symptom turns a vague bug report into a fix in an afternoon.

3 How it works

Nine steps: the depth test, its precision and how it fails, the stencil test, the three common ways of blending, cut-outs, and finally the transparency problem and the ways around it.

1 The depth test keeps the nearest surface

Before a frame is drawn, the depth buffer is cleared to the far value: nothing has been drawn anywhere. Then every fragment, the candidate colour a triangle produces for one pixel, goes through the depth test. The GPU compares the fragment's depth with the value stored for that pixel. If the fragment is nearer, it passes: its colour goes on to blending and, normally, its depth replaces the stored one. If not, it is thrown away.

That second part, storing the new depth, is the depth write, and it can be switched off separately. Opaque objects test and write. Transparent ones usually test but do not write, for reasons step 8 makes clear.

The comparison rule is a setting. Usually it is "pass if nearer". It can also be "pass if equal", used when a second pass redraws exactly the same surfaces, or "always", used for things that must sit on top of everything, such as a debug gizmo. Together with blending and the stencil rules, these settings are part of the fixed state of a draw, which 5.6 Shaders in practice shows is baked into a pipeline state object.

2 Z-fighting: when two surfaces tie

The depth buffer stores a number with a fixed number of bits, so it can only tell apart distances that differ by at least one step. When two surfaces lie closer together than that step, the test cannot decide which is in front. Tiny rounding differences decide instead, and they change from pixel to pixel and from frame to frame. The result is z-fighting: two surfaces showing through each other in jagged stripes that crawl as the camera moves.

The usual fixes are cheap. Move the surfaces apart by more than a step. For decals and other things meant to lie flat on a surface, apply a depth bias, a small offset the GPU adds to a draw's depth so it always wins the tie. And make the steps smaller where the problem appears, which is what the next step is about.

3 Depth precision and reversed-Z

The steps are not the same size everywhere. As 3.4 Spaces and transforms showed, a standard perspective projection spends most of its depth range right in front of the camera: with the near plane at 0.1 m, everything beyond 1 m shares the last tenth of the range. Far away, one step of stored depth covers a long distance.

Reversed-Z flips the range, so the near plane stores 1 and the far distance stores 0, and stores depth as a 32-bit floating-point number. Floating-point numbers are densest close to 0. In the standard range that density is wasted on the space just in front of the near plane; reversed, it lands on the distance, where the standard projection is weakest. The two unevennesses nearly cancel out, and precision becomes almost even across the whole view.

Reversed-Z costs nothing at run time and needs no extra memory. All three major engines use it where the graphics API allows. It does change what a stored depth of 0 and 1 mean, so custom shaders that read the depth buffer, such as water foam or soft particles, must be written for it. Godot found exactly that when it switched in 4.3.

Maths Where the two lines come from

For near plane n, far plane f and distance z, standard depth and reversed depth are:

standard = f ÷ (f − n) × (1 − n ÷ z) reversed = 1 − standard = n × (f − z) ÷ ((f − n) × z)

Both change with distance at the same rate, f × n ÷ ((f − n) × z²). The smallest gap the buffer can tell apart is one step of the stored number divided by that rate. A 24-bit integer has a fixed step of 1 ÷ 224, so the gap grows with z². A 32-bit float's step is about its value × 2−23; reversed depth is roughly n ÷ z, so the gap grows only with z.

With n = 0.1 m and f = 10 km, at z = 200 m: 2−24 × (f − n) × z² ÷ (f × n) = 2.4 cm for the standard buffer, and about 0.023 mm reversed.

4 The stencil buffer: a mask you can draw into

The stencil buffer stores a small whole number for every pixel, usually 8 bits, so 0 to 255. It usually shares memory with the depth buffer. Nothing in it is visible. Effects use it to mark pixels in one draw and to restrict later draws to the marked pixels, or to everything else.

The stencil test compares a reference value set by the draw with the stored value, using a rule such as "equal" or "not equal". Separate settings say what to do to the stored value when the test fails, when it passes but the depth test fails, and when both pass: keep it, replace it with the reference, increment it and so on. That small set of operations covers a surprising range of effects.

The same tool makes many other effects. A portal or a mirror marks its opening with one value, then draws the other room only where that value is stored. An x-ray view draws a character behind walls in a flat colour, only where its depth test fails. Shadow volumes, an older shadow technique, counted in and out of shadowed regions with stencil increments. Deferred renderers use stencil marks to skip pixels that a lighting pass does not need to touch.

5 Blending: combining with what is already there

A fragment that survives both tests reaches the blender, a fixed unit that combines its colour, the source, with the colour already stored, the destination. The blend mode picks the formula. Opaque surfaces use "replace". Three others cover nearly everything else in a game.

Alpha blending mixes by the source's alpha, its opacity from 0 to 1, held in its fourth colour channel. It is right for glass, smoke, and anything that partly covers what is behind. Additive blending adds the source to the destination, so it can only brighten: fire, sparks, laser beams, light shafts. Multiplicative blending multiplies them, so it can only darken or tint: stains, tinted overlays.

Blending happens per render target, on the stored values. A renderer that works in linear colour blends light, which is why particle edges and fades look wrong in a renderer that does not, as 3.2 Colour done right showed. Additive results above 1 survive only in a floating-point target, the reason HDR rendering makes glow and fire look right.

6 Premultiplied alpha

With premultiplied alpha, a texture or shader stores its colour already multiplied by its alpha, and the blender uses "source plus (1 − alpha) × destination". For a single colour the picture is identical. Two things improve.

First, filtering. When the GPU averages neighbouring texels, for example at the edge of a leaf texture or in a smaller mip level, it averages colour and alpha separately. With straight alpha, the invisible texels just outside the leaf still carry colour, often black, and that colour leaks into the average. The edge comes out with a dark or bright halo. Premultiplied texels outside the leaf are exactly zero, so they add nothing, and filtering gives the right answer.

Second, one blend mode covers two. With alpha 1 it behaves like normal blending; with alpha 0 and a non-zero colour it adds, like additive blending. A particle can go from glowing spark to dark smoke in one draw, with no change of blend state, so whole particle systems batch together. Unreal calls this mode AlphaComposite, Unity's URP calls it Premultiply, and Godot calls it Premultiplied Alpha.

7 Cut-outs: alpha test

Many see-through things are really either there or not there: leaves, grass, chain-link fences, hair cards, lace. For these, the alpha test skips blending altogether. The pixel shader compares alpha with a threshold, typically 0.5, and discards the pixel if it is below. Everything that survives is fully opaque, so it can write depth and be drawn in any order like any solid object.

It has two costs. A shader that can discard pixels cannot let the GPU write depth before it runs, so the early depth test from 5.4 works less well; engines often draw alpha-tested foliage in a depth-only pass first for this reason. And as a texture gets smaller in its mip levels, its averaged alpha drifts below the threshold, so distant trees lose leaves. Dithered transparency is a cousin. It discards a noisy pattern of pixels whose density follows alpha, to fake partial opacity, and usually relies on anti-aliasing such as TAA to smooth the pattern out. Godot's Alpha Hash mode is one example.

8 Transparency sort order

Alpha blending only gives the right picture when each layer is blended over everything behind it. Engines therefore draw a frame in a fixed order. All opaque objects first, in any order, writing depth. Then all transparent objects, sorted from the furthest to the nearest. This transparency sorting is done per object, usually by the distance from the camera to the centre of each object's bounds.

Transparent objects keep the depth test on, so glass behind a wall stays hidden, but switch the depth write off. If glass wrote depth, any glass drawn later and further away would fail the test against it and vanish. Try both mistakes, and the case no order can fix.

Per-object sorting fails in predictable ways. A large transparent object, such as a whole building's windows in one mesh or a long water surface, has one centre and one place in the order, although the camera may be inside it or beside it. Objects that cross, like the two panes, cannot be ordered at all. Triangles inside a single mesh are drawn in the order they are stored, so a glass bottle can show its back faces in front of its front ones. And when two objects' centres pass each other as the camera moves, their order flips from one frame to the next and the picture pops.

Engines give artists a manual override for the worst cases, a sort priority per object or material, and the usual advice is structural: split large transparent meshes, avoid intersecting glass, prefer cut-outs and additive effects, which need no sorting, wherever they look acceptable.

Simplified

Sorting by bounds centre is the common default; engines also offer sorting by distance along the view direction or along a fixed axis for 2D games. Sorting also fights with performance. Opaque objects are sorted by material and shader to cut state changes, but transparent ones must be sorted by distance, so they batch poorly and cost more draw calls.

9 Order-independent transparency

Order-independent transparency (OIT) is the family of techniques that make blended layers come out right per pixel, whatever order they arrive in. There are two broad approaches, and they trade memory against accuracy.

Between the two sit depth peeling, which renders the transparent layers several times and peels off one layer per pass, and newer schemes that keep a few layers per pixel or approximate how light is absorbed along each view ray. Unreal Engine has two experimental options of this kind. None of them is the default in a major engine. They cost memory, extra shader variants and GPU time, and most games get by with sorting, cut-outs and careful art direction.

4 What it costs

The tests themselves are nearly free: fixed hardware does them for many pixels per clock. What costs is memory, and the memory traffic of reading and writing buffers for every layer.

Blending is where the bill grows. Each blended layer must read the colour already in the target and write the result back. Take an explosion whose smoke covers half of a 1080p screen in 10 overlapping layers, drawn into a 16-bit float HDR target:

10.4 Mblended pixels per frame: 1,036,800 pixels × 10 layers

That is overdraw, measured. Every one of those 10.4 million pixels also runs a pixel shader, which 5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw prices. On phones, where memory traffic also costs battery and heat, a few full-screen layers of smoke can be the single most expensive thing in a frame.

What makes the cost grow

  • Layers × screen areaSmoke filling the screen in front of the camera is the worst case; the same effect far away costs a fraction.
  • Resolution and format4K has four times the pixels of 1080p. A 16-bit float target moves twice the bytes of an 8-bit one per blend.
  • Sorting and batchingDistance-sorted transparents cannot be grouped by material, so each costs its own draw call on the CPU.
  • Stencil and outline passesA stencil outline draws its objects at least twice. Unreal's custom depth draws marked objects into a separate depth and stencil target.
  • OITPer-pixel lists cost memory per layer; weighted blending adds two screen-sized targets. Unreal notes its voxel-based OIT compiles an extra shader version of every translucent material.
  • Alpha-tested foliageDiscard weakens early depth rejection, so dense foliage often needs its own depth pre-pass.

5 How it breaks

These six pictures cover most of the bug reports in this area. Each has a typical cause and a short, checkable request.

Flickering stripes on distant surfaces

Cause
Z-fighting: two surfaces closer together than one depth step at that distance. Common with decals, roads on terrain, coplanar walls and a very small near plane.
Ask for
The near and far planes and whether reversed-Z is on; a depth bias for decals; and geometry fixes that separate coplanar faces.

Glass hides what is behind it

Cause
A transparent material writes depth, and something behind it was drawn after it, so it fails the depth test inside the glass's outline.
Ask for
Depth writes off for blended materials, transparents drawn after opaques, sorted back to front; a check of any custom depth or sort settings on that material.

Transparent things pop in front of each other

Cause
Per-object sorting by bounds centre: large or overlapping transparent objects swap order when their centres pass each other as the camera moves.
Ask for
Splitting large transparent meshes, a sort priority for the pair that matters, or switching the effect to additive, cut-out or dithered so it no longer needs sorting.

Dark or bright halos around cut-outs

Cause
Straight-alpha textures filtered or mip-mapped: colour from invisible texels leaks into edge pixels.
Ask for
Premultiplied alpha in the texture and the blend mode, or a texture export that fills invisible texels with the edge colour.

Outlines drawn through walls, or missing

Cause
The outline pass ignores depth when it should not, or the stencil marks are never written: stencil not enabled for the pass, a wrong reference value, or a material that skips the marking draw.
Ask for
A frame capture showing the stencil buffer after the marking pass, and the stencil and depth settings of both draws.

Foliage thins out with distance

Cause
Alpha test on mip-mapped textures: averaged alpha in small mips falls below the threshold, so distant leaves are discarded.
Ask for
Mips generated to preserve coverage, a tuned threshold, or alpha to coverage; and a check with the camera at gameplay distance.

A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX confirms most of these in minutes. It shows each draw's depth, stencil and blend settings, and lets you view the depth and stencil buffers as images after any draw.

Depth reads in custom shaders

Any custom shader that reads the depth buffer, for soft particles, water edges, fog or outlines, depends on the engine's depth convention and the platform's graphics API. Unity's own documentation shows the same shader needing a flip on some APIs. Ask for depth reads to go through the engine's helper functions, never raw comparisons with 0 or 1.

7 Say it to your AI

Good briefs here name the material settings involved, describe the symptom with the camera movement that triggers it, and ask for the cheapest fix that removes the need for perfect sorting.

Bug reportUse when transparent objects sort wrongly
Engine: Unity 6, URP, PC and PS5. In the greenhouse level, glass panels in the roof sometimes hide the plants below them, and steam particles pop in front of and behind the glass as the camera pans.
The glass uses the URP Lit shader, Surface Type Transparent, Blending Mode Alpha. The roof is one mesh of 120 panels. The steam is a particle system with Blending Mode Alpha.

1. Explain each symptom in terms of depth writes, the per-object sort and draw order.
2. Propose fixes in order of cost: material settings, mesh splitting, Sorting Priority, changing the steam to additive or premultiplied.
3. For each fix, say what could still look wrong and from which camera angles.
4. Tell me what to look at in a RenderDoc capture to confirm the cause before we change anything.
  • Naming the shader and its settings lets the AI check depth writes and blend mode first.
  • "One mesh of 120 panels" points straight at the single-centre sorting problem.
  • Asking for residual failures stops it presenting a sort tweak as a cure.
BriefUse when adding a selection or x-ray outline
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.9, desktop. We need a 2-pixel selection outline on interactable objects, visible only where the object itself is visible, plus an optional x-ray silhouette when the object is behind a wall.

Design this with Custom Depth and the custom stencil:
1. Which project setting and per-object settings to enable, and which stencil values to reserve for which effect.
2. The post-process material logic for the outline and for the x-ray case, and how it tests depth.
3. The GPU cost at 1440p with up to 30 marked objects, and what grows it.
4. How we would check it with a stencil buffer view in a frame capture.

  • Reserving stencil values early avoids two features fighting over the same numbers.
  • A pixel count and an object count make the cost estimate concrete.

Questions to ask when reviewing depth, stencil and blending work

  1. Does any blended material write depth? Why?It is the most common cause of glass hiding what is behind it.
  2. Could this effect be additive, premultiplied or a cut-out instead of alpha-blended?Those need no sorting among themselves and batch better.
  3. What are the near and far planes, and is reversed-Z on for every target platform?Z-fighting at a distance usually comes from a near plane far smaller than needed.
  4. Which stencil values does this use, and what else in the project uses them?Stencil bits are a shared resource with no registry unless someone keeps one.
  5. How many blended layers can cover the screen in the worst case, at what resolution and format?Full-screen smoke or glass layers are where blending cost explodes.
  6. Do shaders that read depth use the engine's helpers?Raw depth comparisons break across reversed and standard depth.

8 Check yourself

  1. A glass window renders correctly from outside, but from one angle a lamp behind it disappears wherever it overlaps the window. What is the most likely setting at fault, and why does the angle matter?

    Show answer

    The window's material writes depth. From that angle the sort draws the window before the lamp, so the lamp fails the depth test inside the window's outline. Turn depth writes off for the window; the angle matters because it changes the per-object sort order.

  2. An open-world game flickers on roads 300 m away. The camera's near plane is 1 cm. What two changes would you ask about first?

    Show answer

    Raising the near plane, which shrinks depth steps everywhere, and making sure reversed-Z with a 32-bit float depth buffer is in use. For the roads themselves, a depth bias or a small physical offset from the terrain.

  3. Your VFX artist says the spark particles do not need sorting, but the smoke does. Are they right?

    Show answer

    Yes, if the sparks are additive: addition gives the same result in any order. Alpha-blended smoke does depend on order. Both still need the depth test against opaque geometry.
