11.2 Where GPU time goes

When the GPU sets the frame time, the next question is which part of its work, and which part of the chip, is running out. This chapter shows what grows with the number of pixels and what does not, the five parts of a GPU a pass can be limited by, what render targets cost in bytes, why a GPU needs many warps in flight, and the first questions to ask of a slow GPU frame.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

11.1 Frame budgets showed how to tell that the GPU is the bottleneck. A frame that is GPU-bound is still many separate passes, and each pass can be slow for a different reason. Some work happens once per pixel, so it grows with the internal resolution. Some happens once per triangle or once per shadow map, and the screen size does not touch it.

Inside the GPU, a pass also uses several different pieces of hardware: the parts that handle triangles, the shader cores that do the arithmetic, the texture units that fetch images, the memory that every byte passes through, and the output stage that writes finished pixels. Every pass uses all five. Whichever one is busiest sets how long the pass takes, and only work removed from that part makes it faster.

An everyday comparison

A restaurant kitchen on a busy night. The number of diners sets how many plates go out, the way the pixel count sets the work in a frame. But the kitchen can back up at the chopping boards, at the oven, in the walk to the pantry or at the pass where plates leave. Hiring another chef does nothing if every dish is waiting for the oven.

The problem it solves

Without knowing which part is full, GPU optimisation is guesswork: shaders get simplified in a pass that was waiting for memory, or triangles get cut in a frame dominated by pixels. Asking what grows with resolution, and which unit limits the slowest pass, turns "the GPU is slow" into a short list of specific jobs.

Why a lead cares

Internal resolution is the biggest single dial on GPU cost, and it is a product decision that trades against image quality, upscaling and frame rate (9.3). Beyond that dial, every GPU fix is aimed at one unit, and a fix aimed at the wrong one costs weeks for nothing. A lead who asks "does it scale with resolution, and what limits the pass?" before approving optimisation work keeps the team on the part of the frame that will pay back.

3 How it works

The steps start with the frame as a whole, what scales with pixels and what limits each pass, then go inside the chip to bytes, warps and registers, and finish with async compute, the counters that measure all of this and a short list of first questions.

1 Pixels, fill rate and resolution scaling

A 1080p frame has 2,073,600 pixels, 1440p has 3,686,400 and 4K has 8,294,400. Every pass that runs a pixel shader or a full-screen compute shader does its work once for each of them, and more where surfaces overlap (overdraw, from 5.4). Resolution scaling is how a pass's cost changes with the pixel count: a pure per-pixel pass costs 1.78 times as much at 1440p as at 1080p and 4 times as much at 4K, while shadow maps, vertex work and the CPU stay flat.

Fill rate is how many pixels per second the GPU's output stage can test, blend and write. Teams also use the phrase loosely for any cost that follows the number of pixels drawn, layers included. Godot's GPU optimisation guide gives the simplest test for it: turn off v-sync, compare the frame rate in a large window and a very small one, and if it rises a lot, the project is to some extent fill-rate-limited. Lowering the internal resolution with the engine's screen percentage does the same in any engine.

Simplified

Some modern systems choose their detail by screen pixels, so part of their cost grows with resolution too. Nanite picks triangles by how big they are on screen (4.5), and virtual shadow maps render pages only where on-screen pixels need them (7.5). Measure at two resolutions rather than assuming which side of the line a pass falls on.

2 Five ways to run out

A pass is ALU-bound when the shader cores' arithmetic is the busiest part: long material graphs, many lights per pixel, heavy maths in a post effect. It is texture-bound when the texture units, which fetch and filter texels, cannot keep up: blurs and screen-space effects with many taps, or high anisotropic filtering on large textures. It is bandwidth-bound when the bytes moving between VRAM, the caches and the chip are the limit, which is common for full-screen passes over large images; 3.5 introduced memory bandwidth.

It is geometry-bound when vertex shading, triangle setup or the rasteriser is the limit: shadow passes over dense scenes, very high triangle counts, or many triangles only a few pixels across, which waste most of their shading (small-triangle cost). And it is fill-rate-bound when the output stage, blending and writing pixels, is the limit: layers of smoke, glass and particles, or MSAA at high resolution.

The cure follows the limit. An ALU-bound pass needs less maths; a bandwidth-bound one needs fewer or smaller bytes; a texture-bound one needs fewer samples; a geometry-bound one needs fewer or larger triangles; a fill-rate-bound one needs fewer layers. Cutting the wrong thing leaves the pass as slow as before.

3 Pass by pass

A frame timeline (10.1) shows where the milliseconds go; adding what limits each pass, and whether it grows with pixels, shows where to cut. In a deferred frame the expensive passes are mostly per-pixel, so most of the GPU's time follows the resolution. The few that do not, such as shadow maps and simulations, are why lowering the resolution never removes the whole cost.

4 Render target formats and their bytes

Every render target has a render target format, which sets how many bytes each pixel takes and so how many bytes every pass that writes or reads it moves. RGBA8 and RGB10A2 take 4 bytes. R11G11B10F, a floating-point colour with no alpha, also takes 4. RGBA16F, the usual HDR colour format, takes 8, and RGBA32F takes 16. A 32-bit depth buffer takes 4.

Vendors give the same advice. NVIDIA's memory guidance recommends 32-bit colour formats such as R11G11B10_FLOAT over 64-bit ones such as R16G16B16A16_FLOAT to reduce bandwidth, and AMD's RDNA guide says to minimise the data a pixel shader writes and to avoid RGB32 formats. Godot's Mobile renderer uses R10G10B10A2 instead of the RGBA16F that Forward+ uses, which its documentation says halves the bandwidth required. The price of smaller formats is precision: Unity's URP documentation notes that its 64-bit colour option avoids banding at the cost of bandwidth.

5 Occupancy and latency hiding

Reading memory is slow compared with doing arithmetic. A shader that asks for a texel may wait hundreds of cycles for it. A GPU hides that wait. Each compute unit keeps many warps loaded at once, and when one stops to wait for memory, the scheduler switches to another that is ready, at no cost. 3.5 introduced the idea; this is latency hiding.

GPU occupancy is how many warps are loaded compared with the most that could be. AMD's guide defines it as assigned wavefronts divided by available slots, and RDNA 2 and RDNA 3 have 16 slots per SIMD. More warps in flight means more chances to find one ready to run, so low occupancy shows up as shader cores sitting idle while every warp waits.

More is not always better

AMD's occupancy guide warns that occupancy is not a silver bullet: a shader that is already limited by memory gains nothing from more warps, and more warps can thrash the caches. High occupancy matters for passes that wait a lot; for ALU-bound passes it matters little.

6 Register pressure

What limits occupancy most often is registers. Each compute unit has a fixed bank of very fast registers, and every warp loaded onto it needs its own set for as many values as the shader keeps live at once. A shader that holds more values needs more registers per lane, so fewer warps fit. This is register pressure. NVIDIA's analysis method names registers per thread as the most common occupancy limiter for pixel and compute shaders, and AMD's occupancy guide names the vector registers as the key resource.

Register pressure explains a common surprise: a small edit, such as one more texture sample kept alive across a loop, makes a whole pass slower out of proportion to the extra work. The edit pushed the shader over a register boundary, occupancy fell, and the waits stopped being hidden. AMD's RDNA guide suggests ways to cut register use, such as half-precision FP16 maths.

7 Async compute: filling idle units

Async compute, from 10.1, runs compute passes on a second queue alongside the graphics work. It pays off when the two passes use different units. NVIDIA's guidance is to overlap different datapaths, pairing bandwidth-heavy work with maths-heavy work, and not to overlap passes with high L1, L2 and VRAM throughput, because the caches start fighting. It also says to measure the difference over the whole frame or several frames, never one pass.

8 GPU counters and what they tell you

Timing a pass says how long it took; GPU counters say why. They are hardware performance counters that the GPU keeps for each unit, read out by vendor and console tools during a capture (11.5 covers the tools). The most useful single reading is each unit's unit throughput: how busy it was as a percentage of its peak. NVIDIA calls it SOL%, for speed of light.

NVIDIA's Peak-Performance-Percentage method reads them in a fixed order. Look at the units with the highest throughput first. If the top one is above 80%, the pass is running efficiently and the only fix is to give that unit less work. If it is below 60%, no unit is saturated and the pass is wasting time on idle or stalled cycles, so look at occupancy and what the warps are waiting for. Between the two, try both.

9 The first questions

Put together, the steps above make a short diagnosis that anyone can run before reaching for counters. Most slow GPU frames are answered by the first question alone.

Worked example. A PS5 has 448 GB/s of memory bandwidth, which at 60 fps is 7.47 GB per frame for everything the CPU and GPU read and write. A bloom pass at 4K reads an RGBA16F image once and writes one once: 8,294,400 pixels × 8 bytes × 2, or 133 million bytes. Even if it had the whole bus to itself it could never finish in less than 0.30 ms, however simple its maths. Running it at half resolution in each axis cuts the bytes to a quarter, 0.07 ms; storing the result as R11G11B10F halves that again. Simplifying the shader's arithmetic would have saved nothing, because the pass was waiting on bytes.

  • Internal resolutionPer-pixel passes scale with the pixel count, so 4K costs four times 1080p.
  • Layers per pixelEvery layer of smoke, foliage or glass shades and blends the same pixels again.
  • Bytes per pixelWider formats and extra render targets multiply the traffic of every pass that touches them.
  • Full-screen passesEach post effect, denoiser or upscaler reads and writes whole images.
  • Shader weightMore maths, more texture samples and more live registers per pixel; the last also lowers occupancy.
  • Triangles and shadow castersDense meshes, tiny triangles and many cascades load the geometry units at any resolution.

The usual trade

Most console games render below the output resolution and upscale (9.3), with dynamic resolution absorbing heavy scenes, because pixels are the cheapest dial to turn. Expensive screen effects such as AO, reflections and volumetrics often run at half resolution. Teams move render targets to the smallest formats that do not band and merge full-screen passes where they can. Geometry and shadow costs are tuned separately, through LODs, culling and shadow settings, because resolution does not reach them.

5 How it breaks

GPU time goes wrong when work grows with something nobody planned for: pixels, layers, bytes, registers, or two passes fighting over the same unit.

Fine at 1080p, far over budget at 4K

Cause
Most of the frame is per-pixel work, and the higher mode or platform renders four times the pixels without a matching budget.
Ask for
Per-pass GPU times at both resolutions, the internal resolution of each mode, and whether dynamic resolution or upscaling is on.

Explosions and smoke tank the frame

Cause
Overdraw: many large translucent layers shading and blending the same pixels, a fill-rate and bandwidth cost.
Ask for
The overdraw view of the worst moment, particle counts and screen sizes, and the cost with particles at half resolution or with fewer, smaller layers.

Lowering the resolution changes nothing

Cause
The frame is geometry-bound or limited by fixed-size work, or it is not GPU-bound at all and the CPU sets the pace.
Ask for
The lane times from 11.1, the triangle and shadow-caster counts, the shadow pass cost, and a timeline showing GPU idle gaps.

A new effect costs far more than its estimate

Cause
It was estimated at 1080p, or it adds wide formats and extra full-screen passes, so its bandwidth grows with every pixel and every byte.
Ask for
Each render target it adds, with format, size and reads and writes per frame, its cost at the shipping resolution, and a half-resolution version.

A small shader edit slowed a whole pass

Cause
Register pressure: the edit raised the registers per lane, fewer warps fit, and memory waits that were hidden now stall the cores.
Ask for
Register counts and occupancy before and after from the vendor's tools, and the unit throughputs of the pass.

Async compute made the frame slower

Cause
The overlapped passes use the same units, usually memory and caches, so each runs slower than it did alone.
Ask for
Whole-frame times with async on and off, on each target, and the unit throughputs of the paired passes.

7 Say it to your AI

GPU briefs go well when they give the resolution, the lane times and a per-pass capture, and ask for the limiting unit before any fix.

DiagnosisUse when the GPU lane is over budget
Unreal Engine 5.8, PS5, 60 fps target, 1440p internal with TSR to 4K output. In the market square stat unit reads Frame 19.2, Game 10.1, Draw 9.8, GPU 19.0. stat GPU captures at r.ScreenPercentage 67 and 33 attached.

1. For every pass over 0.5 ms, say whether its cost follows the pixel count, using the two captures.
2. For the three most expensive passes, say which unit most likely limits each (ALU, texture, bandwidth, geometry, fill rate) and what counter reading would confirm it.
3. Propose cuts that save 2.5 ms of GPU time, cheapest in image quality first, with the expected saving of each.
4. Say which changes would not help and why.
  • Two captures at different resolutions separate pixel cost from fixed cost without guessing.
  • Naming the limiting unit before the fix stops shader work on passes that wait for memory.
  • Asking what would not help catches optimisations aimed at the wrong unit.
AuditUse when post effects and render targets pile up
Unity 6.6, HDRP 17.4, PC and Xbox Series S, 1440p and 1080p internal.

1. List every full-screen render target our custom passes create: format, resolution, and how many times each is written and read per frame.
2. Compute the MB per frame and GB/s at 60 fps for each, at both resolutions.
3. Flag 16-byte formats, alpha channels nothing reads, and passes that could run at half resolution or merge with a neighbour.
4. Do not change anything yet. For each proposal, state the precision or banding risk and how we would see it on screen.
  • A table of formats and reads turns bandwidth from a feeling into a number.
  • Computing at both resolutions shows what the weaker console really pays.
  • Asking for the visual risk first keeps format changes from adding banding.

Questions to ask when reviewing GPU work

  1. Did the cost change when the resolution changed, and by how much?That one test separates pixel cost from everything else.
  2. Which unit limits the pass, and which reading shows it?The fix has to take work from that unit and no other.
  3. How many bytes per pixel do new render targets add, written and read?Bandwidth grows with every pixel and every pass that touches them.
  4. Did the shader's register count or occupancy change?Small edits can cost a whole pass through fewer warps.
  5. Was it measured over the whole frame, on the weakest target?Async overlap and cache effects only show at frame level.

8 Check yourself

  1. A frame's GPU time at 1080p is 10 ms, and 7 ms of it follows the pixel count. Estimate the GPU time at 1440p and at 4K.

    Show answer

    The fixed 3 ms stays. The per-pixel 7 ms grows by 1.78 at 1440p, to about 12.4 ms, and by 4 at 4K, to 28 ms. So about 15.4 ms at 1440p and 31 ms at 4K: inside a 60 fps budget at 1440p, far outside it at 4K.

  2. A blur reads a 4K RGBA16F image once and writes one once. How many bytes is that, and what is the least time it can take with a PS5's 448 GB/s?

    Show answer

    8,294,400 pixels × 8 bytes × 2 is about 133 million bytes, 126.6 MB. At 448 billion bytes per second that takes at least 0.30 ms, even with the whole bus to itself and no maths at all. Half resolution or a 4-byte format would cut it; simpler maths would not.

  3. On an RDNA 3 GPU with 1,536 registers per SIMD and 16 warp slots, a lighting shader goes from 96 to 160 registers. How many warps fit before and after, and when does it matter?

    Show answer

    Before, 1,536 ÷ 96 = 16, which is also the slot limit. After, 1,536 ÷ 160 = 9.6, so 9 warps. It matters if the shader spends much of its time waiting for memory, because 9 warps hide less waiting than 16. If the pass is ALU-bound it may barely change. Measure the pass before and after.
