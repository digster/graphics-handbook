3.2 Colour done right

The numbers stored in an image are not amounts of light: they sit on a curve chosen to suit the eye. Renderers must decode them to real light before adding, mixing or blurring, and encode again for the screen. Get that wrong and every blend, light and shadow in the game is subtly off.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Every pixel stores a colour as three numbers: how much red, green and blue light to show. It is natural to assume a bigger number means proportionally more light, so that 128 out of 255 is half as bright as 255. It is not. Images store their numbers on a curve that packs more steps into dark tones, because eyes notice small differences there and barely notice them in bright tones.

That curve is good for storage and wrong for arithmetic. Light adds up: two lamps give twice the light of one. Adding the stored numbers instead gives the wrong answer, and so does averaging, mixing or blurring them. A correct renderer decodes every colour to real light, does all its maths there, and encodes the result again just before the screen.

Everyday comparison

The decibel scale on a mixing desk. The fader marks are spaced to match how loud things sound, not how much sound energy there is. Two singers at "80 dB" together are not "160 dB"; you convert to energy, add, and convert back.

Image numbers are like the fader marks. Light is like the sound energy.

The problem it solves

Doing maths on the stored numbers makes blends between colours dark and muddy, light falloff harsh, blurs lose brightness, and lighting that looks right in one scene look wrong in the next.

Working in linear light makes lights add up, shadows scale and soft edges behave the way a camera would see them.

Why a lead cares

The colour pipeline is a project-wide decision that is expensive to change late: switching a project from gamma to linear means re-checking every texture, every hand-picked colour and every lighting setup. Mistakes show up as "the art looks washed out in engine" or "the particles look dirty", and artists lose days tuning around them. HDR output adds another layer: a separate signal, separate brightness settings, and a calibration screen players expect.

3 How it works

Follow a colour from a texture file to the screen. There are seven steps.

1 RGB, channels and bit depth

Screens make colour by mixing red, green and blue light, so renderers describe colour as RGB: three amounts, one per light. Each amount is a colour channel. Images and textures often carry a fourth, alpha, for opacity or for any other data that needs a slot.

The bit depth says how many bits store each channel, and so how many levels it can take. With 8 bits a channel has 256 levels, 0 to 255, and three channels make 16.7 million combinations. With 10 bits each channel has 1,024 levels, four times finer. Inside the renderer, values are usually floating-point numbers with far more precision; the bit depth matters most where colour is stored: textures, the framebuffer and the signal to the screen.

2 Stored numbers are not light: the sRGB curve

Almost every image a game uses is in sRGB, the standard colour space for PC screens and the web, published as IEC 61966-2-1. It fixes two things. First, exactly which red, green and blue the three channels mean. Second, a transfer function: the curve that turns a stored value into an amount of light.

That curve is close to "light = value to the power 2.2", which is why people call this gamma, and why values still on the curve are said to be "in gamma space". Values converted to plain amounts of light, where twice the number really is twice the light, are linear colour. A stored 0.5 decodes to 0.214 of full light. Going the other way, half the light encodes to 0.735, which is 188 out of 255.

Maths The sRGB transfer functions

Decoding a stored value V (0 to 1) to linear light L:

L = V / 12.92 if V ≤ 0.04045, otherwise L = ((V + 0.055) / 1.055)2.4

Encoding linear light back to a stored value:

V = 12.92 × L if L ≤ 0.0031308, otherwise V = 1.055 × L1/2.4 − 0.055

For 8-bit data, divide the stored number by 255 first. GPUs do both conversions in hardware when a texture or framebuffer uses an sRGB format, so shaders never write these formulas for ordinary textures.

sRGB also defines a colour gamut: the range of colours its three primaries can mix. It is the same gamut as Rec. 709, used by HD television. HDR uses a much wider one, Rec. 2020, which comes back in step 7.

3 Why images are stored on a curve

If the curve makes maths harder, why use it? Because of the eye. People notice a small change in a dark tone far more readily than the same change in a bright one. An 8-bit image has only 256 levels per channel to spend, and the sRGB curve spends them where they show.

4 Lighting maths must happen in linear

Light is additive. Two lamps on a wall give the sum of their light; a shadow that blocks half the light leaves half; a soft edge is an average of the light on each side. Every one of those operations is only correct on linear values. On stored values, sums overshoot, scaling undershoots, and averages come out too dark.

Mixing colours is the same sum in disguise: a 50/50 mix is half of one light plus half of the other. Try it below. Each pair is mixed twice, once on the stored numbers and once as light. Watch the middle of red and green, and then blur the bright lights.

Simplified

"Brightness" in the demo is relative luminance, which weights green most and blue least, as the eye does. Perceived lightness is a further curve on top of luminance. For lighting maths, only the linear values matter.

5 Decode on the way in, encode on the way out

A renderer that works in linear colour does three things. When it reads a colour texture, the GPU decodes sRGB to linear as it samples, before filtering, so the shader only ever sees light. Everything in between, lighting, blending, blur and post-processing, works on linear values. When the frame is finished, the output is encoded for the screen.

Not every texture holds colour. Normal maps, roughness and masks hold data: directions and amounts that were never put on the sRGB curve. They must be read exactly as stored. Marking a data texture as sRGB, or a colour texture as linear, is one of the most common colour bugs in games.

Because values in the middle are real amounts of light, they are not limited to the 0 to 1 a screen can show. A sunlit wall can be many times brighter than a lamp-lit one. Rendering with this open-ended range in floating-point buffers is HDR rendering, also called scene-referred rendering, because the values describe the scene rather than the screen. Squeezing that range into what a screen can show is tone mapping, the subject of 9.1 HDR, exposure and tone mapping.

6 Bit depth, banding and dithering

Wherever colour is squeezed into a fixed number of levels, a smooth gradient becomes a staircase. In bright, busy areas nobody notices. In a dark, gentle gradient, a night sky, fog, a vignette, a light falling off on a wall, the steps become visible stripes: colour banding. Video compression, low-precision intermediate images and 8-bit output all add to it.

The standard fix costs almost nothing. Dithering adds a tiny amount of noise, less than one level, just before the image is rounded to fewer levels. Instead of a hard edge between two levels, each pixel rounds up or down depending on the noise, and the mix of the two levels changes smoothly across the gradient. Up close it is fine grain; at viewing distance it reads as a smooth ramp.

7 HDR rendering is not HDR output

"HDR" means two different things in games, and briefs often mix them up. HDR rendering, from step 5, is how the renderer computes light internally; almost every modern game does it, even on a standard screen. An HDR display is about the screen: one that can show far brighter highlights and richer colours, and a signal that tells it to.

Screen brightness is measured in nits, candelas per square metre. The sRGB standard assumes a reference screen with white at 80 nits; typical PC and phone screens show standard white at roughly 100 to 300. HDR screens reach 400 to 1,000 nits or more in highlights; VESA's DisplayHDR certification has tiers from 400 to 1,400.

The most common HDR signal is HDR10. It uses 10 bits per channel, the Rec. 2020 gamut, and a new curve called PQ (perceptual quantizer, SMPTE ST 2084). Unlike sRGB, which is relative to whatever white the screen has, PQ values mean absolute brightness, up to 10,000 nits. The game has to know how bright the player's screen gets and where its interface white should sit, which is why HDR games ship a calibration screen.

HDR output is its own feature

Supporting HDR output means a second output path, brightness and paper white settings for players, interface brightness that matches, testing on several TVs and monitors, and screenshots and video capture that still look right. Budget it as a feature, not a checkbox.

4 What it costs

Working in linear colour is close to free at runtime: GPUs convert sRGB textures and framebuffers in hardware. The real costs are memory and bandwidth for higher-precision images, and people-time when the pipeline is set up wrong or changed late.

Unity's URP shows the trade directly: its HDR Precision setting offers 32-bit (the default) or 64-bit scene colour, and its documentation notes the 64-bit option avoids banding at the cost of bandwidth. Every screen-sized image is read and written several times a frame, so doubling its bytes per pixel shows up in GPU time, most of all on mobile.

What makes the cost grow

  • Precision of screen-sized images16-bit float doubles the memory and bandwidth of every full-screen pass that uses it.
  • Number of HDR intermediatesLighting, bloom, blur and post chains each keep screen-sized buffers alive.
  • Switching lateMoving from gamma to linear mid-project changes how every texture, colour and light looks, and all of it needs re-checking.
  • Mislabelled texturesEach colour texture marked linear, or data texture marked sRGB, costs an artist time chasing a look that the engine is distorting.
  • HDR outputCalibration UI, interface brightness, testing on many screens, and a second look for every marketing capture.

In proportion

The decision that matters most is cheap if made on day one: linear colour space, sRGB flags set correctly by texture type, and an import rule that enforces them. Unreal and Godot light 3D scenes in linear out of the box; Unity keeps a Gamma option only for hardware that needs it.

5 How it breaks

Colour bugs rarely crash anything. They make the game look slightly wrong, and artists compensate by hand until the compensations themselves become the problem. Learn these five.

Dirty blends and dark fringes

Cause
Blending, mixing or blurring on stored values: gamma colour space, an 8-bit non-sRGB render target, or a shader that mixes colours read without decoding. Shows on gradients, particle edges, UI fades and soft shadows.
Ask for
The project's colour space setting, the format of the render targets involved, and a test scene with red-to-green and black-to-white gradients.

Washed-out or too-dark textures

Cause
The sRGB flag is wrong for the texture. A colour texture read as linear looks pale and washed out; a normal or roughness map read as sRGB has its values bent, giving wrong shine and lumpy lighting.
Ask for
A list of textures whose sRGB setting does not match their type, and an import rule that sets it from the texture's role or file name.

Stripes in skies and fog

Cause
Too few levels somewhere in the chain: an 8-bit intermediate image, low HDR precision, compression, or 8-bit output without dither.
Ask for
Which buffer the steps first appear in (a frame capture shows it), the scene colour precision, and dithering on the final output.

HDR looks grey, or the HUD is blinding

Cause
The output does not match the screen: SDR content sent in an HDR signal without conversion, wrong peak or paper white values, or the interface drawn at full peak brightness instead of reference white.
Ask for
The HDR output path and its brightness settings, a calibration screen, and checks on at least one bright and one modest HDR screen.

Harsh light falloff that needs constant tweaking

Cause
Lighting computed on stored values, usually a project left in gamma colour space or a custom shader that skips decoding. Lights look too small with hard edges, and brightening them blows out the centre.
Ask for
Confirmation that lighting runs in linear, a list of custom shaders that read colours without decoding, and a before-and-after comparison scene.

Most of these can be confirmed in a frame capture: check each texture's format (an sRGB format name, such as one ending in _SRGB, means the GPU decodes it) and each render target's format and bit depth.

Unity projects started long ago

A project created in Gamma colour space keeps it until someone changes it. Changing the setting can reimport assets and changes how everything looks, so do it early, on a branch, with the art team watching.

7 Say it to your AI

Colour briefs go wrong when they say "make it look better". Name the colour space, the formats and the symptom, and ask for checks an artist can see.

BriefUse before switching a project to linear colour
Engine: Unity 6, URP. The project is in Gamma colour space and we want to move to Linear before content lock.

1. List every setting that changes: Player colour space, URP HDR and HDR Precision, camera Dithering.
2. Scan our textures and list any whose sRGB (Color Texture) setting does not match its role: colour textures with it off, masks, roughness or other data with it on.
3. List custom shaders and UI materials that mix or blend colours, and say which will look different in Linear and why.
4. Propose a test scene that shows the difference (gradients, overlapping lights, particle edges, UI fades) and the order to do the switch in so artists can review it.
  • Naming the settings stops the AI changing only the colour space switch.
  • A texture audit by role catches the most common colour bug in one pass.
  • A test scene gives the art team something concrete to sign off.
Bug reportUse when gradients show stripes
Godot 4.7, Forward+. In our night levels, the sky gradient and the fog show visible stripes on PC monitors. They get worse after the post-process chain and in video captures. Debanding is off.

Find which step introduces the steps: check the precision of each buffer from sky rendering to final output, and whether any pass writes to an 8-bit target before tone mapping. Then propose fixes in order of cost (debanding, higher precision for the offending buffer, dithering in a custom pass) and tell me how to verify each with a frame capture and a screenshot comparison.
  • Asking where the steps first appear finds the cause instead of hiding it.
  • "In order of cost" puts the cheap fix, dithering, first.
  • Video captures add their own banding, so the report says so up front.

Questions to ask when reviewing colour work

  1. Is every colour read decoded before it is used in lighting or blending?One shader that skips it produces the dark fringes everyone blames on art.
  2. How is the sRGB flag set for new textures?A rule based on texture role beats relying on people to remember a checkbox.
  3. What precision is each screen-sized buffer, and why?Too low bands; too high costs bandwidth on every pass.
  4. Where is the final image dithered?Without dither, 8-bit output shows steps in dark gradients.
  5. For HDR output, what are the paper white and interface brightness, and who chose them?Wrong values make HDR look worse than SDR, or make the interface glare.

8 Check yourself

  1. An 8-bit sRGB pixel stores 128. Roughly how much of full light does it give off? Which stored value gives off half?

    Show answer

    About 22%: 128 / 255 is 0.502, which decodes to about 0.216. Half the light encodes to 0.735, which is 188 out of 255.

  2. An artist says every colour texture looks pale and washed out in engine, while normal maps look fine. What do you check first?

    Show answer

    The sRGB flag on the colour textures. If it is off, their stored values are treated as linear light, so the midtones come out too bright when the result is encoded for the screen. Data textures like normal maps should have it off, which is why they look right.

  3. A foggy night level shows rings in the fog on an 8-bit screen. Name two fixes, and say which is cheaper.

    Show answer

    Dithering the final output, which turns the steps into fine noise at almost no cost, and raising the precision of whichever buffer first shows the steps, which costs memory and bandwidth. Dithering is cheaper, but it cannot fix steps that were baked in earlier at low precision.
