7.6 Reflections and image-based lighting

A shiny surface shows what is around it. Working out exactly what it should show means tracing rays through the scene, so real-time renderers cheat: they photograph the surroundings in advance, reuse the picture already on screen, or draw the scene a second time. Each cheat sees some things and misses others, and the same photographs also light every surface softly.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Look at a puddle and you see the sky and the buildings in it. Each point of the puddle shows whatever you would hit if your line of sight bounced off the water there. For a renderer, that means following a new ray from every reflective pixel into the scene and finding what it hits: the expensive question ray tracing answers directly.

Real-time engines mostly answer it with pictures. Some are taken ahead of time from fixed points in the level and looked up by direction. One is the frame the camera has just drawn, searched through its depth. One is a second rendering of the scene from a mirrored camera. Since physically based materials (6.5) give every surface some specular reflection, these pictures matter everywhere, not just on mirrors. Blurred, the same pictures also supply the soft light arriving from all around, which is what image-based lighting means.

Everyday comparison

A car advert needs the street reflected in the paintwork. The crew can film the real street around the car, which is exact and expensive. They can paste in a panoramic photo taken from the middle of the street, which looks right from roughly where it was taken. Or they can reuse footage the main camera already shot, which only contains what that camera happened to see.

The problem it solves

Every reflective pixel needs the colour of whatever its reflected ray hits, in any direction, every frame. Tracing those rays against the whole scene costs dedicated hardware.

Captured pictures turn it into one or two texture lookups per pixel, and the screen-space search reuses work the frame has already done.

Why a lead cares

Reflections carry the look of wet streets, cars, glass, water and metal, and their faults are easy to spot: reflections that vanish at the edge of the screen, interiors that glow with reflected sky, reflections that slide as the camera moves. Probes need placing and rebuilding, which is level-design time; planar reflections can double the cost of a frame; ray-traced reflections need hardware many players lack. Choosing the mix per platform is as much a production decision as a rendering one.

3 How it works

Start with what a reflection needs, then the captured pictures and how they are prepared, then the screen-space and rendered alternatives, and finally how engines chain them together.

1 What a reflection needs

For a perfect mirror, the reflected direction is the view direction bounced about the surface's normal, like a ball off a wall. Real materials have roughness: their microscopic facets point slightly different ways, so a pixel gathers light from a cone of directions around the mirror direction, and the reflection blurs. The Fresnel effect decides how strong the reflection is: water reflects about 2% looking straight down and almost everything at a grazing angle, which is why a puddle seen from far away is a mirror.

2 Environment maps: the surroundings as a picture

An environment map is a picture of the light arriving at one point from every direction, stored so it can be read by direction. Almost always it is a cubemap (6.1): six square images, each taken by a 90° camera pointing along one axis. To find the reflection, the shader takes the reflected direction and reads the cubemap there. It is one texture read, and it does not care where on the object the pixel is.

That is exactly right for things infinitely far away, such as the sky, whose direction does not change as you walk. It is wrong for nearby things, whose direction depends on where you stand: the further a pixel is from where the map was captured, the more its reflections of nearby walls and objects slide out of place.

The same picture can do more than reflect. Image-based lighting, IBL, uses it as a light source: every texel is light arriving from its direction, so a sky or an HDR photograph of a real place lights objects with all its colours and brightness at once. A material's specular part reads the environment along its reflection cone; its diffuse part needs the light from a whole half-sphere. Making both cheap is step 4.

3 Reflection probes: captures placed in the level

A reflection probe, which Unreal calls a reflection capture, is an environment map captured at a chosen point inside the level and applied to the surfaces within its area of influence. Artists place them in rooms, corridors and courtyards, so each area reflects its own surroundings instead of the sky.

  • Baked or real-time. Most probes are captured in the editor and never change. Real-time probes re-render all six faces while the game runs: Unity can spread that over 9 or 14 frames, and Godot's default Once mode captures when the probe moves and builds its blurred levels over the following six frames. Godot recommends at most one probe that updates every frame per scene.
  • Overlap and blending. Where probes overlap, engines blend them by influence and priority. In Unreal, smaller captures override larger ones; Unity uses an Importance value; Godot blends up to four at one spot.
  • Resolution. Unreal captures every probe at one shared resolution, 128 by default, set in the project settings, with at most 341 captures enabled at once.

A probe is still captured at one point. It shows the room as seen from that point, so reflections near the probe look right and reflections far from it slide, and anything that moves after the capture is not in it at all.

4 Rough surfaces: prefiltering and the split sum

A rough surface needs the average of the environment over a wide cone. Averaging hundreds of texels per pixel would be far too slow, so engines do it ahead of time. A prefiltered environment map stores blurred copies of the environment in the cubemap's mip levels, one blur per roughness: the sharp map in mip 0, ever wider cones in the smaller mips. At runtime the shader picks a mip from the pixel's roughness and reads once.

Prefiltering hides a problem: the right blur also depends on the viewing angle and on the material's BRDF, which one map cannot store for every case. Brian Karis's 2013 notes for Unreal Engine 4 describe the split-sum approximation most engines now use. It splits the reflection into two parts that can each be precomputed: the prefiltered environment map, and a small 2D table, indexed by roughness and viewing angle, that gives a scale and a bias for the material's base reflectance, F0. Multiply the two and the result is close to the full calculation. The material sphere in 6.5 uses exactly this for its ambient light.

Maths The split sum in one line

The specular light from an environment is an integral over directions l of incoming light L(l) times the BRDF f and the cosine term. Karis approximates the sampled version by splitting it into two averages:

(1/N) Σ L(l)·f(l,v)·cos θ / p(l,v) ≈ [ (1/N) Σ L(l) ] × [ (1/N) Σ f(l,v)·cos θ / p(l,v) ]

The first bracket is the prefiltered environment map, computed per roughness with the assumption that the view, normal and reflection directions are equal (n = v = r). The second depends only on roughness and the viewing angle once F0 is factored out, so it becomes a 2D lookup table of a scale and a bias: specular = prefiltered(R, roughness) × (F0 × A + B). The approximation is exact for a constant environment; Karis notes that the n = v = r assumption is the larger source of error, and it loses the long, stretched reflections seen at grazing angles.

Diffuse light needs the same treatment taken to the limit. An irradiance map stores, for each direction a surface could face, the total light arriving over its whole half-sphere, weighted by angle: the environment's irradiance, a term chapter 8.1 explains. It is so blurred that a cubemap of a few texels, or nine numbers per colour channel called spherical harmonics (8.2), can hold it.

5 Box projection: fixing parallax indoors

Inside a room, a probe's slide is obvious: a window reflected on the floor seems to follow you around. Box projection, also called a parallax-corrected cubemap, fixes it for the room itself. The probe is given a simple shape, usually a box matching the room's walls. For each pixel, the shader first intersects the reflected ray with that box, then reads the cubemap in the direction from the probe to the hit point. Sébastien Lagarde and Antoine Zanuttini presented the technique at SIGGRAPH 2012.

Box projection costs a ray-box test per pixel and works best in rectangular rooms; Godot's documentation notes it works less well in irregular ones. Unity calls it Box Projection on the probe, and in URP it must also be enabled in the URP Asset. Unreal offers Box Reflection Captures for corridors and rectangular rooms, and HDRP gives each probe a separate Proxy Volume shape: Box, Sphere or Infinite.

6 Screen-space reflections

Screen-space reflections, SSR, reuse the frame being drawn. For each reflective pixel, the shader steps along the reflected ray, projects each step onto the screen and compares its depth with the depth buffer. The first step that lands behind a stored surface is a hit, and the reflection takes that pixel's colour from the lit image. It is ray marching through what the camera saw, and it reflects anything on screen, including things that move.

The Lantern Courtyard's puddle uses SSR. Its shader takes 40 steps along each reflected ray, out to 16 m, with steps that grow with distance, then refines a hit with five halving steps. Hits fade out near the screen edges, and rays that miss or leave the screen take the sky's colour instead.

Production SSR adds three things. A depth pyramid, the same Hi-Z chain occlusion culling uses (4.4), lets the march skip empty space in big jumps. Rough surfaces read a blurred copy of the colour: HDRP's SSR reads a colour buffer with a blurred mip chain from the previous frame. And many implementations jitter rays and accumulate them over frames, which reduces noise at the risk of ghosting. McGuire and Mara's 2014 paper gives a production-proven tracer that ran ripply water reflections in 2 ms at 1080p on a 2012 laptop GPU. Chapter 8.4 takes SSR's blind spots further.

7 Planar reflections: render it twice

For a flat mirror, water surface or polished floor, the exact reflection is the scene seen from a camera mirrored below the plane. Planar reflections render exactly that view into a texture, clipped at the plane so nothing below it draws, and the reflective surface reads it at each pixel's screen position. Everything is right: off-screen objects, moving ones, correct parallax. The catch is that the scene is drawn a second time.

Epic's documentation is blunt: budget about half your frame time for a planar reflection, because the level renders again. Their examples measured about 23 ms extra in the Kite demo and about 1.67 ms in a mobile-oriented map; scene complexity, not screen resolution, drives the cost. Planar reflections only suit surfaces that are close to flat, one plane per render, and are usually limited to one or two visible at a time.

8 Ray-traced reflections

Hardware ray tracing (8.6) answers the reflection question directly by tracing each reflected ray against the scene's real geometry, on screen or not. What it hits still has to be lit, which is costly, so systems simplify there. Unreal's Lumen, on by default in new Unreal Engine 5 projects, traces against the screen first and falls back to tracing a simplified scene when that fails; its hit lighting comes from a cached representation of the surfaces by default, with full hit lighting as a more expensive option. Lumen uses dedicated reflection rays only below a roughness threshold, Max Roughness To Trace. HDRP's ray-traced reflections fall back to reflection probes and the sky when a ray misses, by default both.

9 Fallbacks: what to show when a method has no answer

No single cheap method covers every pixel, so engines chain them. A reflection fallback order decides which method answers a pixel when the more accurate one has nothing. HDRP documents its order as a reflection hierarchy: screen space first, then any reflection probe whose influence covers the pixel, then the sky, and finally black. Frostbite's 2014 course notes describe the same layering of four kinds: distant probes for the sky, local probes, SSR and planar reflections, mixed so that SSR fades into the local probes where it fails.

4 What it costs

Reflections spend in very different places: memory and editor time for probes, per-pixel work for SSR, whole extra renders for planar reflections and real-time probes.

Worked numbers. A 128 × 128 cubemap has 6 × 128 × 128 = 98,304 texels in its top mip, and about a third more with the mip chain: 131,072. At 8 bytes per texel, four half-floats for HDR colour, that is 1 MB per probe; at 256 it is 4 MB. A real-time probe re-renders the scene six times per update, which is why Unity time-slices it across frames. Lumen's reflections share one budget with its global illumination, translucency and fog: Epic targets 4 ms for all of them at 60 fps and 8 ms at 30 fps, at 1080p on consoles.

What makes the bill grow

  • Reflective pixelsSSR and ray-traced reflections cost per pixel; a screen full of wet street costs far more than a few puddles.
  • Roughness rangeRough surfaces need wider cones: more rays, more blur, more accumulation, or a fall back to probes.
  • Real-time updatesEvery real-time probe update and every planar reflection renders the scene again.
  • Probe count and resolutionMemory grows with the square of the resolution, and every probe needs placing, building and checking.
  • Steps per rayMore SSR steps reach further and miss less, linearly more expensive.
  • Hit lightingLighting what a ray-traced reflection hits can cost more than the trace itself.

In proportion

Most games combine cheap methods: baked, box-projected probes everywhere, SSR on top for contact and moving things, planar reflections only for a hero mirror or a lake, and ray tracing as a high-end option. The probes are also people-time: someone has to place, size and rebuild them whenever the level changes.

5 How it breaks

Reflection bugs are easy to see and usually point at one method in the chain.

Reflections fade at the screen edges

Cause
SSR has no data for rays that leave the screen, so it fades them out, and the fallback behind it is missing, too coarse or does not match.
Ask for
A probe placed and box-fitted for the area, so the fallback matches, and a check of the screen-edge fade setting.

Reflections in the wrong place that slide

Cause
A probe without parallax correction, or a box that does not match the room, so reflections of nearby walls answer from the capture point.
Ask for
Box projection with the box fitted to the walls, the capture point moved to where players stand, or a smaller probe for that area.

Interiors glow with reflected sky

Cause
No probe covers the area, so the fallback chain reaches the sky, or the probe captured the sky through an opening.
Ask for
Interior probes covering every enclosed space, set to ignore the sky (Godot's Interior option), and a review pass with the reflection view in each room.

Seams or popping between probes

Cause
Probes meet with no blend distance, or with very different captures, so the reflection switches at a line or changes as the player crosses it.
Ask for
Blend distances and priorities for overlapping probes, captures taken at matching lighting, and a walk-through recorded on video.

Reflections show an old version of the scene

Cause
Baked probes captured before a change: a door now open, a time of day that moved on, a lamp switched off.
Ask for
Which probes need real-time or on-demand updates, a rebuild step in the content pipeline, and SSR on the surfaces where it matters most.

To diagnose, look at one method at a time, as the courtyard's reflections view does above: switch SSR off, then the probes, and see which link of the chain the bad reflection comes from. A frame capture shows whether a planar reflection or a real-time probe is rendering the scene again.

Lumen replaces the classic chain

In Unreal, Lumen Reflections replace SSR, reflection captures stop contributing when Lumen global illumination is on, and planar reflections are not composited with Lumen GI and Reflections enabled. Advice written for Unreal Engine 4's capture-and-SSR setup often does not apply to a default Unreal Engine 5 project.

7 Say it to your AI

Reflection briefs need the engine and pipeline, which methods are in the chain, where the reflective surfaces are and how rough, and the budget. Adapt these.

BriefUse when planning reflections for a level
Engine: Unity 6.6, HDRP, Deferred. Target: PS5 at 60 fps, 2 ms for reflections.
Level: a rainy market street at night, wet cobbles (smoothness 0.6 to 0.9), shop windows, a few deep puddles, and three interiors visible through doorways.

1. Propose the reflection chain: which areas get SSR, which get reflection probes (baked or real-time), and whether any surface justifies a Planar Reflection Probe.
2. For each probe, give its position, Influence and Proxy Volume sizes, and whether it should exclude the sky.
3. Estimate the GPU cost of each part and the probe memory.
4. List the views and test route we use to check for sky leaking into interiors, edge fading and probe seams.
  • Smoothness ranges decide where SSR helps and where probes take over.
  • Asking for Proxy Volume sizes forces box projection to be thought through.
  • A test route turns a vague impression into checks someone can repeat.
Bug reportUse when puddle reflections disappear
Unreal Engine 5.8, Lumen on with hardware ray tracing off. In the courtyard the puddles reflect the lanterns well when the camera looks level, but when the player looks down at the puddles the lantern reflections vanish and the puddles show a dull grey.

Find the cause. Explain which part of Lumen's reflection tracing is answering in each case, why the lanterns drop out when they leave the screen, and what the grey comes from. Compare fixes: Lumen Scene settings, emissive lantern meshes in the Lumen Scene, hardware ray tracing, and a planar reflection for the largest puddle. Give the cost of each on PS5 and before-and-after screenshots from the same camera positions.
  • Looking down pushes the lanterns off screen: the classic screen-space miss.
  • Asking what the grey comes from points at the fallback in the chain.
  • Fixed camera positions make the comparison fair.

Questions to ask when reviewing reflection work

  1. What is the full fallback order for a reflective pixel here?Every visible failure is one link in that chain answering badly.
  2. Which probes are box-projected, and do their boxes match the walls?An unfitted box makes reflections slide.
  3. Which interiors could see the sky through the fallback?Missing interior probes make dark rooms glow.
  4. What renders the scene more than once: real-time probes, planar reflections?Each is a whole extra scene render.
  5. How are rough surfaces handled: blur, accumulation or probes?Temporal accumulation brings ghosting; probes bring parallax errors.
  6. When are baked probes rebuilt, and who owns that?Stale probes are a content-pipeline problem that no shader change fixes.

8 Check yourself

  1. A puddle reflects a street lamp well until the camera tilts down, then the lamp's reflection disappears. Which method was reflecting the lamp, and why did it stop?

    Show answer

    Screen-space reflections. Tilting down pushes the lamp off the top of the screen, so the depth buffer and the lit image no longer contain it, and the pixel falls back to a probe or the sky.

  2. In a long corridor, the reflection of a bright doorway on the floor seems to slide along with the player. What would you ask for?

    Show answer

    Box projection on the corridor's probe, with its box fitted to the corridor walls, so the shader intersects each reflected ray with the walls before reading the cubemap. If it is already on, check that the box matches the walls.

  3. Why can a rough metal ball and a polished one read the same reflection probe for about the same cost?

    Show answer

    The probe is prefiltered: blurred versions for increasing roughness are stored in its mip levels ahead of time. Each pixel reads once from the mip its roughness selects, and the split-sum table supplies the rest of the specular response.
