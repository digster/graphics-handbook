Part 1 · How games get made

1.2 Who does what

A game's graphics pass through many hands on the way to the screen: artists who design and build, technical artists who connect art to the engine, programmers who build the renderer and the tools, and producers and testers who keep it moving and honest. Knowing who makes what and who owns each decision tells you who should brief, review and sign off any piece of work, including work an AI agent does.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Making a game's graphics is a relay. Someone imagines a thing, someone builds it in 3D, someone paints its surfaces, someone makes it react to light in the engine, someone lights the place it lives in, and someone checks that the result runs fast enough. Programmers build the machinery that every one of those steps runs on.

Each person in the relay owns some decisions and hands on some files. Most graphics problems on real projects are not about the technique itself. They come from a decision nobody owned, or a file handed on without the information the next person needed.

Everyday comparison

Building a house needs an architect who decides how it looks, builders and decorators who make it, electricians and plumbers who install the systems, a project manager who books everyone in the right order, and an inspector who checks the work. A good site engineer translates the architect's drawings into something the builders can actually build with the materials and the regulations they have.

The art director is the architect, the artists are the builders and decorators, the programmers install the systems, the producer is the project manager and QA is the inspector. The technical artist is the site engineer.

The problem it solves

Without clear roles, two things go wrong at once. Some decisions get made twice, by people who disagree, and some never get made at all until they surface as a bug.

Clear roles and clear handoffs give every decision one owner, and every file one next stop.

Why a lead cares

Graphics problems are usually ownership problems in disguise: a level that runs at 24 fps because nobody owned the light budget, a shader that looks wrong on one console because nobody checked it there. When AI agents take on slices of these roles, the question gets sharper. Every piece of agent output needs a named human in the matching role who reviews it, and you need to know which role that is.

3 How it works

Meet the people first, then follow how they hand work on, how decisions are owned, and how the same roles shrink or multiply with the size of the team. There are nine steps.

2 The handoff chain

Follow one asset, a market stall in a fantasy town, along the middle row of the picture.

  1. The concept artist paints the stall from a few angles, with notes on materials and scale, and the art director approves it.
  2. The 3D artist builds a detailed high-poly model for baking fine detail, and a lighter low-poly model for the game, within the triangle budget for props.
  3. A texture artist, often the same 3D artist, paints the colour, roughness and other surface maps.
  4. The technical artist has already built the material the stall uses, with the parameters artists may change. The stall becomes a set of material instances of it, imported with the agreed rules.
  5. The lighting artist lights the market, which is when the stall's materials are judged under the real light of the game.
  6. The performance review captures the market on target hardware. If it is over budget, a fix list goes back to whichever step caused the cost.

Every arrow carries more than a file. It carries a spec, such as scale, budgets and naming, and an acceptance check. When one of those is missing, the next person either guesses or sends the work back. 1.3 The content pipeline follows the files themselves through the tools and formats.

3 The technical artist: the bridge

Technical artists rarely make the final art, but nearly every visual in the game passes through something they built. Creative Assembly's Jodie Azhar described the role as the area between art and technology: working closely with the graphics programmers so that visuals render correctly in the game, streamlining art pipelines, and building tools for artists. GDC's long-running Technical Artist Bootcamp has covered the same ground, from optimising assets for run-time performance to the observation that technical artists spend much of their time on internal tools and libraries.

Two pieces of technical art matter most to a lead. The first is the material library: a small set of master materials with exposed settings, so that artists can make a thousand surfaces without a programmer and without a thousand separate shaders. The second is the import rules and budget checks that catch an over-budget asset on the day it arrives, months before alpha.

4 Four kinds of programmer

The word programmer covers very different jobs. The four kinds that matter for graphics work at different heights of the same stack. Gameplay programmers work at the top, on what the player does. Graphics programmers own the renderer. Engine programmers own the foundations everything stands on. Tools programmers work beside the stack, on the software the team uses to make the game.

On most teams that use a commercial engine, there are few or no engine programmers: the engine's maker does that work. Graphics programmers on those teams spend more time configuring, extending and profiling the engine's renderer than writing a new one.

5 Lighting and effects come last

Lighting artists and VFX artists work at the end of the chain, which makes them sensitive to every change upstream. Move a wall and the lighting in that room may need redoing; if the game uses baked lighting, the room's lighting has to be recalculated too. Change a material and an effect that blends with it can look wrong.

They are also where performance budgets are most often spent. Every light that casts a shadow and every layer of overlapping smoke costs GPU time, and the cost depends on the camera, so the same effect can be cheap in one shot and expensive in another. That is why lighting and VFX artists need a clear budget from the technical artist or graphics programmer, and access to the same profiling tools.

6 Producers, QA and the review loop

Producers and QA keep graphics problems moving from found to fixed. QA testers play every build, write reproducible bug reports and, on many teams, run scripted performance captures on each target platform. Producers turn those reports into prioritised tasks with owners and dates. Together they run a loop that repeats with every build.

7 Who decides: a RACI grid

A RACI grid lists decisions against roles and marks, for each decision, who is Responsible for doing the work, who is Accountable for the result, who is Consulted before it is made, and who is Informed afterwards. The rule that makes it useful is one Accountable person per row. Here is a typical grid for graphics decisions; your team's will differ, and writing it down is the point.

8 Same roles, different team sizes

The roles do not change with team size. The number of people holding them does. Eric Barone made Stardew Valley alone over about four and a half years, acting as designer, programmer, artist, writer and composer. Supergiant's creative director put the studio at fewer than 20 people while it made Hades. Larian had about 400 people across seven studios working on Baldur's Gate 3 in 2022.

Two things carry over at every size. First, the technical artist and graphics programmer rows are the ones small teams most often leave implicit, and they are where performance problems hide. Second, the bigger the team, the more of each person's time goes into handoffs, which is why large studios invest so heavily in tools and pipeline.

9 Where an AI agent takes a slice

An AI agent takes a slice of the drafting inside a role, and the human in that role decides, checks and signs off. The useful question for each role is which part is drafting and which part is judgement.

Agents also blur the handoff chain. One agent can draft a model, its material and a lighting pass in an afternoon, skipping the specs and checks that each handoff normally carries. Treat each step's acceptance check as still required, with the matching human role signing it off, however the draft was made.

4 What it costs

Roles cost headcount, and handoffs cost time. The headcount range across real projects is enormous.

1person made Stardew Valley: design, programming, art, writing and music
< 20people at Supergiant while it made Hades
≈ 400people across seven Larian studios on Baldur's Gate 3, 2022

Handoffs cost calendar time

Take an illustrative prop that needs 4 days of hands-on work spread across six steps: concept, model, textures, material setup, placement under the level's lighting, and a performance check. If it waits an average of 2 working days in someone's queue at each of the six handoffs, it reaches the game after 4 + 6 × 2 = 16 working days, more than three weeks. Three quarters of that time is waiting. Fewer handoffs, smaller queues and clear acceptance checks shorten it far more than faster work at any single step.

Missing roles cost people-time

Suppose a 20-artist team has no technical artist and no material library, so artists wait for a programmer to expose a material setting or fix an import. If that costs an artist half a day, and it happens 40 times a month, the team loses 40 × 0.5 = 20 artist-days a month, about one full-time artist, before counting the programmer's time. A technical artist who builds the library and the import checks pays for the role on that alone. These numbers are illustrative; measure your own by counting requests in the bug tracker.

  • Number of handoffsEach one adds a queue and a chance to lose information.
  • Unowned decisionsThey resurface later as bugs and arguments, usually at a worse time.
  • Single points of knowledgeOne graphics programmer who understands the renderer becomes a queue for the whole team.
  • Outsourcing distanceTime zones and contracts turn a quick question into a day's round trip, so specs must be complete.
  • Review capacityMore drafted content, from people or agents, needs more reviewer hours in the right roles.
  • Rework loopsAn asset sent back twice costs its hands-on time three times.

Hiring order

On small and mid-size 3D teams, the first technical artist is often the hire with the largest effect on graphics quality per person, because it multiplies the output of every other artist. If you cannot hire one, name who does the job and give them time for it.

5 How it breaks

Role problems look like technical problems from the outside. These five are the commonest in graphics work.

Art that fails late

Cause
Nobody owns the space between art and code. Budgets exist on paper, but no import check enforces them and nobody profiles art content until alpha.
Ask for
A named owner of budgets and import rules, technical artist or not, and automatic checks that run on every new asset.

Every shader tweak waits for a programmer

Cause
Materials are hand-written shaders with nothing exposed, so artists queue for the one graphics programmer for every colour, tiling or roughness change.
Ask for
A material library with the common settings exposed, built and owned by a technical artist, with the programmer reviewing its cost.

Lighting redone again and again

Cause
Lighting started before level geometry was locked, and nobody told the lighting artist when walls moved. Each change triggers relighting, and rebaking where lighting is baked.
Ask for
A geometry lock date per level before final lighting, and a notification whenever a locked level changes.

Performance found by players

Cause
QA tested function on development PCs, and nobody owned scripted performance captures on the target hardware, so regressions were never measured before release.
Ask for
Automated captures of fixed scenes and camera paths on each target, every build, with an owner for each regression.

Agent output with no owner

Cause
Agent-written shaders, tools or assets were merged because they worked on one machine, without review by the person in the matching role.
Ask for
A rule that every agent change names its human reviewer by role, using the right-hand column of the slice figure as the review checklist.

6 In the engines

Each engine gives the graphics roles their own tools. Knowing their names helps you match a task, and an agent's output, to the role that should review it.

Shader Graph builds shaders from connected nodes; supported in URP, HDRP and the Built-in Render Pipeline; custom render pipelines are unsupported. 7 Say it to your AI

When you brief an agent on role-shaped work, say which role it is drafting for, who will review the result, and what that reviewer will check. That gives the agent the acceptance check the handoff would normally carry.

BriefUse when assets keep arriving over budget
You are drafting a tool for our technical artist, who will review it before any artist uses it.
Engine: Unity 6.6, URP. Targets: PC and mid-range Android phones.

Write an editor tool that checks every newly imported model and texture against our budgets (table attached: triangles per asset type, texture sizes, material count, LOD count).
1. Report each asset over budget, by how much, and which rule it breaks. Do not change any asset automatically.
2. Run on import and on demand for a whole folder.
3. Include tests with one asset that passes and one that fails each rule.
4. List anything in the budget table that is ambiguous, so the technical artist can decide it.
  • Naming the reviewer's role tells the agent who the output is for and what will be checked.
  • Report-only keeps the decision with the technical artist.
  • Asking for ambiguities surfaces rules nobody has written down.
PlanUse when nobody is sure who owns a graphics decision
Our team: 1 art director, 6 3D artists, 1 technical artist who also does VFX, no dedicated lighting artist, 2 programmers (one mostly rendering), 1 producer, external QA. Unreal Engine 5.7, PC and consoles.

Draft a RACI grid for these graphics decisions: visual target, frame-time and memory budgets, shader and material complexity, LOD rules, lighting approach and light counts, quality tiers per platform, milestone performance sign-off, fix priority.
- Exactly one Accountable per row.
- Flag rows where one person is Accountable for so much that they become a bottleneck.
- Flag work no one on this team can be Responsible for, and suggest whether to hire, train or outsource.

  • The one-A rule forces the useful argument.

Questions to ask when reviewing AI work in any graphics role

  1. Which role would normally own this, and has the person in that role reviewed it?Agent output skips the handoff checks unless you put them back.
  2. Does it change something another role hands on, such as a material other artists use?A change to shared work ripples down the chain.
  3. Is it inside the budgets the budget owner set, measured on target hardware?A draft that only runs well on a development PC moves the cost downstream.
  4. Who maintains this tool or shader after today?Code nobody owns breaks quietly at the next engine upgrade.
  5. Would the person in that role put their name to it?It is the simplest test of whether the review really happened.

8 Check yourself

  1. New rock assets look great, but the canyon level has dropped from 60 to 40 fps on console. Who do you go to first, and with what?

    Show answer

    The owner of budgets and import rules, usually the technical artist, with a frame capture from the graphics programmer or QA on the target console. Together they can tell whether the rocks break the triangle, texture or material budget, and send a fix list back to the 3D artist.

  2. Your 6-person team has no technical artist. Does that mean nobody does technical art?

    Show answer

    No. Materials, import rules, budgets and art profiling still have to happen, so they fall on whoever is nearest, usually a generalist artist or the programmer. Name who it is and give them time for it.

  3. An AI agent writes a new water shader that looks good in the editor. Who should sign it off before it ships?

    Show answer

    The technical artist for how it fits the material library and the budgets, the graphics programmer for correctness and performance on every target platform, and the art director for the look.
