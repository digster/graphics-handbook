4.1 Meshes

Everything you see in a 3D game is a hollow shell of flat triangles. A mesh is that shell plus a few facts stored at every corner: where it is, which way the surface faces there, and how a flat image wraps over it.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A 3D model in a game is hollow. There is nothing inside a character or a crate, only a surface, and that surface is made of flat triangles joined edge to edge. The surface plus the data stored at its corners is a mesh.

Each corner, with its data, is a vertex. It always has a position. It usually has a normal, the direction the surface faces at that corner, which lighting needs. It usually has UV coordinates, which say which point of a flat image lands on that corner. Meshes that use normal maps add a tangent, which says which way the image runs across the surface, and some add a vertex colour, a value painted onto each corner.

None of this is drawn as such. The GPU fills each triangle with pixels and blends the corner values across it, so a few numbers at three corners decide the look of everything in between.

Everyday comparison

A tailored jacket is made of flat pieces of cloth sewn into a curved shape. On the cutting table every piece lies flat; the seams are where the flat pieces meet on the body.

A mesh works the same way. The surface is flat pieces, triangles, joined into a shell. Its UV layout is the cutting pattern that lets a flat image wrap around it, and it has seams too.

The problem it solves

A GPU cannot draw a sphere, a face or a rock directly. It can fill triangles extremely fast.

Describing every surface as triangles plus a few numbers per corner gives the GPU one simple job to repeat millions of times a frame, and puts everything that makes objects look different into the data.

Why a lead cares

Meshes are where art, engineering and performance meet. Triangle budgets per platform shape how artists model. Import settings for normals and tangents decide whether a model looks in the engine the way it looked in the artist's tool. Seams, hard edges and flipped faces are among the most common visual bugs between art and code. With this vocabulary you can write the asset rules, review them, and have an AI check every import against them.

3 How it works

Follow a mesh from the shape itself to the data at each corner. There are seven steps.

1 Why triangles

Three points always lie on one flat plane. Whatever three corners you pick, there is exactly one flat triangle through them, so the GPU never has to guess what surface it is filling. Four points need not lie on a plane: lift one corner of a square and it can fold two different ways, depending on which diagonal you fold along.

A triangle is also always convex. A line across it goes in once and comes out once, and a pixel is inside it exactly when it is on the inner side of all three edges. That test is simple enough to build into fixed hardware, which is why every GPU draws triangles and everything else is made of them. Modelling tools call their faces polygons; by the time a mesh reaches the GPU, every polygon is a triangle.

2 Quads, n-gons and topology

Artists rarely model in triangles. Modelling tools work mostly in quads, four-sided faces, because rows of quads form clean lines of edges called edge loops that are easy to select, slide, cut and smooth. Tools also allow an n-gon, a face with more than four corners, which is convenient while modelling and risky later.

On export or import, every face becomes triangles. This triangulation is why a model of 6,000 quads arrives in the engine as 12,000 triangles, and why teams quote a polygon count in triangles. A quad can be split two ways and an n-gon many ways, and different tools choose differently. Triangulate once, at a fixed point before normal maps are baked, so the baker and the game see the same triangles. glTF files hold only triangles, so a glTF exporter always triangulates.

How the corners, edges and faces connect is the mesh's topology. Good topology puts edge loops where the shape bends, around eyes, mouths and joints, so it deforms cleanly when animated, and keeps the rest simple so it is easy to unwrap and reduce. A mesh should also be manifold: a surface that could be the skin of a real object, with every edge shared by at most two faces. Lightmap bakers, physics, mesh reduction and many other tools assume it.

3 Winding order and front faces

Each triangle lists its three corners in an order. Seen from one side, that order goes anticlockwise; seen from the other side, clockwise. The GPU uses this winding order to tell a triangle's front from its back, and most renderers skip back faces entirely, because on a closed mesh they are hidden anyway. That is backface culling. On a closed mesh roughly half the triangles face away at any moment, and culling skips all of them.

Which order counts as the front is a convention that varies between APIs and file formats; glTF, for example, uses anticlockwise. The face's normal follows from the same order: the cross product of two edges, from 3.3 Vectors you can see, points out of the front.

Mirroring flips it. Scaling a mesh by −1 along one axis turns every anticlockwise triangle clockwise, so a mirrored object would render inside out unless something compensates. glTF spells out that a negative scale flips the front-facing order, and Unreal can build a reversed index buffer for meshes used with mirrored transforms.

4 Normals: smooth and faceted

Normals are stored with each vertex; the GPU never works them out from the triangles. Each vertex carries one, and the GPU blends the three normals of a triangle across its surface, pixel by pixel, before lighting. That is what lets a low-poly sphere look round: with averaged normals it is lit as if it were curved, and only its outline gives the triangles away.

Where two faces meet, the artist chooses. If the vertices on the shared edge use one averaged normal, shading flows across the edge and it looks soft. If each face keeps its own normal, the shading jumps and the edge looks sharp. This choice, made edge by edge, is called hard and soft edges; some tools group faces into smoothing groups instead, and Blender marks edges as sharp. A hard edge needs separate vertices on each side, one per normal, which is where split vertices come from.

5 UVs and seams

UV coordinates place each vertex on a flat image, with U across and V up, usually each from 0 to 1. 6.1 Textures and UVs covers the images themselves; here the point is the cut. A closed surface cannot lie flat without being cut open, like the jacket's pattern pieces. Each cut is a UV seam, and each separate flat piece is called a UV island.

A vertex on a seam belongs to two places in the layout, so it needs two sets of UVs and is stored twice. Seams also show: a texture rarely lines up perfectly across one. So artists put seams where they hide, under arms, on the back of props, and along hard edges, which split their vertices anyway and so cost nothing extra.

6 Tangents and bitangents

A normal map, covered in 6.4 Normal maps and fake detail, stores small tilts of the normal in a texture, written relative to the surface: tilt a little toward the texture's U direction, a little toward its V. To apply that, the shader needs to know which way U and V run across this particular triangle. The tangent gives the U direction along the surface. The bitangent gives the V direction. Together with the normal they form the tangent frame.

Engines usually store the tangent plus a sign and rebuild the bitangent with a cross product. The sign records whether the UVs are mirrored, which artists do to reuse texture space on symmetrical models; glTF keeps it in the tangent's fourth number. Tangents must also be computed the same way the normal map was baked, or the lighting bends wrongly. MikkTSpace is the common standard for this, and Unreal, Unity and Godot can all generate MikkTSpace tangents on import.

4 What it costs

Triangles cost in three places. Every vertex is processed in every pass that draws the mesh. Every vertex takes memory, as 4.2 shows. And triangles that cover only a few pixels waste pixel work, because GPUs shade pixels in small blocks and a tiny triangle fills only part of each block. The first two are why teams set budgets; the third is why more triangles stop helping once they shrink to pixel size.

Put numbers on it. A Quest 2 scene with 12 characters of 20,000 triangles each spends 240,000 triangles, a quarter to a third of Meta's 750,000 to 1 million, before any environment. Each of those characters has roughly 12,000 vertices after hard edges and seams. At the 56-byte skinned layout from 4.2, that is 672,000 bytes of vertex data plus 120,000 bytes of 16-bit indices: about 0.8 MB.

The other big cost is people-time. Clean topology, UV unwrapping and the lower-detail versions of each model (4.5 Level of detail) are skilled work, and budgets agreed late in production force artists to redo it. Set triangle budgets per asset type and platform before full production, and check them on import.

What makes the costs grow

  • Triangles on screenEvery visible triangle costs vertex work in every pass, including each shadow pass.
  • Vertices after splitsHard edges and UV seams add vertices beyond the corner count: three per corner on a crate.
  • Tiny trianglesTriangles a few pixels across waste pixel work and gain no visible detail.
  • Extra attributesA second UV set, colours and tangents add bytes to every vertex and can split more of them.
  • AnimationSkinned meshes carry bone weights and cost more per vertex; 4.6 Moving geometry covers them.
  • ReworkEvery change of budget after modelling means new topology, new UVs and new bakes.

5 How it breaks

Mesh bugs show up as shading problems, missing faces and texture errors. Most come from the hand-over between the modelling tool and the engine.

Faceted shading on a smooth model

Cause
Normals were not exported, or the engine recomputed them with every edge hard, discarding the artist's smoothing.
Ask for
The import's normal setting (import or compute) and whether the exporter wrote normals and smoothing groups.

Lit from the wrong side, or black

Cause
Normals point into the mesh instead of out, usually after flipping faces or mirroring in the modelling tool without recalculating them.
Ask for
A check of normal direction in the tool's normals view, and recalculating normals outward before export.

Faces vanish from one side

Cause
Backface culling on a single-sided surface, such as a leaf, a flag or an open window, or triangles whose winding order was flipped by mirroring or an axis conversion.
Ask for
Two-sided materials for thin surfaces that need them, and a check of mirrored transforms and import axis settings.

Visible texture seams or stretching

Cause
Seams placed where players look, islands with different texel density on either side, or UVs squashed in one direction.
Ask for
A checker-texture review of the UVs, with seam placement and texel density checked against the asset rules.

Normal-map seams, or bumps that turn into dents

Cause
Tangents in the engine differ from the ones the normal map was baked with, often on mirrored UVs, or the mesh was triangulated differently for the bake.
Ask for
MikkTSpace tangents on both sides, triangulation before baking, and the import's tangent setting checked.

To confirm a mesh problem, look at the mesh in the engine's own viewers first, then in a frame capture tool such as RenderDoc, which can show any draw's vertices with their normals, UVs and colours.

7 Say it to your AI

Good mesh briefs name the engine, the source tools and file format, the budgets in numbers, and exactly which import settings are in play. Adapt these.

BriefUse when setting up mesh import checks
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7. Artists export FBX from Blender and Maya. Platforms: PC and PlayStation 5.

Write an editor validation that runs on every static and skeletal mesh import and reports:
1. Triangle count against our budgets: props 3,000, large props 20,000, characters 80,000 (LOD 0).
2. Missing UV channels, and lightmap UVs that overlap.
3. Non-manifold edges and degenerate triangles.
4. Whether normals were imported or computed, and whether tangents use MikkTSpace.
5. Faces with more than four corners in the source, if the importer can tell us.

For each check, say whether the engine already offers it or it needs custom code. Output a CSV per import batch and an editor warning per failing asset.
  • Budgets in numbers give the AI something to test against.
  • Asking which checks already exist avoids rebuilding engine features.
  • Normal and tangent settings are the most common cause of art looking different in the engine.

Find the cause. Compare the tangent basis used by the baker with the model's Tangents import setting, check how the mirrored half's tangent sign is handled, confirm the mesh was triangulated before baking, and check the Normals setting is Import.   • "Correct in the baker" rules out the texture itself.

Questions to ask when reviewing mesh work

  1. Are normals and tangents imported from the file or recomputed by the engine?Recomputing throws away the artist's hard and soft edges and can break normal maps.
  2. Where is the mesh triangulated, and is it the same triangulation the bake used?Two different splits of the same quads shade differently.
  3. What are the triangle budgets per asset type and platform, and what checks them?Budgets that nothing checks are not budgets.
  4. How many vertices does the mesh have compared with its corners?A ratio far above what its hard edges and seams explain means wasted splits.
  5. Does any code assume a winding order or axis convention? Where is it converted?One wrong assumption turns every mesh inside out.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why do GPUs draw triangles rather than quads?

    Show answer

    Three points always lie on one plane and always form a convex shape, so filling a triangle is unambiguous and needs only three simple edge tests. Four corners can fold two ways, so a quad does not even define a single surface until it is split.

  2. An artist's tool shows 6,000 faces on a prop modelled in quads. The engine reports 12,000 triangles. Is something wrong?

    Show answer

    No. Each quad becomes two triangles on export or import. Check the model for n-gons, which split into more triangles and often into thin slivers.

  3. A rock looks smooth in Blender but faceted in the engine. What do you check first?

    Show answer

    The normals: whether the exporter wrote them, and whether the engine imports or recomputes them. In Unreal, Compute Normals discards the file's normals and smoothing groups; in Unity, Normals set to Calculate with a small Smoothing Angle makes every edge hard.
