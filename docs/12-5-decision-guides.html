12.5 Decision guides

The big rendering choices are made early and are expensive to change: the engine and its defaults, the render path, shadows, global illumination, anti-aliasing and upscaling. This chapter gives a short decision guide for each, driven by three constraints: platform tier, team size and art style. It ends with a comparison across platform tiers, and with how to use this book with your AI.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A decision guide is a short chain of questions about a project's constraints that ends at a starting choice and says what that choice trades away. It is not a substitute for measuring. It narrows a wide-open question, such as "which global illumination should we use?", to one or two options worth prototyping, and it records why, so the decision can be revisited when a constraint changes.

Three constraints do most of the narrowing. A platform tier groups target devices with similar power, memory and frame-rate expectations: mobile, handheld, console, high-end PC and VR. Team size decides how many systems the team can own and tune. Art style decides what must look right: a photoreal game needs bounced light and stable fine detail; a pixel-art game needs neither, and is harmed by some of the techniques that serve the other.

An everyday comparison

Choosing a vehicle for a job. A delivery firm in a medieval city centre, a farm and a removals company all need "a vehicle", and the right one follows from the roads, the loads and the drivers. Nobody starts from which vehicle is best; they start from where it must go and what it must carry.

The problem it solves

Without a guide, teams pick techniques from trailers, talks and other studios' games, which were made under other constraints. With one, every big choice starts from the project's own platforms, people and look, and the reasons are written down.

Why a lead cares

These choices are made in pre-production and paid for through to release. They set the frame budget's biggest line items, the content pipeline the artists work in, and which platforms are easy or painful. They are also the decisions an AI is least able to make for you, because they depend on the team, the schedule and the art direction. A guide makes them explicit enough to brief, review and revisit.

3 How it works

Each guide is a short tree. Questions run down the left; a "yes" leads right to a starting choice, with what it trades away and the chapters that explain it; the final "no" drops to the default answer. Read the questions with your own targets in mind, and treat each leaf as the first option to prototype and measure.

1 Choosing a render path

The rendering path decides how lighting is organised (7.3). The first question is the hardware: tile-based GPUs in phones and standalone headsets pay heavily for the bandwidth a G-buffer needs. After that, it is a matter of how many lights touch each pixel and what else the art needs.

Epic's documentation for Unreal's forward renderer gives the headset case in numbers: forward rendering provides a faster baseline that may improve VR performance, and MSAA is often preferred there because the constant sub-pixel movement of head tracking makes temporal anti-aliasing blurry. It also lists what forward gives up in Unreal: screen-space reflections, SSAO and contact shadows among them.

2 Choosing shadows

Shadows for the sun are the biggest shadow decision (7.4, 7.5). The first question is whether anything moves. If the sun and the world are fixed, most shadows can be baked and only moving objects need real-time shadow maps. If not, the choice is mostly between cascaded shadow maps, which every engine supports, and Unreal's virtual shadow maps.

3 Choosing global illumination

Global illumination is the most expensive and the most visible of these choices (Part 8). The first question is whether the lighting changes while the game runs. If it does not, baked lighting gives the best quality per millisecond (8.2). If it does, the platform decides how much of the frame real-time GI can have.

Real-time GI also changes the team's work. Lighting artists stop waiting for bakes and start tuning a system that reacts to every change, including the ones nobody intended. A small team with a static world often gets a better-looking game from baked lighting and good probes than from a real-time method it cannot afford to tune.

4 Choosing anti-aliasing and upscaling

Anti-aliasing and upscaling are now one decision for most games (9.2, 9.3). Style comes first, because some looks are damaged by temporal techniques. Then the renderer, because MSAA only fits some paths. Then GPU time, because rendering fewer pixels and upscaling is the biggest single saving most games have.

5 Choosing an engine and its defaults

The engine decision bundles many of the others, because each engine's engine defaults already answer them: a new Unreal project turns on Lumen, virtual shadow maps and TSR; a new URP project starts on the Forward path; a new Godot project uses Forward+ on desktop. Those defaults suit each engine's typical target, which may not be yours (2.4, 10.3).

Whatever the engine, the defaults are a decision too. Shipping a handheld or a phone on defaults tuned for a PS5 is a common way to spend months of optimisation later. Write down which defaults the project keeps, which it changes per platform, and why.

7 How to use this book with your AI

The decisions in this chapter are where a person's judgement matters most. An AI can lay out the options, gather the documentation for your engine version and argue the trade-offs; it cannot know your team, your schedule or what your art director will accept unless you tell it. The rest of Part 12 turns that judgement into work.

Keep two chapters open

When you hand graphics work to an AI, keep 12.2 Briefing AI on graphics work open while you write the brief and 12.3 Reviewing AI-written graphics code open while you read what comes back. Paste the brief template and the review checklist into your team's tools, add the decision you made from this chapter to the brief's context, and ask for the evidence that would show the decision was right. Then measure on the hardware, because every guide in this chapter is a starting point, and the numbers have the last word.

4 What it costs

The decisions cost milliseconds every frame and people-time every week. They also cost something harder to see: the price of changing your mind, which grows with everything built on top of the choice.

Worked example, illustrative. A 12-person team targets PC and Switch 2 at 60 fps, so the frame budget is 16.7 ms on both (11.1). Epic's guidance budgets about 4 ms of a 60 fps frame for Lumen on current consoles (8.5): 24% of the frame on hardware much faster than a handheld. The team's world has a fixed sun and few dynamic lights. The GI guide's first question sends it to baked lightmaps and probes: close to zero GI time at run time, an overnight bake on the build machine, and a lightmap memory budget, say 24 MB on Switch 2. If the design later adds a day-night cycle, the guide's second question points at a probe-based method or a light real-time mode, and the cost of that change is the reason to settle the design question in pre-production.

  • Platform spreadEach extra tier adds a column of choices to maintain and test.
  • Late changesEvery material, level and bake made under the old choice must be checked again.
  • Defaults kept by accidentHigh-end defaults on low-end targets turn into months of optimisation.
  • Systems nobody ownsA real-time GI or custom renderer needs people to tune it for the life of the project.
  • Unwritten reasonsA choice without its reasons cannot be revisited when a constraint changes.

The usual trade

Teams keep their engine's defaults wherever they can, change the few that their lowest tier or their art style demands, and write each change down with its reason and the measurement behind it. They prototype the riskiest choice on the weakest target in pre-production, when changing it is still cheap.

5 How it breaks

Decisions fail in a handful of ways, and most of them start with a constraint nobody wrote down.

High-end defaults on a handheld

Cause
The project kept defaults chosen for PC and current consoles, then added a weaker target late. The frame is several times over budget.
Ask for
The weakest target named in pre-production, and a prototype level measured there before content production starts.

Baked lighting with a moving sun

Cause
The GI was chosen for a static world, and the design added a day-night cycle or destruction afterwards.
Ask for
The GI guide's first question answered by design, in writing, before the lighting pipeline is built.

A blurry headset

Cause
A desktop configuration in VR: deferred rendering with temporal anti-aliasing, which smears with every head movement.
Ask for
The headset as its own tier: forward rendering with MSAA, and a frame budget at the headset's refresh rate.

Chosen for one demo

Cause
A technique picked because it looked spectacular in a trailer or one hero scene, then applied to a game that is mostly something else.
Ask for
Prototypes of the typical scenes as well as the hero one, measured on every tier.

A pipeline switch in production

Cause
The render pipeline or path changed after content was made for the old one, so shaders, materials and lighting are redone.
Ask for
The pipeline decision closed in pre-production, with a written list of what would make the team reopen it.

Copied from another studio's game

Cause
Settings and techniques copied from a reference game made by a far larger team, on other platforms, with another art style.
Ask for
The reference's constraints written next to yours, and the decision guides run on yours.

7 Say it to your AI

Two briefs: one asks for the options for a decision laid out against your constraints, the other asks the AI to argue against a decision you have already made.

Lay out the optionsUse before a big rendering decision
We need to choose [render path / shadows / global illumination / anti-aliasing and upscaling] for our game.

Constraints:
- Engine: [engine and exact version], [render pipeline].
- Platform tiers: [platforms], each with frame rate and budget, e.g. [Switch 2 handheld, 30 fps, 33.3 ms].
- Team: [size, and who would own this system].
- Art style: [one or two sentences, and a reference image].
- What changes at run time: [time of day, destruction, lights switched, building, none].

1. List two or three options that fit these constraints, with the engine settings each needs in our version, linked to that version's documentation.
2. For each: expected GPU cost on our weakest target, memory, people-time to author and tune, and what it does badly.
3. Recommend one to prototype first, and the measurement that would prove it wrong.
Do not change the project.
  • The constraints are the questions of the decision guides, written down.
  • Version-linked settings catch answers written for another engine version.
  • "What would prove it wrong" turns a recommendation into a test.
Argue against our choiceUse when a decision is about to close
We have decided to use [choice] for [decision], because [reasons]. Our constraints: [engine and version], [platform tiers and budgets], [team], [art style], [what changes at run time].

Argue against this decision as a sceptical graphics lead would:
1. Which constraint does it fit worst, and what would that cost us in milliseconds, memory or people-time?
2. Which planned or likely design change would break it?
3. What is the cheapest experiment that would show whether the concern is real?
Be specific to our engine version and platforms. If the decision holds up, say so and say why.
  • A deliberate counter-argument surfaces risks that agreement hides.
  • Tying each concern to a cost keeps the argument concrete.
  • An experiment settles the question with numbers rather than opinions.

Questions to ask when reviewing a decision

  1. Which platform tiers, team and art style was this decided for?A decision without its constraints cannot be checked or revisited.
  2. Has it been measured on the weakest target, in a typical scene?Hero scenes on the fastest machine flatter every technique.
  3. Which engine defaults does it keep, and which does it change, per platform?Defaults are decisions too, and the most often unexamined.
  4. What design change would force us to revisit it?Naming it early lets design and rendering agree before content is built.
  5. Who owns this system for the rest of the project?Real-time systems need tuning for as long as content keeps changing.

8 Check yourself

  1. A small team is making a stylised game for phones and standalone VR headsets, with a fixed sun. Which render path, GI and anti-aliasing would the guides suggest as starting points?

    Show answer

    Forward rendering, because both targets have tile-based GPUs and headsets need crisp, stable images; baked lightmaps and probes, because the light does not change and the platforms cannot afford much real-time GI; and MSAA, perhaps 2×, with FXAA or SMAA where needed, because forward rendering allows it and temporal techniques smear in a headset.

  2. An Unreal project made for PS5 at 60 fps adds a Switch 2 version late in production. Which engine defaults should the team examine first, and why?

    Show answer

    The ones that cost the most milliseconds: Lumen, virtual shadow maps and Nanite-dependent content, with TSR settings. Epic's guidance budgets about 4 ms of a 60 fps console frame for Lumen alone; on a handheld the same choice may not fit at all. The team should measure a typical level on Switch 2, then pick the GI and shadow leaves for that tier, such as baked or probe GI and cascades, and set them through per-platform settings.

  3. Why do the guides ask about team size at all, when the techniques are the same whoever uses them?

    Show answer

    Because every choice has to be built, tuned and maintained by people for the life of the project. Real-time GI needs ongoing tuning as content changes; a custom engine needs a permanent team; many platforms need many configurations tested. A technique the team cannot afford to own will look worse in the shipped game than a simpler one it can.
