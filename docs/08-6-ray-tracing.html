8.6 Ray tracing

Ray tracing asks the scene itself, not the screen, what lies in a given direction. GPUs now answer that question in hardware by walking a tree of boxes wrapped around the triangles. This chapter shows how the tree works, what it costs to keep it up to date as things move, and why games still draw the picture with rasterization and spend about one ray per pixel on each effect.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A ray is a question: starting here and looking in this direction, what is the first thing I meet? Ray tracing answers it by testing the ray against the scene's actual triangles. Ask it from a surface towards a light and you get a shadow. Ask it along the mirror direction and you get a reflection. Ask it in random directions and you gather bounced light.

Rasterization (5.3) works the other way round: it takes each triangle and finds the pixels it covers. That is extremely fast for the one question every frame must answer, what the camera sees, but it cannot ask about anything else. Everything since Part 7, shadow maps, reflection probes, screen-space tricks, has been a workaround for that. Ray tracing removes the need for most of those workarounds, at a price.

Everyday comparison

To find one book in a big library you do not check every shelf. You go to the right floor, then the right room, then the right bookcase, and ignore everything else. A ray does the same with nested boxes: if it misses the box around the whole east wing of a level, it never looks at a single triangle inside it.

The problem it solves

Screen-space effects (8.4) cannot see behind objects or past the screen edge. Shadow maps blur and alias. Probes are baked in the wrong place. Rays against the real geometry fix all three: correct contact shadows, reflections of things off screen, and bounced light from objects the camera cannot see.

Why a lead cares

Hardware ray tracing sets a hardware floor. Epic's requirements for it in Unreal are an NVIDIA RTX 2000 or AMD Radeon RX 6000 series GPU or newer on Windows, or a PlayStation 5, Xbox Series or Switch 2. Each traced effect costs milliseconds and needs a denoiser. And content decides much of the cost: how many objects move, how many are skinned, and how much alpha-tested foliage there is. Those are art and design decisions, not only engineering ones.

3 How it works

A ray-traced effect is three problems stacked: test one ray against one triangle, avoid testing almost all of the triangles, and keep the structure that makes the second possible up to date while the game runs. Then the hardware, the APIs and the frame decide how many rays you can afford.

1 What a ray is, and the two answers it can want

In the DirectX Raytracing specification a ray is an origin, a direction and a range of distances to search, from a minimum to a maximum. Every point on it is the origin plus some distance times the direction. The minimum is set a hair above zero so a ray leaving a surface does not hit that surface; the maximum stops a shadow ray at the light.

Effects want one of two answers. Reflections and bounced light need the closest hit, because that is what you would see. A shadow ray only needs to know whether anything is in the way, so it can stop at the first hit it finds, whichever that is. That shortcut makes shadow rays the cheapest kind.

2 Testing one ray against one triangle

The basic operation is the ray-triangle intersection test. First, find where the ray crosses the flat plane the triangle lies in. Second, decide whether that point is inside the triangle. The second step uses the same barycentric coordinates as rasterization (5.3): three weights, one per corner, that describe the point. If all three are zero or more, the point is inside. The widely used Möller–Trumbore method (1997) does both steps in a few dozen multiplications and additions, and the weights it produces are exactly what a hit shader needs to interpolate UVs and normals.

3 Skip most of the scene: the bounding volume hierarchy

An acceleration structure groups triangles so a ray can ignore most of them. The APIs leave its exact format to the GPU maker, and both NVIDIA's and AMD's hardware walk the same kind: a bounding volume hierarchy, or BVH. It is a tree of boxes, the same axis-aligned bounding volumes that culling uses (4.4). The root box holds everything. Each box holds two or more smaller boxes, and the smallest, the leaves, hold a few triangles each.

A ray first tests the root box. Where it enters a box, it tests that box's children; where it misses one, everything inside is skipped without a single triangle test. Only in leaves does it test triangles. For a closest hit there is one more saving: visit the nearer child first, and once a hit is found, skip any box that starts farther away than that hit.

  • Press Restart, then Step: the ray tests the root, then both of its children, and goes down only one side of the tree.
  • Drag the start so the ray crosses empty space: two or three box tests, and no triangle tests at all, settle that it hits nothing.
  • Switch to Any hit (shadow ray). The aim point becomes a light, and the search stops at the first triangle in the way, or at the light.

The demo splits each box's triangles at the middle. Production builders choose splits with the surface area heuristic, which estimates how likely a ray is to enter each child from the child's surface area and picks the split with the lowest expected cost. Either way the payoff is the depth of the tree: each level roughly halves what is left, so a million triangles sit about 20 levels below the root, because 220 is about a million. A ray makes tens of box tests and a handful of triangle tests instead of a million triangle tests.

Maths Testing a ray against a box

An axis-aligned box is the overlap of three slabs, one per axis, each between two parallel planes. For each axis, the ray enters its slab at one distance and leaves at another:

tnear = max over x, y, z of the slab entry distances,   tfar = min of the exit distances

The ray hits the box if tnear ≤ tfar and the range overlaps the ray's own search range. That is six subtractions and multiplications and a few comparisons, cheaper than a triangle test, which is why the tree spends box tests to save triangle tests.

4 Two levels: one tree per mesh, one tree over the scene

One BVH for a whole level would have to be rebuilt every time anything moved. So the APIs split it in two. A bottom-level acceleration structure, or BLAS, is a BVH over one mesh's triangles, built in the mesh's own coordinate space. A top-level acceleration structure, or TLAS, is a BVH over instances: each entry points at a BLAS and stores that instance's transform, an ID and a mask that lets rays include or ignore it. Microsoft announced DirectX Raytracing in 2018 with exactly this two-level design.

5 When things move: update the instance, refit, or rebuild

Keeping the structures current is the part of ray tracing that rasterization never had to pay for. What has to change depends on how an object moves:

  • It moves as a whole, like a pushed crate or a car. Its BLAS is untouched; only its transform in the TLAS changes. Engines rebuild the TLAS every frame, and Epic notes that cost grows mostly with the number of instances.
  • Its vertices move, like a skinned character or a flag. The BLAS must follow. The cheap option is a refit: keep the same tree and grow each box bottom-up to fit the new vertex positions. DXR calls this an update, and allows it only if the BLAS was built to allow updates and only vertex positions changed.
  • It changes a lot. Refitting keeps the old grouping, so triangles that have moved apart leave boxes stretched and overlapping, and rays enter more of them. The DXR specification warns that updates are faster than a full build but can lower ray tracing performance, especially if the geometry has moved far from where it was built. The fix is a rebuild: a new tree from scratch.

The same trade appears when building. DXR offers a build flag that prefers fast tracing, which the specification recommends for static geometry and expects to take roughly two to three times as long to build, and one that prefers fast building, roughly a half to a third of the default build time, for geometry rebuilt every frame. Unity's per-renderer Ray Tracing Mode exposes the same choices to artists: Static, Dynamic Transform (rigid motion), and Dynamic Geometry (vertices move). Getting that setting right per object is a content task that directly changes the frame time.

6 The APIs: ray tracing shaders, or a ray query in any shader

DXR, DirectX Raytracing, was announced by Microsoft in March 2018 as part of DirectX 12. Vulkan ray tracing followed as final Khronos extensions in November 2020: one for acceleration structures, one for ray tracing pipelines and one for ray queries. Both offer the same two ways to trace.

The first is a ray tracing pipeline. A ray generation shader launches rays; the hardware walks the acceleration structure; and the API calls back small shaders you supply: an any-hit shader for candidate hits, which can reject them, for example where a leaf texture is transparent; a closest-hit shader to shade the nearest hit; and a miss shader for rays that hit nothing, which usually returns the sky. A table, the shader binding table, says which shaders belong to which object.

The second is inline ray tracing, added in DXR tier 1.1 and available in Vulkan as ray queries. Any shader, including a compute shader or a pixel shader, can trace a ray and handle the result in its own code, with no separate shaders. The DXR specification gives the simplest case: an opaque, triangles-only shadow ray that ends at the first hit, which the system can run very efficiently. It also warns that inline ray tracing with many materials depends heavily on how alike neighbouring rays are, which is the subject of step 8.

Simplified

Both APIs also support custom geometry tested by an intersection shader (spheres, hair curves), callable shaders, recursion limits and payload sizes. DXR tier 1.2 adds opacity micromaps, which mark parts of a triangle as opaque or transparent so fewer any-hit shaders run, and shader execution reordering, which lets the GPU regroup rays by what they hit. Engines handle these; what a lead needs is the pipeline-or-inline distinction and its effect on cost.

7 The hardware: RT cores and ray accelerators

Walking a BVH in ordinary shader code is slow: each step loads a node, tests boxes, decides where to go next, and waits on memory. NVIDIA's Turing GPUs (GeForce RTX 20 series, 2018) added RT cores, fixed-function units beside each group of shader cores that do BVH traversal and ray-triangle tests. AMD's RDNA 2 GPUs, the generation in PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series, add a ray accelerator to each compute unit, which tests a ray against four boxes or one triangle per clock; there, the traversal loop itself still runs as shader code.

8 Coherence and divergence: why some rays cost more

GPUs run shaders in warps of 32 or 64 lanes that execute the same instruction together (3.5). Coherence is how alike neighbouring rays are. Shadow rays from neighbouring pixels to the same light start close together and point the same way, so they visit the same boxes, which stay in the cache, and finish at about the same time. Reflection rays from a rough floor scatter: they visit different parts of the tree, miss the cache, and hit different materials. That is divergence, and it costs twice: in memory traffic during traversal, and in shading, where a warp runs each material's shader in turn while lanes that need a different one wait.

9 Hybrid rendering: rasterize what the camera sees, trace the rest

Games rarely trace camera rays for the normal view. Rasterization answers "what does each pixel see?" faster than any ray tracer, and the G-buffer it writes holds each pixel's position, normal and material. Rays start from there. This is hybrid rendering, and it is how Microsoft introduced DXR in 2018: rasterize most of the scene, and use rays for effects such as shadows and reflections.

The common uses, each a separate pass with its own ray budget, the number of rays per pixel it may trace each frame:

  • Ray-traced shadows: one any-hit ray per pixel towards a random point on each light. Contact shadows are exact, and the spread of points over an area light gives soft edges that widen with distance, as real penumbras do.
  • Ray-traced reflections: one closest-hit ray along the reflection direction, spread by roughness. They show what SSR cannot: off-screen and hidden objects. The hit then needs lighting, either full shading or a lookup in a cache such as Lumen's surface cache.
  • Ray-traced GI: rays in many directions from surfaces or probes, gathering bounced light. 8.5's DDGI and Lumen's hardware mode both trace this way, from far fewer points than there are pixels.
  • Ray-traced ambient occlusion (RTAO, 8.3): short any-hit rays in the hemisphere above each pixel.

Every one of those passes is noisy at these budgets. One shadow ray per pixel to a random point on an area light gives a speckled penumbra; one reflection ray on a rough surface gives a speckled reflection. Hybrid ray tracing is only usable because of denoising, which blends neighbouring pixels and previous frames. Taken to its limit, with every bounce traced, the same idea becomes path tracing, the subject of 8.7.

4 What it costs

Ray tracing costs in three places: the rays themselves (traversal plus shading at the hits), keeping the acceleration structures current, and the denoising each effect needs. The first is set by the ray budget. At one ray per pixel, 1080p is 2,073,600 rays per effect per frame; at 60 fps that is about 124 million a second, and 4K is four times that.

Two worked examples make the scaling concrete. A ray-traced shadow pass for three shadow-casting lights at one ray per pixel at 1440p traces 3 × 3,686,400 = 11,059,200 rays a frame before denoising. Unity's HDRP documents that execution time grows linearly with a reflection's sample count but exponentially with its bounce count, because each bounce spawns more rays from every hit.

  • Rays per pixel and resolutionLinear: twice the rays is roughly twice the traversal and shading. Most engines trace at a lower internal resolution and upscale.
  • BouncesEach extra bounce traces again from every hit, and those rays are less coherent than the first.
  • Shading at the hitsFull material shading at reflection hits (Unreal's Hit Lighting) costs far more than reading a cache such as Lumen's surface cache.
  • Moving and deforming geometryThe TLAS is rebuilt every frame and grows with instance count; every skinned mesh needs its BLAS refit, in proportion to its triangle count.
  • Alpha-tested foliageEvery leaf candidate runs an any-hit shader to read the alpha texture, the ray-traced version of alpha-test cost (5.4).
  • Overlapping geometryWhere meshes overlap, a ray must traverse each of them to find the closest hit. Epic lists overlapping meshes, skyboxes and grass as common traversal costs.
  • MemoryBLAS memory grows with triangle count. AMD's advice is that smaller BVHs are always better, and DXR's compaction option reclaims memory for static geometry.

The usual trade

Ray tracing is usually a quality tier rather than a baseline: shadows or reflections traced on capable PCs and current consoles, with shadow maps, probes and screen-space effects as the fallback. That doubles part of the lighting work, because both paths must look acceptable. A game that requires hardware ray tracing saves that duplication but drops every player below the hardware floor.

5 How it breaks

Ray tracing fixes the blind spots of screen-space effects and brings new failures: too few rays, geometry that differs between what is drawn and what is traced, and update costs that spike when content moves.

Grainy or boiling shadows and reflections

Cause
Too few rays for the area light or roughness, or a denoiser that is off, too weak, or losing its history when the camera moves.
Ask for
The rays per pixel per effect, the denoiser settings, and a capture in motion; then more rays only where the noise is worst, such as large area lights.

Objects missing from reflections or casting no shadow

Cause
The object is not in the acceleration structure: its ray tracing setting is off, it was culled from the ray tracing scene, or, in HDRP, a skinned mesh off camera stopped updating.
Ask for
The object's ray tracing mode, the ray tracing culling settings, and the engine's view of the ray tracing scene.

Shadows that do not match the drawn mesh

Cause
Rays hit a simpler version of the mesh than the one drawn, such as Unreal's Nanite fallback mesh, or vertex animation that is not applied in the ray tracing scene. Epic notes that fallback meshes can cause shadow artifacts.
Ask for
The fallback or proxy detail for the worst meshes, World Position Offset evaluation where animation matters, and a bias check on self-shadowing.

Frame-time spikes when lots of things move

Cause
Many dynamic instances or deforming meshes at once: TLAS size, BLAS refits or rebuilds, or new objects whose BLAS is built on first use.
Ask for
A GPU capture of the spike frame showing acceleration structure build time, the count of dynamic instances, and limits or simpler proxies for debris and crowds.

Foliage and fences make ray tracing slow

Cause
Alpha-tested cards are traced as non-opaque, so each candidate hit runs an any-hit shader to read the texture, and dense, overlapping cards multiply those calls.
Ask for
Foliage marked opaque or excluded from ray-traced effects where acceptable, simpler ray tracing proxies, and opacity micromaps where the engine supports them.

To diagnose, use the engine's ray tracing views: Unreal's ray tracing debug modes show traversal cost per pixel and which instances are dynamic, and its stat GPU lists the ray tracing scene update. A frame capture tool (11.5) shows acceleration structure builds as their own GPU work.

7 Say it to your AI

Ray tracing briefs need the engine and version, the target hardware, which effects are traced and at what budget, what the fallback is, and what in the content moves. Ask for update costs as well as ray costs.

Questions to ask when reviewing ray tracing work

  1. How many rays per pixel does each effect trace, at what resolution?That number, times pixels, is most of the cost.
  2. What is in the acceleration structure, and how does each kind of object update?Static, rigid and deforming objects cost differently; missing objects vanish from reflections and shadows.
  3. Is the traced geometry the same as the drawn geometry?Fallback meshes, proxies and skipped vertex animation cause mismatched shadows and self-shadowing.
  4. How is each effect denoised, and what happens when the camera moves fast?At these budgets the denoiser decides the final look.
  5. What does a player without ray tracing hardware see?The fallback path has to be designed, tested and budgeted too.
  6. What happens to the frame when 200 objects move at once?Update costs spike in exactly the moments a game most wants to look good.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why can a shadow ray be cheaper than a reflection ray against the same scene?

    Show answer

    A shadow ray only needs to know whether anything blocks the light, so it can stop at the first hit and needs no material shading. A reflection ray must find the closest hit, which means continuing to search until no nearer hit is possible, and then the hit has to be lit. Shadow rays from neighbouring pixels also tend to be coherent.

  2. A level has 400 identical crates, one skinned dragon and a static castle. What happens to the acceleration structures each frame as the dragon flies and players push crates around?

    Show answer

    The 400 crates share one BLAS that never changes; pushing them only changes their transforms in the TLAS, which is rebuilt every frame. The castle's BLAS was built once. The dragon's BLAS must be refitted every frame as its vertices move, and rebuilt now and then if its pose changes enough that the refitted boxes get loose.

  3. Your game renders the G-buffer with rasterization and traces one shadow ray per pixel. A colleague asks why you don't trace the camera rays too. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Rasterization already answers what each pixel sees, faster than tracing, and the G-buffer gives every pixel's position and normal, which is exactly where the shadow rays start. Tracing primary rays would spend the ray budget on the one question that does not need rays. That is hybrid rendering.
