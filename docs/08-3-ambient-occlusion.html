8.3 Ambient occlusion: SSAO to GTAO

Corners, creases and the ground under objects receive less of the light that bounces around a scene. Ambient occlusion darkens them. This chapter shows how renderers estimate it from the depth buffer every frame, and why each generation of the technique, from SSAO to GTAO, looks better and fails in its own way.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Stand in an open field on an overcast day and light reaches you from the whole sky. Step into a narrow alley and the walls hide most of that sky, so it is darker, though nothing about the clouds changed. Crawl under a table and it is darker still.

Ambient occlusion, or AO, is that effect for every point on every surface: how much of the surrounding sky or environment the point can see. A value of 1 means wide open; 0 means completely enclosed. The renderer multiplies the soft, surrounding light by that value.

The problem it solves

Renderers usually approximate the light bouncing around a scene, the indirect light, as a soft, even fill. An even fill lights the inside of a crease as brightly as an open floor. Objects look pasted on and seem to hover.

AO puts back the darkening where surfaces meet. It is the cheapest single effect that makes objects feel grounded.

What it is not

AO is not a shadow from a light. A shadow depends on where the lamp or the sun is. AO depends only on the shape of the nearby geometry, so it looks the same whichever way the light comes from.

That is also its limit: it is a rough correction to the ambient light, not a simulation of bounced light.

Why a lead cares

AO is in almost every game's graphics menu. It costs from around half a millisecond to a couple of milliseconds a frame. Its faults, such as dark halos, shimmering and ghost trails, are among the most reported visual bugs. Knowing which AO a game uses tells you which bugs to expect.

3 How it works

Every real-time AO method approximates the same ideal measurement. The story of SSAO, HBAO and GTAO is the story of getting closer to that ideal for less work.

1 The ideal: count the open sky

Fire rays from a point in every direction above its surface, out to some radius. Count the share that escape without hitting anything. That share is the point's visibility; ambient occlusion is what is missing from it.

Not every ray counts equally. Light arriving along the normal, straight onto the surface, lights it fully. Light arriving at a grazing angle spreads thin across it. So rays near the normal carry more weight than rays near the horizon. This is called cosine weighting, and it is why the percentages in the alley picture are lower than the raw share of visible sky.

Maths The visibility integral, in one line

For a point with normal n, visibility is the cosine-weighted average of a yes-or-no visibility test V over the hemisphere of directions ω above the surface:

visibility = (1 / π) ∫ V(ω) · (n · ω) dω,   AO = 1 − visibility

The n · ω term is the cosine weighting. Dividing by π makes a fully open hemisphere come out at exactly 1. Every method in this chapter is a way of estimating that integral.

2 Bake it, or compute it every frame

AO that never changes can be computed once, offline, with as many rays as you like. Artists bake an AO texture for each asset, capturing the creases within the model. Level lighting bakes capture AO between static objects as part of baked lighting.

Baking cannot handle anything that moves: a character's feet on the floor, a crate pushed against a wall, a door opening. For those, the renderer estimates AO every frame from what it has already drawn. Most games use both: baked AO for detail inside each asset, and per-frame AO for contact between objects.

3 What the renderer knows: the depth buffer

By the time AO runs, the renderer has drawn the scene's geometry into the depth buffer and, in a deferred renderer, the G-buffer. For every pixel it knows how far away the visible surface is and which way that surface faces. That is all. The triangles themselves are gone.

So per-frame AO is a screen-space technique: it reconstructs the shape of the scene from the depth buffer. That shape has two blind spots, and every artifact in section 5 comes from one of them.

4 SSAO: test random points

SSAO, screen-space ambient occlusion, was introduced by Crytek for Crysis in 2007. For each pixel it scatters a handful of sample points in the half-sphere above the surface, typically 8 to 16, and checks each against the depth buffer. A point that lies behind the stored surface is inside geometry, so it counts as blocked. The share of unblocked points is the visibility.

A dozen random samples cannot estimate a smooth quantity cleanly. Each pixel gets a differently rotated pattern, so the raw output is noisy, and a blur pass follows. It has to be a bilateral filter, one that only mixes pixels at a similar depth, or the darkness of a crease would smear onto the object in front of it. SSAO also treats every sample equally, ignoring cosine weighting, so its darkness is not physically scaled. Its results look plausible rather than correct.

5 HBAO: look for the horizon

HBAO, horizon-based ambient occlusion, was presented by NVIDIA in 2008. Instead of scattering points, it walks outward from the pixel across the screen in a few directions. In each direction it records the steepest angle at which the surrounding surface rises above the pixel: the horizon angle. Below the horizon the sky is blocked; above it, it is open.

6 GTAO: integrate the visible arc exactly

GTAO, ground-truth ambient occlusion, was published by Activision in 2016 and used in Call of Duty. It keeps the horizon search but treats each direction as a slice: a thin vertical cut through the half-sphere above the pixel. In that slice the two horizons bound one arc of open sky. GTAO computes the cosine-weighted visibility of that arc with an exact formula, so for the shape the depth buffer describes, its answer matches what ray tracing would give. That is the "ground truth" in the name.

GTAO is fast because it spreads the work out. The original ran at half resolution and evaluated just one slice direction per pixel per frame. Neighbouring pixels used different directions, and each frame rotated them again. A 4 × 4 bilateral blur combined neighbours, and temporal accumulation blended six frames of rotations. The paper reports 96 effective directions per pixel at a cost of 0.5 ms on a PlayStation 4 at 1080p.

The same paper adds two refinements you will see in settings menus:

  • Multi-bounce AO brightens AO on light-coloured surfaces. In a white corner, light bounces several times before it is absorbed, so the corner is not as dark as single-bounce AO predicts. A darker surface absorbs more at each bounce, so its AO stays close to the raw value.
  • Specular occlusion darkens reflections inside creases. It uses the bent normal, the average direction of open sky, to judge whether a reflection would point into geometry.

Simplified

Real GTAO also tilts each slice's integral to account for the surface normal not lying flat in the slice, and fades samples near the edge of the radius instead of cutting them off. The idea in the figure, two horizons and an exact integral between them, is the whole core of the method.

8 Where AO sits in the frame

AO is its own render pass, usually a compute shader that runs after depth and normals exist and before lighting. It writes a single-channel texture: one visibility value per pixel. Most renderers compute it at half resolution, a quarter of the pixels, then denoise and upsample it.

The lighting pass then multiplies it into the sky light and indirect light only. Direct light from the sun or a lamp has its own shadows, so darkening it with AO as well makes sunlit corners look dirty. Many engines nevertheless expose a slider for exactly that, because artists sometimes want the extra contrast.

9 Beyond the screen

Screen-space AO can only fix its blind spots with heuristics. A thickness heuristic assumes surfaces in the depth buffer are only so thick, so that a wall behind a character is not treated as touching it. Fading AO near the screen edges hides the moment occluders leave the screen. These help, but they are guesses.

The real fix is to stop relying on the screen. RTAO, ray-traced ambient occlusion, traces short rays against the actual scene with ray tracing hardware, so it sees under tables and past the screen edge. Full real-time global illumination systems such as Unreal's Lumen compute occlusion as part of bounced light, and add their own short-range AO for fine contact detail. Chapters 8.4 and 8.5 cover those, and SSGI, which applies the same screen-space idea to bounced colour.

4 What it costs

AO cost is a multiplication: pixels, times directions per pixel, times steps per direction, times two sides. A 1080p frame has 2,073,600 pixels. With one slice per pixel and 8 steps on each side, GTAO makes 16 depth reads per pixel: about 33 million at full resolution, or about 8.3 million at half resolution.

  • ResolutionCost scales with pixel count. 4K is four times 1080p; half resolution is a quarter.
  • Directions and stepsEach extra slice or step adds reads for every pixel. Quality presets mostly change these two numbers.
  • RadiusA larger radius spreads samples further apart in memory, so they miss the cache more often.
  • DenoisingBlur and temporal passes add their own cost, and the temporal pass needs motion vectors from the rest of the frame.

The usual trade

Computing AO at half resolution saves about three quarters of the work, at the price of softness and slight leaking at the edges of thin objects. Most games make that trade on consoles and keep full resolution as a "High" or "Ultra" option on PC.

5 How it breaks

Each of these traces back to the two blind spots of the depth buffer, or to the shortcuts taken to make AO cheap.

Dark halos around objects

Cause
The depth buffer cannot tell a character a metre in front of a wall from one touching it, so the wall around the silhouette is treated as enclosed.
Ask for
A thickness heuristic or distance falloff, a smaller radius, or a method with better depth-discontinuity handling.

AO fading or popping at screen edges

Cause
Near the edge, samples land off screen where there is no depth data. Occlusion from objects that just left the view disappears.
Ask for
An edge fade tuned to hide the transition, or a world-space method such as RTAO where it matters.

Noise, shimmer and crawling patterns

Cause
Too few samples, or a denoiser too weak for them. The pattern moves when the camera moves, so it shimmers.
Ask for
More samples or directions, a stronger bilateral blur, or temporal accumulation if the game already has TAA.

Ghost trails behind moving objects

Cause
Temporal accumulation keeps blending last frame's AO, which was computed with the object somewhere else, and does not reject it fast enough at disocclusion.
Ask for
Tighter history rejection, a lower temporal blend weight, and checking that moving objects write correct motion vectors.

A dirty, over-dark look

Cause
AO applied to direct light as well as ambient, intensity or radius set too high, or AO counted twice: baked AO textures plus screen-space AO plus GI occlusion on the same creases.
Ask for
AO on ambient light only, one source of AO per scale of detail, and settings compared side by side in a test scene.

7 Say it to your AI

AO briefs go well when they name the engine, the AO method in use, the exact symptom and where it appears, and ask for ranked options with their side effects.

Questions to ask when reviewing AO work

  1. Is AO multiplied into direct light, or only into ambient and indirect light?On direct light it makes sunlit corners look dirty.
  2. What resolution is AO computed at, and how is it upsampled?Half resolution is normal; an upsample that ignores depth edges leaks AO onto the object in front.
  3. Does the temporal filter reject history when things move or reappear?If not, moving objects leave ghost trails.
  4. What happens near the screen edges and around thin objects?These are the two blind spots. Ask what heuristic covers each.
  5. How many sources of AO touch the same pixel?Baked AO maps, screen-space AO and GI occlusion stacked together over-darken.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why does screen-space AO paint a dark outline on a wall behind a character, even when the character stands well in front of it?

    Show answer

    The depth buffer stores only the nearest surface per pixel. It cannot tell how thick the character is or how far behind it the wall is, so the space between them is treated as solid and the wall near the silhouette counts as enclosed. Thickness heuristics and distance falloff reduce it; RTAO avoids it.

  2. GTAO reads far fewer points per frame than SSAO typically does, yet looks cleaner. How?

    Show answer

    It looks for the horizon in each direction, which captures the shape with a single maximum instead of many hit-or-miss tests, then integrates the open arc exactly with cosine weighting. It also spreads directions across neighbouring pixels and across frames, and denoises spatially and temporally.

  3. After AO is turned on, sunlit areas look grubby in the corners. Which setting do you ask about first?

    Show answer

    Whether AO is being applied to direct light: Direct Lighting Strength in Unity, Light Affect in Godot. AO belongs on ambient and indirect light; direct light already has shadows.
