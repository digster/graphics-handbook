Part 8 · Global illumination and ambient occlusion

8.5 Real-time GI families

Every method that computes bounced light while the game runs does three things: it traces light against a simplified copy of the scene, stores the result in some shape, and reuses it over many frames. The shape it chooses, a grid, voxels, probes, a cache on surfaces or a stack of cascades, decides what it costs, how fast it reacts and where light leaks through walls.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Baked lighting (8.2) computes bounced light once, in the editor. Screen-space GI (8.4) computes it every frame, but only from what is on screen. The families in this chapter sit between them: they compute bounced light while the game runs, from the whole scene around the camera, so it follows moving lights, opening doors and the time of day.

None of them can afford to trace rays against every triangle for every pixel. Instead each one keeps a cheaper version of the scene to trace against, stores the light it finds in a structure that is much coarser than the screen, and refreshes only part of it each frame. Every pixel then looks up nearby stored light.

Everyday comparison

To know how warm every spot in a building is, you could hang thermometers on a grid through the rooms, or stick a note on every wall saying how warm it is there. The grid is easy to set up and cheap to read, but a thermometer next to a thin wall cannot tell which room it belongs to. The notes are tied to the right side of each wall, but you need one for every wall, kept up to date.

The problem it solves

Baked light cannot follow change, and screen-space light forgets what leaves the screen. Games with day-night cycles, destructible or player-built levels, or lights the player carries need bounced light that is computed live, for the whole space around the camera, within a few milliseconds a frame.

Why a lead cares

The choice of GI family is made early and is hard to undo. It sets a GPU budget of several milliseconds, platform limits (most of these methods need a desktop or current-console GPU), content rules such as minimum wall thickness and how big emissive surfaces may be, and the look of interiors. It also replaces or reduces the bake step of 8.2, which changes how artists work every day.

3 How it works

The families are covered roughly in the order they appeared, because each one answered a problem in the one before: blur, then leaks, then cost and coverage.

1 Light propagation volumes: spread light through a grid

Light propagation volumes, LPV, were presented by Anton Kaplanyan of Crytek in 2009 for CryEngine 3, then extended with Carsten Dachsbacher in 2010. The method has three steps. Render the scene from each light into a reflective shadow map, which records the lit surfaces as small points of light. Inject those points into a coarse 3D grid, each cell holding light as spherical harmonics with two bands, four numbers per colour. Then spread the light: in each iteration every cell passes light to its six neighbours, like a ripple.

Kaplanyan's notes also describe nested cascades of volumes around the camera, fine near it and coarse further away, an idea most later methods reuse. LPV gives only soft, low-detail bounce, and later families exist largely to fix its leaks.

2 Voxel cone tracing: voxels with blurred copies

Voxel cone tracing stores the scene itself, not just the light, as voxels: small cubes recording each region's colour, how opaque it is and how brightly it is lit. Like a texture's mipmaps, coarser levels hold blurred averages of the levels below. To gather light, a pixel sends a few cones instead of many rays. Each cone steps outward and, at each step, reads a voxel from the level whose size matches the cone's width there, so one read stands for a whole bundle of rays.

Godot's VoxelGI node uses voxels in a box you place around an area: 20 m by default, divided 128 times. Baking voxelizes the static meshes inside, which takes 5 to 20 seconds in most scenes and also works in exported games; after that, lights left in the default Dynamic bake mode can move and change, and their bounce updates in real time. It runs only on the Forward+ renderer, Godot renders at most 8 VoxelGI nodes at once and blends at most 2 at any pixel, and its documentation describes it as fairly demanding on the GPU.

Voxels have the same weakness as LPV cells. A voxel that contains a thin wall holds both its lit side and its dark side, averaged together. Epic's Lumen team reports trying voxel cone tracing and rejecting it because merging geometry into a volume caused a lot of leaking.

3 Light leaking: the family weakness

Light leaking is light appearing where it cannot reach, most often inside a room through a wall. It happens whenever a method stores light more coarsely than the geometry that should block it. The reverse also happens: darkness leaks into lit areas, for example when a probe ends up inside a wall and reports black.

Every family has a leak rule. Epic says walls should be no thinner than 10 cm for Lumen. Godot's documentation says walls and floors should be at least one voxel thick for VoxelGI, and offers Use Occlusion and a smaller Y Scale against leaks in SDFGI. Unity's Adaptive Probe Volumes (8.2) add leak reduction and probe adjustment volumes. For an art team this becomes a modelling rule: no single-plane walls between bright and dark spaces.

4 DDGI: probes that know how far they can see

DDGI, dynamic diffuse global illumination, was published in 2019 by Zander Majercik, Jean-Philippe Guertin, Derek Nowrouzezahrai and Morgan McGuire. It is a grid of light probes, as in 8.2, with two changes. The probes are updated every frame: each one traces rays into the scene, usually with ray-tracing hardware, and blends the new light into what it had. And alongside its light, stored at 8 × 8 directions, each probe stores how far its rays went before hitting something, at 16 × 16 directions.

The paper's timings, on a GeForce RTX 2080 Ti, are 2.6 ms for the indirect lighting of a scene with 32 × 8 × 32 probes and 64 rays per probe, updating every probe every frame. Light reacts within a few frames, set by a hysteresis value that controls how much old light each update keeps. A 2021 follow-up by Majercik, Marrs, Spjut and McGuire describes the changes needed for production: moving probes out of walls automatically, switching off probes that cannot affect the image, and cascaded volumes for large worlds. It reports integrations in NVIDIA's RTXGI SDK, in Unity and in Unreal Engine 4, and in several commercial games.

5 SDFGI: Godot's probes in distance-field cascades

SDFGI, signed distance field global illumination, is Godot's semi-real-time GI. It builds signed distance fields (7.5) of the static geometry around the camera, in cascades like a clipmap: each cascade twice the size of the one inside it, with cells twice as big. Probes in that volume trace the distance fields to gather light, with occlusion data between probes to reduce leaks.

Its limits are set out in Godot's documentation. It runs only on the Forward+ renderer. It supports dynamic lights but not dynamic occluders or emissive surfaces: moving objects receive its light but do not block or bounce it. It takes 30 frames by default to converge, and cascade shifts can be visible when the camera moves quickly. Its Bounce Feedback, 0.5 by default, adds further bounces, and higher values risk feedback loops. Godot recommends it as the first GI method to try for desktop games, because it needs the least setup.

6 Lumen: a cache on surfaces, gathered by screen probes

Lumen is Unreal Engine 5's real-time global illumination and reflection system, on by default in new projects. Its design, presented by Daniel Wright, Krzysztof Narkowicz and Patrick Kelly at SIGGRAPH 2022, combines ideas from this whole Part: screen traces from 8.4, distance fields from 7.5, probes, and caching over frames.

The surface cache is the core idea. At import, Lumen places cards around each mesh, 12 by default, like cameras photographing it from several sides. At run time those cards capture the mesh's material and are lit, direct light first, then light gathered from the cache itself, so each update adds another bounce. Surfaces the cards do not cover cannot bounce light and show black in reflections; Epic notes that only meshes with simple interiors are supported well.

Screen probes are Lumen's final gather. Rather than trace from every pixel, it places probes on the visible surfaces, about one per 16 × 16 pixels and more where geometry is detailed, and traces 8 × 8 directions from each. Light from further than about two metres comes from a world-space radiance cache, with fewer probes but more directions per probe. The talk is frank about the cost of stability: the temporal filter reacts slowly to lighting changes, which shows as ghosting behind moving objects.

Lumen traces the Lumen Scene in software or hardware. Software ray tracing works on any GPU with Shader Model 6, but represents only static meshes, instanced meshes and landscape, so skinned characters appear only through screen traces. Hardware ray tracing needs a ray-tracing GPU, an RTX 2000 or Radeon RX 6000 or newer on Windows, or a PlayStation 5, Xbox Series or Switch 2; new projects in Unreal 5.8 enable it and use it where supported. Epic warns that its scene updates get expensive above about 100,000 instances. Lumen does not support the Mobile renderer, forward shading or VR, and it cannot be combined with baked lightmaps.

7 Radiance cascades: spend directions where they matter

Radiance cascades come from Alexander Sannikov at Grinding Gear Games, who describes them in a self-published paper. The observation behind them: to capture light from something close by, you need probes close together but only a few directions; to capture light from far away, you need many directions, but the probes can be far apart. So the light field is split into ranges. Each cascade covers a range of distances twice as long as the one below it, with probes twice as far apart and finer directions.

Path of Exile 2 uses radiance cascades as its GI. Its version works in screen space and fills the cascades by marching the depth buffer, which the paper says was chosen because the game's camera is fixed. That puts it in 8.4's territory, with the same blind spots, softened by a camera that never looks at the horizon. The paper also describes world-space versions in 2D and 3D.

8 Precomputed real-time GI: fixed walls, live light

Precomputed real-time GI splits the work differently. Unity's documentation describes Enlighten, the best-known version: in the editor it divides static surfaces into small patches, works out how much each patch can see each other one, and groups patches that see each other into systems. At run time it uses that visibility to bounce light from real-time lights between patches, writes the result into low-resolution lightmaps, and spreads the update over several frames.

Lights can move, change colour and switch off, and the sun can cross the sky, at a modest CPU cost. Geometry cannot move, because the visibility was precomputed, and updates take several frames, which Unity says suits slowly changing light and not effects or flickering lamps. Unity supports Enlighten Realtime GI until the end of Unity 6. Silicon Studio, which sells Enlighten, offers integrations for Unreal Engine 4 and 5 and lists titles from Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice to Infinity Nikki.

9 Choosing a family

Most projects do not choose an algorithm; they choose an engine feature and accept its family. The questions are what changes at run time, which platforms must run it, and what the art needs.

4 What it costs

Real-time GI is one of the biggest single items in a modern frame. Epic budgets Lumen at 4 ms of a 16.7 ms frame budget at 60 fps, for GI, reflections and fog together. The costs come from rays traced, the size of the store, and how much of it is refreshed each frame.

Two worked numbers show why these methods trace from a few thousand points rather than from every pixel. DDGI with 32 × 8 × 32 = 8,192 probes at 64 rays each traces 524,288 rays a frame. Lumen's screen probes at 1080p, one per 16 × 16 pixels, come to 120 × 67.5 ≈ 8,100 probes before adaptive extras; at 8 × 8 = 64 directions each, about 518,400 rays. One ray per pixel would be 2,073,600. Storage is modest by comparison: DDGI's 8,192 probes, at 64 texels of 4-byte light and 256 texels of 4-byte distance each, take 8,192 × 1,280 bytes = 10 MB before the gutters around each probe.

  • Rays per frameProbes times rays per probe, or screen probes times directions. Quality presets mostly change these two numbers.
  • Scene complexity for tracingDistance fields cost disk space and memory per mesh; hardware ray tracing pays per instance, and Epic warns above about 100,000.
  • Reach and resolutionEvery extra cascade or larger volume adds cells or probes; finer cells near the camera multiply them.
  • Update rateRefreshing more of the store each frame reacts faster to change and costs more. Lumen and DDGI both trade lag for time.
  • ReflectionsGlossy reflections need far more directional detail than diffuse light; Lumen's cost grows with the share of smooth surfaces on screen.
  • Internal resolutionPer-pixel gathering scales with pixels, which is why Lumen's budget assumes 1080p upscaled.

What you trade for the milliseconds

Real-time GI buys you no bake step, lighting that follows change, and faster iteration for lighting artists. It costs GPU time on every frame of every player, minimum hardware requirements, and content rules about wall thickness and emissives. Many shipped games keep both: real-time GI on capable hardware, baked or simpler lighting on the lowest settings.

5 How it breaks

Real-time GI goes wrong in space, where light lands in the wrong place, and in time, where it arrives late or flickers.

Light leaking through walls

Cause
Walls thinner than the method's cells, voxels or probe spacing, or single-plane walls. Epic's minimum for Lumen is 10 cm.
Ask for
Wall thickness checks against the method's rule, the method's leak settings, and probe placement kept out of walls.

Dark blotches in lit areas

Cause
Darkness leaking: a probe buried inside geometry reports black and is blended into nearby pixels, or a mesh the surface cache covers badly bounces no light.
Ask for
Probe relocation or invalidation where the method has it, and the method's debug view of its scene, such as Lumen's surface cache view, where uncovered areas show pink.

Bounced light lags behind changes

Cause
Stores refreshed a slice per frame and blended over time: DDGI's hysteresis, Lumen's temporal filter, SDFGI's 30 frames to converge, Enlighten's multi-frame updates.
Ask for
The method's update-speed settings, such as Lumen's Lighting Update Speed controls, and a recording of sudden changes at the target frame rate.

Popping and shifting as the camera moves

Cause
Cascades or caches centred on the camera move and are rebuilt: SDFGI's documentation notes visible cascade shifts with fast movement; Lumen's scene can lag when the camera moves fast.
Ask for
Captures of fast traversal and camera cuts, more cascades or reach where shifts land in view, and pre-warming after cuts.

Blotchy, boiling indirect light

Cause
Too few rays for a small, very bright source: Epic's Lumen talk notes a limit to how small and bright emissive areas can be without noise. Low quality presets make it worse.
Ask for
Real lights for small bright sources, larger and dimmer emissive areas, and the GI quality setting per platform reviewed in motion.

Moving objects missing from the bounce

Cause
The method's scene leaves them out: SDFGI ignores dynamic occluders and emissives, Lumen's software tracing does not represent skinned meshes, VoxelGI only includes baked or Dynamic-mode meshes.
Ask for
Which objects each method's scene includes, screen-space AO or traces for the rest, and hardware ray tracing where characters must bounce light.

To diagnose, view the method's own data: Lumen's visualisation modes for its scene and surface cache, Godot's SDFGI and VoxelGI debug views, and probe visualisations for probe grids. Most GI bugs are visible there before they are visible in the final image.

7 Say it to your AI

GI briefs need what changes in the game, every target platform and its budget, the kinds of spaces, and the content rules the team can live with. Ask for the method's failure cases as well as its settings.

Questions to ask when reviewing real-time GI work

  1. What does the method store light in, and how big are its cells, voxels or probe spacing?That size sets the thinnest wall that will not leak.
  2. What in the scene contributes to the GI, and what only receives it?Moving objects are often left out, so they cast no bounce or occlusion.
  3. How long does the lighting take to catch up after a sudden change?Every method here trades update speed for cost.
  4. What is the GI cost on the weakest target, and what replaces it on low settings?The fallback is what many players will see.
  5. Have the content rules been written down and checked in the levels?Wall thickness and emissive size decide whether the method behaves.
  6. Have the method's debug views been reviewed, not just the final image?Leaks, gaps and stale data show there first.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why do grid and voxel methods leak light through thin walls, and what does DDGI add to stop it?

    Show answer

    Their cells or voxels are larger than the wall, so one cell holds both sides, or a probe outside the wall is blended into pixels inside it by distance alone. DDGI stores how far each probe can see in every direction; a pixel further away than the nearest surface in its direction must be hidden from that probe, so the probe's weight drops close to zero.

  2. Lumen traces only a few thousand screen probes rather than every pixel. What does that save, and what does it cost?

    Show answer

    About one probe per 16 × 16 pixels at 64 directions is around half a million rays a frame at 1080p, far fewer than many rays per pixel, and each hit reads its light from the surface cache instead of being shaded. The cost is detail between probes, made up by interpolation, a temporal filter and short-range AO, and a slower reaction to change, which shows as lag or ghosting.

  3. A game has a day-night cycle but its levels never change shape, and it must run on Switch-class hardware. Which family fits, and why not Lumen or SDFGI?

    Show answer

    Precomputed real-time GI such as Enlighten, or several baked lighting scenarios blended over the day, because only the lights change. Lumen does not support mobile-class renderers and SDFGI needs Godot's Forward+ renderer and a desktop-class GPU.
