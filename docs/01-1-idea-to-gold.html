1.1 From idea to gold master

A commercial game moves through a series of phases, and each one ends at a gate where the team must prove something before it is allowed to spend more. Knowing what each gate proves, and when graphics decisions turn expensive, is most of what a lead needs to steer the work.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Most commercial games are made in phases. Each phase answers a question. Is the idea any good? Can we build it, and what will it look like? Can we build all of it? Is it finished? Each phase ends at a milestone, a dated checkpoint where the team shows a build and the people paying for the game decide whether to keep going.

The early phases are small, cheap and full of unknowns. The later ones are large, expensive and supposed to be predictable. The whole system exists to move unknowns out of the project before the expensive part starts. Graphics sits right in the middle of that: the look, the target hardware and the performance budget are among the biggest unknowns a project has, and among the most expensive to change late.

Everyday comparison

A film starts as a script and a pitch. In pre-production the team casts, storyboards, scouts locations, shoots tests and fixes a budget. Only then does the full crew arrive for production, and after the shoot the film is edited, finished and locked before copies go out to cinemas.

Nobody books a 200-person crew to shoot a script that has not been tested. Games use the same words, pre-production and production, for the same reason.

The problem it solves

In the project above, monthly spending grows about 25 times from the first months to the peak. A mistake found in month 2 costs a conversation. The same mistake found in month 30 can cost a hundred people's time.

Gates make each big jump in spending wait for evidence: a prototype that is fun, a slice that looks right and runs on the real hardware, a build that is complete.

Why a lead cares

Graphics decisions have a price that rises with every phase. Choosing a lighting approach costs a prototype in pre-production and months of rework in late production. Leads write the gate criteria, so they decide what evidence graphics work must show and when. And because an AI agent can produce an impressive demo in days, knowing what a gate actually has to prove is what stops a fast demo being mistaken for a solved problem.

3 How it works

Follow one project from the first idea to the months after launch. Studios name and split the phases differently, but the order and the questions stay remarkably stable. There are nine steps.

1 Concept: an idea worth testing

The concept phase is where a few people work out what the game is: the core idea, who it is for, which platforms it targets, roughly how big it is and roughly what it will cost. The output is a pitch: a document or deck, some concept art, often a short playable or a video, and a rough budget and schedule.

Graphics enters early here, mostly as decisions rather than work. The platform list sets the ceiling for everything visual. A game aimed at phones and handhelds and one aimed only at current consoles and high-end PCs can share a pitch, but not a renderer, a lighting approach or an art style. The concept ends at a greenlight: approval and money to start pre-production.

2 Prototypes answer one question each

A prototype is a quick, disposable build made to answer one question. Good prototypes are small and narrow on purpose. They are allowed to be ugly, buggy and badly written, because they are thrown away once they have answered their question.

For a lead, the important point is that a prototype only answers the question it was built for. A gameplay prototype says nothing about whether the game can hit its frame rate, and a gorgeous visual target says nothing about whether it is fun.

3 Pre-production: planning by building

Pre-production turns the pitch into a plan that has been tested. A team of perhaps a quarter of its eventual size builds prototypes, the core tools and pipeline, the first finished content, and a schedule and budget for the rest. It is the phase where changing direction is still affordable.

Insomniac's postmortem of Ratchet & Clank shows what that buys. The studio spent about six months of pre-production on a different game, built a first playable that its founder described as not bad but not great, and on 20 March 2001 stopped and started over. The new concept, which became Ratchet & Clank, came together within two weeks. Six months of a small team is an expensive lesson; six months of a full production team would have been far worse.

Three kinds of graphics work belong here:

  • Look development. Look development is the work of finding and proving the visual style in the engine: target images, test scenes, a first set of materials and lighting rigs, until the art director can point at a screen and say that this is how the game will look.
  • Technical targets. Platforms, resolution, frame rate, which rendering features are in and which are out. These decide how much every later asset may cost.
  • Budgets. The target frame rate becomes a frame budget, split between the parts of the frame, plus memory and per-asset limits that artists can build to.

Simplified

Real budgets also cover CPU time, memory per level, streaming bandwidth, draw calls and download size, and they usually differ per platform. Each one is a finite resource split in advance, so that nobody discovers at alpha that everyone has already spent it.

4 The vertical slice

Pre-production usually ends with a vertical slice: a small part of the game, such as one level or ten minutes of play, built to final quality through every layer. Final gameplay, final art, final lighting, effects, audio and interface, running on the target hardware within budget. It is the show home on an estate that has not been built yet.

The slice answers questions no prototype can: what the game really looks and plays like, whether the pipeline can produce finished content, and how long one finished area takes. That last number is what turns a slice into a production schedule. At GDC 2015, Volition described using its vertical slices as the test of whether a team is ready to move from pre-production into production.

The slice is not a trailer

Slices are often shown to publishers and press, which tempts teams to hand-tune one area beyond what the pipeline can repeat, or to run it on a high-end PC. A slice that cannot be reproduced at the same cost, on the target hardware, gives the production schedule a false foundation.

5 Milestones and gates

A milestone has a date, a build and a list of things the build must show. The list matters more than the date. Criteria written at the start of a phase, such as "level 3 at final quality, 60 fps on base console, no crash in a two-hour soak test", make the review a check. Criteria invented in the review make it a negotiation, and negotiations tend to end in a pass.

When a publisher funds the game, milestones are usually written into the contract and payments are tied to them. Self-funded studios run the same reviews internally. Small teams often use short, regular milestones instead of a few big ones: during Hades' early access, Supergiant worked to a monthly cadence, with large code changes early in each month and testing and polish later.

6 Production and alpha

Production is where the bulk of the game gets made. The team grows to its full size, often with outside studios building some of the art, and the work turns from discovery into throughput: levels, characters, props, animations, effects and code, made to the budgets and the pipeline that pre-production proved.

Rendering features are still being added early in production. They should not be added for ever. At alpha the game is feature complete: every system and mechanic is in and playable from start to finish, even if much of the content is rough. From alpha on, the team stops starting new features. This is often called a feature freeze, and it is the point where graphics work turns from building to measuring and fixing.

Studios define alpha differently, and some put a separate content-complete milestone before beta or fold it into alpha. When you join a project, ask for the written definition of each milestone rather than assuming one.

7 Beta, content complete and gold master

Content complete means every level, character, asset, line of dialogue and piece of interface is in the game in its final or near-final form. On many teams this defines beta: a complete game in which the only work left is fixing bugs, optimising and polishing. Wikipedia's summary of common practice puts beta two to three months before the code is released and the first release candidate three to four weeks before.

A release candidate is a build the team believes could ship. For a console game it goes to the platform holder for certification, a technical check against the platform's rules that 1.4 Platforms and what they change covers in detail. The build that passes and is locked is the gold master, the version used to manufacture discs and seed digital stores. Teams say a game has gone gold at the moment they stop changing that build.

8 Launch, the day-one patch and live ops

Gold is not the end of the work. Discs have to be manufactured and shipped, and stores have to be stocked, which takes weeks. The team keeps fixing bugs throughout, and those fixes ship as a day-one patch: an update released on or before launch day that players download over the gold build. When No Man's Sky launched in 2016, Sean Murray said most of the team was back at work the day after the game went gold, building its large launch update.

After launch, many games continue as a service. Live ops is the ongoing work of running a released game: hotfixes, scheduled patches, events, seasons and new content, each one built, tested and certified like a small release. Bungie's GDC 2024 talk on Destiny 2 describes planning expansions, seasonal updates, scheduled maintenance patches and emergency hotfixes across several content teams at once.

Live ops changes the graphics job in two ways. New content has to fit the same budgets on hardware that is now in players' hands, so a heavy new effect cannot quietly break the frame rate on the weakest console. And the renderer has to keep working across platform updates, new driver versions and new hardware for as long as the game is sold.

9 Where AI changes the timeline

AI tools are now part of many teams' daily work. In GDC's 2026 State of the Game Industry survey of more than 2,300 professionals, 36% said they use generative AI tools in their job, most often for research and brainstorming, code assistance and prototyping. The same survey found 52% thought generative AI was having a negative effect on the industry, so expect a range of attitudes on any team.

On a timeline, AI changes how fast drafts appear. It does not change what each gate has to prove.

Two consequences matter for leads. First, cheap demos make the gate criteria more important, because an AI agent can produce something that looks like a vertical slice without the pipeline, budgets or repeatable cost that make a real one. Second, more drafted content means more review: the bottleneck moves from making assets to checking them, so the review band in the picture needs people and time planned for it.

4 What it costs

For a production, the unit of cost is people-time. Add up the team band in the picture month by month and the illustrative project uses about 2,340 person-months, or 195 person-years, from concept to launch.

Money follows directly. At an assumed fully loaded cost of $12,000 per person per month (salary, benefits, equipment and office; the real figure varies widely between countries), 2,340 person-months is about $28 million before marketing. The live team of 35 then costs about $420,000 a month for as long as the game is supported.

The same decision, two prices

Take one graphics decision: moving from baked lighting to fully dynamic lighting, perhaps because the design now needs a day and night cycle. Here is an illustrative estimate of what it costs at two points in the project.

The late version also lands when the team is at full size and the schedule has least slack, so it tends to push something else out or into overtime. That is why baked lighting, LOD strategy and the frame budget are pre-production decisions on well-run projects.

What makes the bill grow

  • Team size × timeEvery month of slip at the peak costs about 25 times a month of slip in concept.
  • Late discoveryA problem found after content is built is paid for once per asset it touches.
  • Platform countEach extra target adds its own performance work, quality settings, QA passes and certification.
  • Unfrozen featuresEach feature added after alpha destabilises a build that everyone else is trying to measure.
  • OvertimeLong hours lift output for a short time, then raise the bug rate and drive people out of the team.
  • Live commitmentsA promised season of content costs people every month it runs.

Crunch is a cost

When a schedule runs out, teams often fall back on crunch, sustained long hours to hit a date. Insomniac's founder wrote of Ratchet & Clank that the team was "always in crunch mode", and the practice was widespread for years. It looks free in the budget because it is unpaid or fixed-salary time, but it shows up as tired mistakes, sick leave and experienced people leaving. Plan buffer and a cut list instead.

5 How it breaks

Projects rarely fail at the moment the trouble starts. They fail a year later, when the trouble meets a gate it cannot pass. These five patterns cover most of the graphics-related cases.

The slice that only ran on a PC

Cause
The vertical slice was built and shown on a development PC. Nobody measured it on the weakest target, so production assumed headroom that never existed.
Ask for
Gate criteria that name the hardware: frame time and memory measured on the weakest target, captured, attached to the milestone.

Budgets arrive after the content

Cause
Production started before triangle, texture and frame-time budgets were set, or they were set but never checked. Hundreds of assets now need rework.
Ask for
Budgets before content, and automatic checks at import that flag any asset over its limit the day it is added.

Features keep landing after alpha

Cause
New rendering features or minor visual upgrades were accepted after the freeze. Each one moves the performance baseline and adds bugs while the team is trying to stabilise.
Ask for
A written feature freeze with a change-control list: any late feature needs a named owner, a cost, and something cut to pay for it.

Production starts with open questions

Cause
Pre-production was cut short, or the gate passed on enthusiasm. The art style, a key rendering feature or the lighting approach is still undecided when the full team arrives.
Ask for
A list of open technical and visual risks at every gate, each with an owner and a date by which a prototype will close it.

Crunch as the plan

Cause
The schedule had no buffer, cuts were postponed, and late surprises were absorbed with overtime. Quality and bug counts get worse just as the team needs them to improve.
Ask for
A visible cut list from pre-production on, schedule buffer before beta, and weekly tracking of open bugs against the time left.

The first pattern has a famous recent example. Cyberpunk 2077 launched in December 2020 to widespread criticism of its performance on the base PlayStation 4 and Xbox One, although the PC version was largely well received. CD Projekt apologised for not showing those versions before release and said it should have paid more attention to how the game ran on them, and on 18 December Sony removed the game from the PlayStation Store and offered refunds. Whatever happened inside the studio, the lesson for leads is the same: the weakest target has to be in the evidence at every gate.

7 Say it to your AI

AI is useful for production work that is mostly structured writing and checking: drafting gate criteria, turning them into automated checks, and reviewing a plan for risks. Give it the platforms, the team size, the dates and the current state in numbers.

BriefUse when planning a vertical slice gate
We are a 25-person team making a third-person action game in Unreal Engine 5.7 for PS5, Xbox Series X|S and PC. Xbox Series S is our weakest target, at 30 fps. Pre-production ends in 8 months with a vertical slice: one 15-minute level.

Draft the graphics criteria for the vertical slice gate.
1. Make every criterion measurable, and say on which hardware and how it is measured (tool, scene, camera path).
2. Cover frame time, memory, loading, visual quality against our target images, and pipeline repeatability (how long the next level will take).
3. For each criterion, say what would make it a conditional pass and what would make it a fail.
4. List the open graphics risks you would expect at this stage, and the prototype that would close each one before the gate.
  • Naming the weakest target stops criteria being written for a development PC.
  • Asking how each criterion is measured turns wishes into checks.
  • Pipeline repeatability is the part of a slice that teams most often forget to prove.
ReviewUse when a schedule needs a risk check
Attached is our production schedule (milestones, dates, team size per month) and our current list of rendering features with their status. We hit alpha in 5 months.

Review it as a production lead with a graphics background.
- Which rendering features are not yet proven on Xbox Series S, and which content depends on them?
- Where does the schedule assume optimisation will happen without time set aside for it?
- What would you freeze now, and what would you put on a cut list?
- Give me three questions to ask the graphics leads this week.
Do not rewrite the schedule; point at the risks and explain each in two sentences.
  • Giving the real schedule and feature list keeps the answer specific.
  • The questions target the two classic late failures: unproven features and unplanned optimisation.
  • Asking for risks rather than a new plan keeps decisions with the team.

Questions to ask when reviewing AI work on production plans and demos

  1. Was this measured on the weakest target hardware, and where is the capture?A demo on a development PC proves nothing about a console or a phone.
  2. Which gate criterion does this evidence satisfy, and which does it only resemble?Fast AI-made demos can look like a vertical slice without the repeatable pipeline behind one.
  3. What would changing this decision cost after the next gate?It tells you whether to decide now or leave it open a little longer.
  4. Is this a new feature after the freeze? Who approved it, and what was cut to pay for it?Late features are the commonest source of late instability.
  5. Does generated content go through the same budgets, import checks and review as hand-made content?More content is only useful if it fits the frame and the memory.

8 Check yourself

  1. The team shows a stunning 3-minute capture of one level, recorded on a high-end PC, and asks to start production. Is that a vertical slice?

    Show answer

    Not yet. A vertical slice is playable, at final quality in every layer, running on the target hardware within budget, and built with the pipeline production will use. Ask for it on the weakest target, with captures, and for how long the level took to make.

  2. In this chapter's example, moving from baked to fully dynamic lighting costs 6 person-weeks in pre-production. Roughly what does it cost in late production, and why?

    Show answer

    About 65 person-weeks, around 11 times more, because 30 finished levels must be relit, re-optimised, adjusted and retested. The work is paid once for every asset the decision touches.

  3. Two months after alpha, a publisher asks for a new ray-traced reflections mode for the PC version. What do you ask before agreeing?

    Show answer

    It breaks the feature freeze, so: what does it cost in people and weeks, who owns it, how it affects performance, testing and stability on every platform, and what will be cut to pay for it. Often the better answer is a post-launch patch.
