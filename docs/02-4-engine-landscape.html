2.4 The engine landscape

Teams choose between three general-purpose engines, Unreal Engine, Unity and Godot, or build their own. Each names the same ideas differently, aims at different hardware and charges in a different way, and the choice shapes a project for years.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A game engine gives a team a ready-made editor, renderer, physics, audio and build system, so it can spend its time on the game. Three general-purpose engines are in wide use today.

Unreal Engine, made by Epic Games, is written in C++ and aims first at high-end PCs and consoles. Its fifth version is known for features that let artists use very detailed models and fully dynamic lighting. Unity, made by Unity Technologies, is scripted in C# and runs on a very wide range of platforms, from phones and headsets to consoles. Godot is free and open source, supported by the Godot Foundation, with its own scripting language, GDScript.

All three share the ideas from 2.3 Scenes, objects and components: a scene full of objects built from components, saved as reusable prefabs. They name those ideas differently, ship different renderers and charge in different ways.

Everyday comparison

A chef who moves to a new restaurant cooks the same way, but the knives live in a different drawer, the stations have different names and the ovens are a different make. A good chef is useful within a week.

Engines are kitchens. The cooking, the rendering ideas in this book, is the same. The names, the menus and the equipment differ.

The problem it solves

Picking an engine fixes which platforms you can reach, which rendering features you get for free, who you can hire and what you pay. Changing engine mid-project means rebuilding content and code, so studios that switch usually do it between games.

Knowing the landscape lets you make that decision once, on purpose.

Why a lead cares

Engine choice is one of the earliest and most expensive decisions a project makes, and the render pipeline inside the engine is a second one that is nearly as hard to undo. Every AI brief also needs the engine, its version and its pipeline at the top: answers for Unreal, Unity URP, Unity HDRP and Godot differ, and an answer for the wrong one can look right and still fail to compile.

3 How it works

Six things decide where an engine fits: the hardware it aims at, how its renderer is organised, its headline features, its licence, its source access, and whether building your own would serve better.

1 Where each engine aims

Every engine runs on more than one kind of hardware, but each is designed around some of it first. Unreal's Nanite and Virtual Shadow Maps need GPUs that support DirectX 12 with Shader Model 6 or later, which means current consoles and modern PCs; phones get a separate, simpler mobile renderer. Unity's main pipeline runs on every platform Unity supports and is tuned for phones and standalone VR headsets. Godot splits its range across three renderers, from the web up to desktop PCs, and reaches consoles through third-party companies.

Team size matters too. Godot costs nothing, Unity is free until a company earns or raises $200,000, and Unreal charges nothing until a game earns $1 million. A studio's own engine only makes sense when it can pay a team to keep it going.

Engines also move quickly. In November 2026 the current releases are Unreal Engine 5.8, Unity 6.6 and Godot 4.7. Epic has announced Unreal Engine 6 and is targeting an Early Access release at the end of 2027. Unity alternates feature releases with LTS (long-term support) releases that receive fixes for longer: 6.3 is the current LTS and 6.7 LTS is due at the end of 2026. Productions usually pick a version early and upgrade only on purpose.

2 Unity's render pipelines: URP and HDRP

A render pipeline is the part of an engine that decides how each frame is drawn: which passes run, in what order, with which lighting model. Most engines have one, with settings. Unity offers more than one, and each project picks one at the start; URP and HDRP are both built on Unity's Scriptable Render Pipeline system.

The Universal Render Pipeline (URP) runs on every platform Unity supports and is tuned for the tile-based GPUs in phones and standalone headsets. It offers four rendering paths, Forward, Forward+, Deferred and Deferred+, which 7.3 explains. The High Definition Render Pipeline (HDRP) targets PCs, Xbox and PlayStation with physically based light units, materials such as skin, hair and eyes, volumetric effects and ray tracing where the hardware supports it.

Unity's render pipeline strategy for 2026 changed the balance. URP is where new work goes, and Unity strongly encourages it for new projects. HDRP receives stability and critical fixes, with no new features planned, though Unity 6.5 added HDRP support for Nintendo Switch 2. The older Built-In Render Pipeline is deprecated as of Unity 6.5.

3 Godot's three renderers

Godot asks each project to choose a rendering method. Forward+ is the desktop renderer and gets new features first. Mobile is a simpler, faster renderer for phones and standalone VR that also runs on desktop. Compatibility uses OpenGL, runs on older and low-end hardware, and is the only choice for games exported to the web.

The choice decides which effects exist at all. Real-time global illumination, screen-space reflections and volumetric fog are Forward+ only. A web game therefore cannot have them, whatever the PC version looks like.

4 Unreal's headline features, by name

Unreal Engine 5 is often described by four features, three of which, Lumen, Virtual Shadow Maps and TSR, are on by default in new projects. Each gets its own chapter later in the book; here you only need the names and what they replace. Nanite draws extremely detailed geometry without hand-made levels of detail. Lumen computes bounced light and reflections every frame, without baking. Virtual Shadow Maps give sharp shadows over large worlds and are designed to work with Nanite. TSR, Temporal Super Resolution, renders fewer pixels and rebuilds a sharp image from several frames.

Epic adds to the list with each release. In Unreal Engine 5.8, MegaLights, a system for scenes with many dynamic lights, reached Production Ready status, and Lumen gained a cheaper Lumen Lite mode in beta.

5 Licences and source access

The three engines charge in three different ways. Unreal takes a royalty, a share of each game's revenue. Unity sells a seat licence for each person using the editor, priced by the company's size. Godot is open source under the MIT licence and charges nothing.

Source access differs too. Unreal's full C++ source is source-available: anyone who links a GitHub account to an Epic account and accepts the licence can read it, change it and build their own version, under Epic's licence, which limits how it may be shared. Unity publishes URP and HDRP and a reference copy of its C# layer; the C++ core comes with Enterprise plans. Godot's whole source is free to read, change and share.

6 When studios build their own

A custom engine is one a studio builds and keeps for its own games. Guerrilla's Decima powers the Horizon games and was also used for Death Stranding. A custom engine can fit one kind of game exactly, carries no royalty and keeps the studio in control of its schedule. It also needs a permanent team to support new platforms, new GPU features and the tools artists use every day.

That cost is why some studios leave. In 2022 CD Projekt Red, which built its own REDengine for Cyberpunk 2077, announced that the next Witcher saga would use Unreal Engine 5 under a partnership covering both licensing and joint work on the engine. In 2024 343 Industries renamed itself Halo Studios and moved Halo from its Slipspace engine to Unreal Engine 5. Its leaders said they wanted the team focused on making games rather than tools and engines, and that some parts of Slipspace were almost 25 years old.

4 What it costs

Take one made-up studio: 10 people use the editor, the game takes three years to make and grosses $3 million in its first year on sale, none of it on the Epic Games Store. The studio's revenue and funding are above $200,000 the whole time. Here is what each engine's licence costs over those four years.

Money is the smaller cost

The larger costs are usually time. Switching render pipeline in Unity means converting shaders and materials, which Unity describes as time-consuming. Switching engine means rebuilding most content and code; CD Projekt Red and Halo Studios both switched for their next games. A custom engine needs engineers whose whole job is the engine, for as long as the studio uses it.

  • RevenueUnreal's royalty grows with every dollar a game earns above $1 million.
  • Headcount and yearsUnity's seats grow with the number of people in the editor and the length of development.
  • PlatformsEach extra platform needs its features checked against the renderer. Godot console ports go through paid third parties.
  • Engine changesEvery local change to engine source must be carried forward at each upgrade.
  • Versions behindThe further a project falls behind, the bigger and riskier each upgrade.
  • Engine teamA custom engine costs a permanent team, on top of the game team.

5 How it breaks

Engine choices rarely fail on day one. They fail months later, when a platform, a feature or a bill arrives that nobody checked for.

The pipeline cannot reach a platform

Cause
The project chose HDRP, which runs on desktop, Xbox and PlayStation (and Switch 2 from Unity 6.5). A later port to phones or a standalone headset has no HDRP to run on, and moving to URP means converting every shader and material.
Ask for
A table of every target platform against the chosen pipeline or renderer, agreed in pre-production before content is made.

A feature is missing on one target

Cause
The feature exists in one renderer only. Godot web exports must use Compatibility, which has no volumetric fog or real-time GI; Unreal's Virtual Shadow Maps need Shader Model 6.6 GPUs.
Ask for
A per-platform feature list with a fallback for each missing feature, and builds on the weakest target from early on.

Code written for the wrong version

Cause
An AI answer or tutorial targets another version or pipeline, such as Godot 3 node names (Spatial became Node3D in Godot 4) or Unity surface shaders, which only the Built-In pipeline supports.
Ask for
Engine, version and pipeline at the top of every brief, and code that compiles in the real project before anyone reviews it.

Upgrades stall

Cause
The project fell several versions behind, with local engine changes and plugins that each need porting, so every upgrade gets bigger and is put off again.
Ask for
An upgrade policy: which LTS or hotfix line to follow, when to move, and a branch where each upgrade is tested before it lands.

A licence bill nobody planned

Cause
The licence was chosen for the studio as it was. Funding pushed it past Unity's $200,000 Personal limit, or a game passed Unreal's $1 million, and the budget had no line for seats or royalties.
Ask for
A licence check at every funding round and launch, with costs modelled at three revenue levels.

The engine eats the team

Cause
A custom engine needs constant work for new platforms, GPU features and tools. Halo Studios gave keeping its team focused on games, rather than engines, as a reason to move to Unreal.
Ask for
The engine team's headcount and roadmap set against what the next game needs, reviewed every year.

7 Say it to your AI

Engine facts change faster than any model's training data. Good briefs pin the version, ask for sources with dates, and make the AI show its arithmetic. Adapt these.

BriefUse when choosing an engine for a new project
We are choosing an engine for a new game.
Team: 12 people, 4 programmers who know C# and some C++, no engine programmers.
Game: third-person action in a walled town, stylised art, day-night cycle.
Targets: PC (Steam), PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series at launch; Meta Quest 3 a year later.

Compare Unreal Engine 5.8, Unity 6.6 and Godot 4.7 for this project. For each:
1. Which renderer or render pipeline you would use, and why.
2. Which of our platforms it supports directly, which need third parties, and what that involves.
3. Licence cost if the game grosses $1M, $5M and $20M, with the arithmetic shown.
4. The two biggest risks for this team.
Cite the official documentation or pricing page for every platform and licence claim, with the date you checked it.

  • The Quest port tests whether the pipeline choice survives the second year.
  • Asking for dated sources exposes stale licence terms.

  1. Which engine version and which renderer or pipeline does this answer assume?Answers silently mix versions and pipelines.

8 Check yourself

  1. A Godot 4 game ships on PC and in web browsers. The art director wants volumetric fog in both. What do you tell them?

    Show answer

    Web exports must use the Compatibility renderer, which has no volumetric fog. The PC build can have it with Forward+; the web build needs a cheaper look, such as Godot's ordinary depth and height fog, which Compatibility supports.

  2. An Unreal Engine game grosses $1.4 million over its lifetime, none of it on the Epic Games Store. What is the standard royalty?

    Show answer

    5% of the revenue above $1 million: 5% × $400,000 = $20,000.

  3. A Unity team plans a standalone Meta Quest 3 game. URP or HDRP?

    Show answer

    URP. HDRP runs on desktop and consoles only, while URP is tuned for standalone VR headsets and is the pipeline Unity recommends for new projects.
