12.4 Visual bug field guide

Most visual bugs in games have names, and each name points at a family of causes that an earlier chapter explains. This chapter is a field guide to them: how to name what you see, four questions that narrow it down, a gallery of 29 common bugs with what to check and what to ask for, and how to capture evidence and catch regressions automatically.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A rendering artefact is a visible error that a rendering technique produces: stripes, halos, flicker, trails, seams, steps. They are not random. Each technique fails in its own characteristic way, which is why every chapter of this book ended with a gallery of how it breaks. This chapter gathers those galleries into one field guide.

A field guide works by asking a few questions about what you see: where it is, what it looks like, and what makes it change. The same questions work for visual bugs. Does it move with the camera, with the light, or on its own? Is it on one model or the whole screen? On one platform or all of them? Since which build? Three or four answers usually narrow a bug to one family of causes, and the family tells you which chapter, which debug view and which settings to look at.

An everyday comparison

A mechanic listening to a car. "It makes a noise" is no help. "A squeal when braking, only when the brakes are cold" points straight at the brake pads, and the mechanic knows what to check first and what part to order.

The problem it solves

A report like "the shadows look weird" sends an AI or a colleague off to guess. A report that names the bug, says what makes it change and attaches the right evidence gets a fix aimed at the cause, and a way to prove it worked.

Why a lead cares

Visual bugs are where non-specialists and specialists most often talk past each other. A shared vocabulary lets QA, artists, producers and AI agents describe what they see in words a graphics programmer can act on. It also makes bug reports searchable, so the team can see that the same artefact keeps coming back from the same system.

3 How it works

The steps follow a bug from first sight to a report: name it, place it, ask four questions, look it up, isolate it with a debug view, and capture the evidence. The last two steps cover catching bugs automatically before anyone sees them.

1 Name what you see

Start by describing the bug in the book's vocabulary instead of in impressions. "Stripes", "trails", "steps", "a halo", "a gap under the object" are all better than "looks wrong", because each matches a known artefact. The name is then a search term, a line for the brief (12.2), and a pointer to the chapter that explains the cause.

2 Where you see it

The gallery sorts bugs into six families by where they appear: in shadows, in lighting, in the geometry itself, on textures, only in motion, or across the whole screen. The family is the first filter. It tells you which settings to open and which debug view to try first.

3 Four questions: the diagnosis flow

The strongest clue is what makes a bug change. Freeze everything you can, then move one thing at a time: the camera, then the light, then nothing at all. Most engines let you pause the game and fly the camera, and lighting artists can move the sun in the editor.

The flow gives a starting point to test. Some bugs answer yes to two questions: z-fighting changes with the camera and flickers over time; a cascade line moves with the camera and its texels change with the light. Follow the first yes, and check the result against the gallery below.

4 The symptom gallery

The gallery collects 29 common bugs from across the book. Filter by where you see the bug and by what changes it, which are the first questions of the diagnosis flow, then pick a thumbnail to read the entry.

5 Isolate it with debug views

A finished frame mixes everything: textures, light, shadows, reflections, fog and post-processing. Debug views separate them. If a bug is still there with lighting alone, it is not a texture problem; if it disappears in the unlit view, it is not a material problem. Switching views is the quickest test there is, and it needs no tools beyond the editor.

6 Capture the evidence

A good visual bug report carries six pieces of evidence, each proving something the others cannot. Together with reproduction steps, they let a colleague or an AI agent see the bug without being in the room, test a fix, and know when it is done.

Motion bugs need video. Record from the game's output at its full frame rate, or use the engine's own recorder: Godot's Movie Maker mode, for example, can write a lossless PNG sequence at a fixed frame rate, from the editor or an exported build. A phone filming a monitor adds its own flicker and blur, and its frame rate rarely matches the game's.

7 Golden images and screenshot comparison

A visual regression is a change that makes something look wrong that looked right before. The way to catch them automatically is a screenshot comparison: render a fixed scene from fixed cameras with fixed settings, and compare each image with a golden image, an approved screenshot stored with the project. Unreal's Screenshot Comparison tool and Unity's Graphics Test Framework both work this way.

Golden images are usually needed per platform and per graphics API, because the same frame differs slightly between GPUs, drivers and graphics APIs. Unity's Graphics Test Framework, for example, stores reference images in folders by colour space, platform and graphics API by default. Unreal's Screenshot Comparison tool offers tolerance presets from Zero to High, and an option to disable noisy rendering features while capturing. Temporal effects are the main source of noise: TAA jitter, particles, time of day and streaming all change pixels between runs, so tests fix the random seed and time step and wait for the image to settle.

Screenshot comparison is the visual check an AI coding agent can run on its own, which is why it sits in the automated gate of 12.3. An agent that changes a shader and re-runs the screenshot tests can see its own regressions; an agent without them can only describe what it thinks the change will look like.

8 Find the change: bisection

When a bug appeared "at some point", the fastest route to its cause is usually the change that introduced it. Bisection finds it by testing the build halfway between a known good one and a known bad one, keeping the half that still contains the bug, and repeating. Version control tools automate the bookkeeping; Git's version is git bisect.

4 What it costs

The cost of a visual bug grows with the time between the change that caused it and the moment someone finds the cause. Found in review, it costs minutes. Found by players, it costs days, because nobody knows which change, which machine or which settings.

Worked example, illustrative. A team keeps 40 camera bookmarks as screenshot tests on 4 platforms: 160 images per run. A lossless 1920 × 1080 image is 8.3 MB uncompressed at 4 bytes per pixel, and typically a few megabytes as a PNG; at 3 MB each, one set of golden images is about 480 MB, and every intended visual change replaces some of them. Each shot needs a level load and a few seconds for streaming, auto-exposure and temporal effects to settle; at 6 seconds a shot, a run takes 16 minutes of machine time per build, plus loading. The people-time is in reviewing failures, so a test that fails at random costs more than it saves.

  • Time to discoveryEvery day between the change and the report adds builds to search and context to rebuild.
  • Platforms and APIsEach needs its own golden images, captures and testers.
  • Temporal noiseTAA, particles and streaming make tests flaky unless the scene is made deterministic.
  • Vague reportsNo name, no steps and no evidence turn a minutes-long fix into an afternoon of reproduction.
  • Intended changesA new tone curve or lighting pass fails every test, and someone has to review and approve each image.

The usual trade

Teams start with a small set of screenshot tests on the scenes that matter most and the platforms that differ most, run them on every change to rendering code, and grow the set each time a regression slips through. A short, reliable set that people trust beats a large one that fails at random.

5 How it breaks

Bug hunting fails in its own ways, and each one wastes the evidence or hides the cause.

The bug vanishes in the capture

Cause
Capturing changes the timing, so race conditions and timing-dependent bugs stop happening while the tool watches.
Ask for
A video at full frame rate and logs from the run that showed it, then captures from several frames around it (11.5).

Compression ate the evidence

Cause
A JPEG or a compressed video smoothed away the banding, or added blocks of its own that look like a new bug.
Ask for
Lossless PNG screenshots and, for motion, a lossless or high-quality recording at the game's frame rate.

The video missed the flicker

Cause
A recording at 30 fps keeps every other frame of a 60 fps game, so a one-frame flicker or pop is never recorded.
Ask for
Recordings at the game's full frame rate, stepped frame by frame when looking for the bad one.

A regression approved as the new golden image

Cause
Someone approved every changed screenshot after an intended change, including one that was a real bug.
Ask for
A reason recorded for each approved image, and a second look at any image the change was not meant to affect.

A screenshot test nobody believes

Cause
Temporal noise, particles, time of day or streaming change pixels between runs, so the test fails at random and the team learns to ignore it.
Ask for
Fixed seeds and time step, warm-up frames, noisy features off during capture, and a tolerance tuned on repeated runs of the same build.

The fix moved the bug

Cause
A setting was pushed until the symptom vanished: more bias cures acne and causes peter-panning; a clamp hides a NaN and keeps its cause.
Ask for
The cause named before the fix, and screenshots of the neighbouring artefact the fix could create (12.3).

7 Say it to your AI

Two briefs: one asks for a diagnosis from evidence before any fix, the other asks for a screenshot test that would have caught the bug.

Diagnose from evidenceUse when a visual bug is reported
Context: [engine and version], [render pipeline and path], [platforms].

A visual bug: [name, if known, such as shadow acne or ghosting].
What we see: [one or two sentences].
What changes it: [moves with the camera / with the light / over time with nothing moving / stuck to one model / whole screen].
Where: [camera bookmark, level], [quality level], [platforms it does and does not happen on].
Since: [last good build] / [first bad build].
Attached: [lossless screenshot], [video at full frame rate], [debug view showing it], [frame capture].

Do not change any code yet.
1. Name the most likely artefact and the two next most likely, with the evidence for and against each.
2. For each, say which pass or setting would produce it, and which debug view or capture value would confirm it.
3. Propose the cheapest test that separates them.
Then stop and wait.
  • The "what changes it" line is the diagnosis flow in one sentence.
  • Ranked alternatives with evidence stop the agent fixing the first plausible cause.
  • A separating test turns a guess into a check a person can run in minutes.
Add a screenshot testUse after a visual bug is fixed
We fixed [bug] in [system]. Add a screenshot test that would have caught it.

- Use [Unreal's Screenshot Comparison with a Functional Screenshot Test actor / Unity's Graphics Test Framework / our own comparison script].
- Scene: [test level], camera [bookmark], [quality level], resolution [W × H].
- Make it deterministic: fixed random seed, fixed time step, [N] warm-up frames, noisy features off if the tool supports it.
- Tolerance: start from the tool's default, run the unfixed and fixed builds five times each, and choose a threshold that fails every unfixed run and passes every fixed one. Report the numbers.
- Golden images for [platforms and APIs], approved by [person].

Deliver the test, the numbers from the ten runs, and the images for review.
  • A test that fails on the old build proves it would have caught the bug.
  • Repeated runs measure the noise before the threshold is set.
  • A named approver keeps golden images from being waved through.

Questions to ask when reviewing a visual bug fix

  1. Which artefact was it, and what evidence confirmed the cause?A fix without a named cause is often a symptom moved somewhere else.
  2. Does the fix create the neighbouring artefact, such as acne to peter-panning?Many fixes trade one artefact for another.
  3. Was it checked with the same camera, settings and platform as the report?A fix confirmed elsewhere is not yet confirmed.
  4. Is there now a screenshot test, and does it fail on the old build?That is what keeps the bug from coming back.
  5. Which golden images changed, and why?An approval without a reason is how regressions become the reference.

8 Check yourself

  1. A dark outline appears on the wall behind a character, even when the character stands a metre away. It shifts as the camera moves and does not change when the sun moves. Which family and which artefact do you suspect, and what is the quickest check?

    Show answer

    Lighting, and a dark ambient occlusion halo: screen-space AO cannot tell a character in front of a wall from one touching it, and it moves with the camera because it is computed from the depth buffer. The quickest check is to switch AO off, or open the AO view, and see whether the outline goes (8.3).

  2. A playtester reports that "the shadows flicker sometimes". What would you ask for before anyone starts on a fix?

    Show answer

    The evidence kit: where it happens (bookmark, quality level, platform), what changes it (camera, light, nothing), a lossless screenshot and a video at the game's full frame rate, the build and the last build where it did not happen, and steps that make it happen every time. With those, the diagnosis flow and the gallery usually name it: crawling edges, a cascade line or an unstable bias.

  3. A screenshot test fails on every platform after a change to the tone curve that the art lead asked for. What should happen next?

    Show answer

    A person compares each failing image with its golden image, confirms that the only difference is the intended one, and approves new golden images with a reason. Any image that changed in an unexpected way, or a place the change should not have reached, is a possible regression and goes back for investigation before approval.
