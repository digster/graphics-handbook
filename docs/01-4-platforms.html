1.4 Platforms and what they change

One game may have to run on a desktop PC, a console under a TV, a phone in a pocket and a headset strapped to someone's face. Each platform fixes a different screen, frame rate, memory size and power budget, and each sets rules the game must pass before it can be sold there.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A platform is everything a game runs on and sells through: the hardware, its operating system, its store and the rules of the company behind it. PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X|S, Nintendo Switch 2, Windows PCs, iPhones and Android phones, Meta Quest headsets and web browsers are all platforms.

Each one changes the game. It sets the screen the art is seen on, how many frames per second the game must deliver, how much memory it can fill, how much power it can burn before the device gets hot or the battery runs down, and which checks the game must pass before release. A studio shipping on four platforms is tuning four versions of one game.

Everyday comparison

A touring band. At an arena the stage, lights and sound system are specified weeks ahead and are the same at every arena on the tour. At a pub gig they play through whatever is there that night.

The songs stay the same. The set list, the volume and the light show change with the venue.

The problem it solves

Knowing each venue's limits in advance lets the band design one show that scales up and down, instead of a show that only works in an arena.

Games do the same with budgets set per platform, settings that scale, and a separate build for each target.

Why a lead cares

Platform choices set the art budget, the technical plan and the schedule. The weakest target you commit to usually decides how much memory every level gets. Each console adds a certification step to the release plan, and every PC game needs a minimum specification someone has actually tested. Make these decisions at the start of the project; discovering them at the end is expensive.

3 How it works

Seven ideas explain most of what platforms do to a game. The hardware words used here, such as GPU and VRAM, are covered properly in 3.5 CPU, GPU and graphics APIs.

2 Fixed and variable hardware

On fixed hardware every player owns the same machine. The studio can tune each scene until it hits its frame rate on that machine, and know that every player gets the same result. Studios build and test on a devkit, a development version of the console that the platform holder supplies under a confidential contract.

Fixed no longer means single. Xbox Series X and Series S are two targets with 16 GB and 10 GB of memory. PS5 has a Pro model. Switch 2 runs its GPU and memory slower in handheld mode than when docked. Each of these is its own fixed target, tuned and tested separately.

On variable hardware players own very different machines. That is why PC games publish a minimum specification, the least powerful hardware the game promises to run on, and a recommended specification for playing it as intended. Steam store pages show both. Where you set the minimum decides who can buy the game.

3 Memory: shared or split

On most PCs the CPU has its system RAM and the graphics card has its own VRAM, and data the GPU needs is copied across. Consoles, Switch 2, phones and headsets use unified memory: one pool that the CPU and GPU share. Textures, meshes, game logic and audio all come out of the same budget, and the operating system takes its share first.

The details matter. Xbox Series X splits its 16 GB into 10 GB at 560 GB/s and 6 GB at 336 GB/s, so what sits where affects speed. Digital Foundry reports that Switch 2 keeps 3 GB of its 12 GB for the system, leaving 9 GB for the game. A game that fits comfortably on a PS5 may not fit on a Series S at all.

4 Power, heat and throttling

Every device has a power budget: how much electricity it can draw and how much heat it can get rid of. NVIDIA rates its current desktop graphics cards at 130 W to 575 W for the card alone. Sony measures a PS5 at about 197 to 219 W while playing PS5 games, depending on the model, and Microsoft's telemetry puts the average Xbox Series X game at 122 W and Series S at 61 W. A phone or a headset runs from a battery in a case held in a hand or worn on the head.

When a chip gets too hot, the system lowers its clock speeds to cool it. That is thermal throttling. Android's documentation puts it plainly: devices can maintain high performance only for a limited time before they are throttled. Meta's headsets lower their CPU and GPU clock limits as they heat up. A phone game can hold its frame rate in a five-minute test and lose it twenty minutes into real play.

The answer is to run at a level the device can sustain. Android's thermal API reports thermal headroom, a forecast of how close the device is to throttling, so the game can lower resolution, frame rate or effects before it gets there. Android's guidance is to test sessions of 15 minutes or more. Apple's iOS reports a thermal state to apps for the same purpose.

5 VR: frame rate as a requirement

In VR a missed frame is felt as well as seen: the world should stay still when the head moves. Meta's store requirements for Quest apps set a display refresh rate of at least 72 Hz and a rendering rate of at least 60 frames per second, outside loading screens. At 72 Hz each frame has 13.9 ms.

Headsets soften missed frames with reprojection. When a new frame is late, the system shows the previous one again, turned to match where the head now points. Meta calls this TimeWarp. It corrects head rotation but not head movement through space, and moving objects judder. Application SpaceWarp goes further: the app renders at half the refresh rate, for example 36 frames per second on a 72 Hz display, and also supplies motion vectors and depth; the system builds the frames in between. Meta's rules allow that half rate for apps that provide the motion vectors.

6 Certification

A platform holder is the company that controls a closed platform and its store, such as Sony, Microsoft, Nintendo or Meta. Before a game can go on sale on a console, it passes certification: the platform holder tests a submitted build against its published requirements, and a build that fails goes back to the studio to fix and resubmit.

Each platform holder has its own name for the list. Sony's is the TRC (Technical Requirements Checklist). Microsoft's was the TCR (Technical Certification Requirements) and is now the Xbox Requirements, numbered XR-001 onwards. Nintendo's process is called Lotcheck. Meta checks Quest apps against its Virtual Reality Checks, which include the performance rule in step 5.

The requirements check how the game behaves as a guest on the platform. Microsoft's public list includes title stability, staying stable through suspend, sleep and hibernate, official naming standards, losing the connection to Xbox services, player data and privacy, user-generated content, and save games that stay compatible across content updates. Most platform documentation is under NDA and changes between versions, so certification belongs in the production plan: someone owns compliance, QA tests against the list before submission, and the schedule allows for a failed submission. Updates and downloadable content go through it as well.

7 Scalability: one game, many settings

Scalability is how one game adapts to all of this: the same content, with settings that trade visual quality for speed and memory, grouped into presets per platform and per device tier. Engines expose it as quality levels with per-platform overrides on top (section 6 compares them).

On consoles the studio chooses the settings for each model and mode. On PC the player chooses, and the game should start from a sensible preset by detecting the hardware. On phones, engines sort devices into tiers: Unreal's example configuration for Android groups devices into memory buckets from 3 GB to 8 GB. Lowering the internal resolution and upscaling is usually the strongest single lever, and chapter 9.3 covers it.

4 What it costs

Every platform you add costs time, money and attention. The hardest cost to see is time per frame: the faster a display refreshes, the less time the game has to draw each frame. Chapter 11.1 turns these into a frame budget.

Testing grows by multiplication. A PC game with 4 presets, tested at 3 resolutions on GPUs from 3 vendors, already has 4 × 3 × 3 = 36 combinations, before counting drivers and memory sizes. PS5, PS5 Pro, Series X, Series S and Switch 2 docked and handheld add six fixed configurations, each with its own certification pass. Getting onto a console at all takes time: Godot's documentation notes that a platform holder's acceptance process can take several months, and devkit prices are confidential.

What makes the cost grow

  • More platforms and modelsEach target, down to Series S and Switch 2 handheld, adds builds, a test pass and, on consoles, certification for launch and every patch.
  • The lowest targetThe smallest memory and slowest GPU you commit to cap what every level can contain.
  • Phones and PCsWide hardware ranges need device tiers, auto-detected presets and a lab of test devices.
  • VRA hard frame-rate floor, comfort testing and two eyes to render in every frame.

In proportion

Adding a platform late is the expensive way. A game designed for 16 GB and a 60 fps PS5 can need content cuts as well as settings changes to reach Series S or Switch 2 handheld. Decide the lowest target before content production starts, and give it a test device from the first month.

5 How it breaks

Platform problems tend to surface late, on the one target nobody tested enough. These six cover most of them.

Crashes on the smallest console

Cause
Content was built and tested on the largest target, then shipped to one with less memory, such as Series S or Switch 2.
Ask for
A memory budget per level for the smallest target, and a nightly test that loads every level on that hardware and reports peak memory.

Smooth at first, slow later

Cause
Thermal throttling on a phone or headset. Short tests run before the device heats up.
Ask for
Tests of 15 minutes or more on real devices, thermal headroom logged next to frame times, and a quality level the device can sustain.

Failed certification

Cause
Platform behaviour was left to the end: suspend and resume, a lost network, a disconnected controller, the wrong name for the console or its buttons.
Ask for
An owner for compliance from pre-production, a pre-check against the platform's list on every milestone build, and time in the schedule for a resubmission.

Judder in VR

Cause
Frames miss the refresh, so the headset shows old frames turned to follow the head. Under SpaceWarp, objects without motion vectors stutter.
Ask for
GPU time per frame measured on the headset against 13.9 ms at 72 Hz, and motion vectors for every moving object if SpaceWarp is used.

Stutter on minimum-spec PCs

Cause
The minimum spec was chosen on paper and never tested, or the default preset needs more VRAM than the card has, so textures stream in and out.
Ask for
A real minimum-spec machine in QA, a preset picked by detecting the hardware, and VRAM use per preset measured against the cards you support.

Web build fails on phones

Cause
The build's memory grows past what a mobile browser will give one tab. Unity's documentation warns that growing the heap can crash if the browser cannot find a large enough block.
Ask for
Peak memory measured in mobile browsers, an initial heap sized to typical use, and a target device list for the web build.

All six have the same root: a target that was not in the daily test loop. The cheapest fix is a device for every target on someone's desk, and builds for every target from the start of production.

7 Say it to your AI

Platform briefs work when they state the target's real limits as numbers, ask for the reasoning behind each trade-off, and ask for a way to measure on the device.

BriefUse when planning settings for a new platform
Game: third-person action game in Unreal Engine 5.8, shipping on PC and PS5 at 60 fps. We are porting to Nintendo Switch 2, docked and handheld.

Switch 2 facts to work from: 12 GB of memory, about 9 GB available to games; memory bandwidth about 102 GB/s docked and 68 GB/s handheld; 1080p screen with VRR up to 120 Hz; up to 4K at 60 fps on a TV.

1. Propose a render resolution and frame-rate target for docked and for handheld, with reasons.
2. Map our scalability groups (view distance, shadows, textures, effects, post-processing) to a docked device profile and a handheld one, and say what each change will look like on screen.
3. Compare our current peak memory per level with a 9 GB budget and list the biggest savings.
4. List the tests we need, including switching between docked and handheld mid-game and a 30-minute handheld session.

Mark any number you are unsure of instead of guessing.
  • Real limits in numbers stop advice written for a PC.
  • Asking what each change looks like keeps art direction in the decision.
  • Mode switching and long sessions are where handheld ports fail.
Bug reportUse when a mobile game slows down during a session
Android build, Unity 6.6, URP. On our mid-range test phone the game holds 60 fps for about 8 minutes, then falls to 35 to 40 fps and stays there. On a high-end phone it holds 60 fps for 25 minutes. CPU and GPU frame times rise together after the drop.

First confirm whether this is thermal throttling: log Android's thermal headroom (PowerManager.getThermalHeadroom, no more than once every 10 seconds) and thermal status next to our frame times. If it is, propose a sustainable quality level for this device tier and an adaptive scheme that lowers render scale, then shadow quality, as headroom falls, with hysteresis so it does not flip back and forth. Show how we test it over a 30-minute session.
  • A drop after minutes, on one device, with CPU and GPU rising together, points at heat.
  • Naming the API and its 10-second limit avoids a broken measurement.
  • Measuring first prevents a fix for the wrong problem.

Questions to ask when reviewing platform work

  1. Which platform and model is the lowest target, and what are its memory and frame-time budgets?Everything else scales down to it.
  2. Was this measured on the device, in a session of 15 minutes or more?Editor runs and short tests hide memory and heat limits.
  3. What does this change cost on each target, including the ones you did not test?A saving on PC can be a loss on a tile-based mobile GPU.
  4. Which certification requirements could this touch?Saves, suspend and resume, connectivity and accounts all have explicit requirements.
  5. How does the game choose its starting settings on a new PC or phone?The starting preset is the game every player sees first.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why does a PC game need a settings menu and a minimum specification when a PS5 game can ship with neither?

    Show answer

    PC is variable hardware: Steam players' GPUs range from 4 GB of VRAM or less (about 15%) to 24 GB and beyond, so the game must scale and say what it needs. Every PS5 is the same machine, which the studio tunes for directly.

  2. Your phone game runs at 60 fps in a five-minute test, but players say it slows down after a while. What is the likely cause, and how do you confirm it?

    Show answer

    Thermal throttling. Run sessions of 15 minutes or more on the real device and log thermal headroom and status alongside frame times. If the drop follows the heat, choose a quality level the device can sustain and scale down as headroom falls.

  3. Your Quest 3 game drops to 50 fps in busy scenes. Does it meet Meta's performance requirement, and what are your options?

    Show answer

    No: Meta requires a rendering rate of at least 60 fps outside loading screens and fades. Either cut the cost of those scenes, or use Application SpaceWarp, which Meta allows at half the refresh rate (36 fps at 72 Hz) if the app supplies motion vectors.
