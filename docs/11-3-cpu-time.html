11.3 Where CPU time goes

When a CPU thread sets the frame time, the work is spread over thousands of function calls, most of them written by the game team. This chapter shows how to read a flame chart, where the game thread and the render thread usually spend their milliseconds, why spreading work over cores has a floor, how allocations turn into garbage collection spikes, and how ticking less and laying data out better win time back.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A frame is CPU-bound when the game thread or the render thread takes longer than the GPU (11.1). Lowering the resolution then does nothing, and the time has to come out of the code those threads run. Unlike GPU time, which goes to a few dozen passes, CPU time goes to thousands of calls: every character's update, every physics step, every animation, every allocation, every draw call recorded.

The tool for finding it is a timeline of nested calls, the flame chart. The first job is to find the bars that matter, then decide whether to do that work less often, do it on another core, or make each piece cheaper. Behind all three sits memory: how much the game allocates each frame, and how its data is laid out for the CPU's caches.

An everyday comparison

An itemised phone bill. The total says the month cost too much; the itemised list says it was one long call abroad, or three hundred short ones, or a subscription that renews every week. Each needs a different fix: stop the one call, bundle the short ones, cancel the subscription.

The problem it solves

"The game thread is slow" leads nowhere. A flame chart turns it into named calls with times and counts, so a team can see whether the cost is one expensive system, a cheap thing done thousands of times, a pause that comes back every few seconds, or a thread waiting for someone else.

Why a lead cares

CPU cost grows with design decisions: how many characters are alive, how often they think, how much the level streams, how many objects spawn per second. A lead who asks for a flame chart of the worst moment, with self times and call counts, can tell a design problem from an engineering one, and can make sure the fix lands on the thread that actually limits the frame.

3 How it works

The steps follow the frame: reading the chart, the game thread's own work, the render thread's draw calls, spreading work over cores, allocation and garbage collection, ticking only what matters, and the memory layout underneath it all.

1 Reading a flame chart

Every engine's profiler can show the game thread as a flame chart, often called a timeline view (11.5 covers the tools). Read it in three passes. First find the frame you care about, usually the slowest in a run, on the frame-time graph. Then scan for wide bars and follow each one down until the bars beneath it stop covering it: that is where the self time is. Finally look for combs, rows of narrow bars with the same name, and count them.

Two habits avoid wrong conclusions. Rank functions by self time; a parent always looks expensive, because its inclusive time contains all of its children. And note the waits: Unity's CPU Usage module, for example, shows idle samples and "Wait for" samples when a thread is waiting for another thread or a job to finish. A wait is a symptom on one thread of a cost on another.

2 The game thread: ticks, scripts, animation and physics

Most of the game thread's time goes to the update of every object that needs one: characters, AI controllers, projectiles, pickups, triggers, cameras. Each is usually cheap. The cost is the count. Unreal's stat game reports how long the gameplay ticks take, and in the picture 60 enemy ticks of 0.06 ms each cost more than the most expensive single gameplay call, perception at 2.7 ms. Code in a scripting layer such as Blueprints or GDScript runs slower than engine code; Godot's CPU optimisation guide suggests moving heavy calculations to a faster language.

The animation system evaluates every visible character's animation graph and pose each frame, so its cost follows the number of characters and the complexity of their graphs. The physics engine often runs on a fixed timestep, as it does by default in Unity and Godot, so after a slow frame it runs two or more steps to catch up, as in the picture, which makes the next frame slower too (2.2). Physics cost grows with the number of bodies that are awake and touching; a pile of debris that never goes to sleep costs every frame.

3 The render thread: cost per draw

The render thread turns what the game thread produced into commands for the GPU. Its biggest cost is usually one draw call after another: each needs its state checked, its constants written and its command recorded. 4.3 used an illustrative 5 µs per draw, which makes 2,000 draws 10 ms. Every shadow cascade draws its casters again, so shadows can double or triple the count.

4 Spreading the work: jobs and the critical path

A job system (2.2) splits work such as animation, culling and physics into small jobs that run on every core. It cannot split everything. Gameplay code that reads and changes the shared world usually stays on the game thread, and some work must wait for other work's results. The critical path is the longest chain of work that has to happen in order. However many cores there are, the frame cannot finish sooner than that chain.

5 Allocation and garbage collection

Languages such as C# hand memory out on request and reclaim it later with garbage collection: the runtime finds the objects nothing refers to any more and frees them. Unity's scripts work this way, and so does Unreal's UObject system. The cost arrives later than the allocation that caused it. A per-frame allocation, such as a string built every frame for the HUD, a temporary list per enemy or a closure passed to a sort, adds a little to the heap every frame until the collector has to run.

A collector that does all its work at once stops the thread. Unity's documentation calls the result a GC spike, because it appears as a large spike in the Profiler's frame-time graph, and says a stop-the-world collection can last hundreds of milliseconds depending on the number of allocations and the platform. Unity's collector is the Boehm-Demers-Weiser collector and does not compact the heap.

Incremental garbage collection splits the collector's work into small slices across frames. It is Unity's default. The documentation notes that it spreads the work out without making collection any faster, and that it adds write barriers that cost a little whenever references change. Unreal offers an experimental incremental reachability analysis, turned on with gc.AllowIncrementalReachability, and its documentation says plain reachability analysis can introduce visible gameplay hitches. Godot's GDScript uses reference counting instead of a garbage collector, while its C# support uses the .NET collector.

The cure is to allocate less. Unity's CPU Usage module says to keep the GC.Alloc value at zero while the application runs. The usual technique is object pooling: keep a set of projectiles, effects or list buffers alive and reuse them, instead of creating and destroying them. Unity provides UnityEngine.Pool, and Godot's CPU optimisation guide names pooling as the common workaround for C# collection pauses.

6 Tick what matters

Many games update every character, every frame, whether the player can see it or not. Most of that work changes nothing the player notices. Significance is a score for how much an object matters right now, from its distance, its visibility or its role, which the game uses to decide how often and how carefully to update it. It is level of detail for logic.

Updating a quarter of the middle tier on each frame, instead of all of it every fourth frame, is time-slicing: spreading periodic work over several frames under a per-frame limit, so no frame pays for all of it. The engines provide pieces of this. Unreal's Significance Manager is a framework for scoring objects; its documentation says it does not improve performance by itself, and the game's code decides what each score changes. Unreal's Animation Budget Allocator caps skeletal mesh animation work at a fixed number of milliseconds, 1.0 by default, and updates less significant meshes less often. Unity's CullingGroup API reports distance bands and visibility so scripts can switch behaviour, and Godot's VisibleOnScreenEnabler3D enables a node only while its box is on screen.

7 Data layout and cache misses

Some frames have no hot spot at all: every bar is a little slow. Often the cause is memory. 2.3 showed that the CPU fetches memory in 64-byte cache lines and that a cache miss costs around 200 times a hit. Code that walks over objects scattered in memory, or reads one field from large structs, spends most of its time waiting for lines it barely uses. Godot's CPU optimisation guide makes the same point: random accesses cause many cache misses, while reading memory in a linear fashion lets the cache work optimally.

This is why entity component systems store components in packed arrays, and why engines rewrite hot systems such as transforms, animation and crowds in that style (2.3). For everyday gameplay code the advice is smaller: keep the data a loop reads together, avoid chasing pointers inside hot loops, and process many similar things in one pass instead of one at a time in many places.

Worked example. An action game at 60 fps is game-thread-bound at 18.5 ms with 200 enemies, each updating in 20 µs: 4.0 ms of the frame. Significance tiers bring that to 0.85 ms, saving 3.15 ms. The frame is now 15.4 ms, inside the 16.7 ms budget with 1.3 ms to spare, though short of the 10% headroom from 11.1. Removing 60 KB a frame of allocations then takes out the GC spike every few seconds, which barely moved the average and set the 1% low. Neither fix touched the GPU, and neither needed a faster CPU.

  • Things aliveCharacters, projectiles, physics bodies and triggers each add their update, every frame.
  • How often they thinkEvery-frame ticks, perception checks and pathfinding requests multiply by the frame rate.
  • AllocationsBytes allocated per frame set how often the collector runs and how much work each run has.
  • Draw calls and cascadesEach draw costs the render thread; each shadow cascade draws its casters again.
  • Serial workCode that cannot leave the game thread sets the floor however many cores there are.
  • Data layoutScattered objects and large structs turn every loop into cache misses.

The usual trade

Teams set a budget for the number of active characters and effects per platform, give distant ones cheaper updates, pool everything that spawns often, and treat any steady per-frame allocation as a bug. Hot systems that must scale, such as crowds, projectiles and animation, move into jobs and packed data, while one-off gameplay logic stays simple and readable. Both choices are made with a flame chart of the worst moment open.

5 How it breaks

CPU time goes wrong when counts grow, when work that should be spread out happens at once, or when the work lands on one thread while the others wait.

A stutter every few seconds

Cause
Garbage collection spikes fed by per-frame allocations: strings, temporary lists, boxing, closures, objects spawned and destroyed.
Ask for
GC.Alloc per frame in steady play with call stacks for the top allocators, the collector mode, and a pooling plan for anything that spawns often.

Frame time grows with every character, seen or not

Cause
Everything ticks every frame at full detail, including characters far away or behind walls.
Ask for
Update time per character type, the count by distance, and significance tiers with update rates and what each tier skips.

Render thread over budget, GPU idle

Cause
Too many draw calls, often doubled by shadow cascades, or per-draw state changes the batcher cannot merge.
Ask for
Draw counts per pass, the render thread's flame chart, and the batching, instancing and shadow culling options with their savings (4.3).

Eight cores, one of them full

Cause
The work is serial: gameplay, AI or animation runs on the game thread instead of in jobs, or jobs wait on one long job.
Ask for
The timeline of all threads for the worst frame, the critical path through it, and which systems could move to jobs.

No hot spot, everything a little slow

Cause
Death by a thousand calls: scattered data and cache misses, scripting overhead, or thousands of tiny updates with no single culprit.
Ask for
Self time grouped by system and by call count, and a data-layout review of the three hottest loops.

Physics time jumps when things pile up

Cause
Many bodies awake and touching in one island, plus catch-up steps after slow frames, so each step costs more and more steps run.
Ask for
Active body counts, steps per frame, the step cap, and sleep settings or limits on debris.

7 Say it to your AI

CPU briefs go well when they come with a capture of the worst moment and ask for self time, call counts and allocations before any rewrite.

DiagnosisUse when the game thread is over budget
Unity 6.6, URP, Nintendo Switch 2, 30 fps target. A Profiler capture of 300 frames from the arena fight with 80 enemies is attached. The main thread averages 38 ms and spikes every few seconds.

1. List the ten markers with the highest self time on the main thread, with call counts per frame.
2. For every marker called more than 50 times a frame, say what it is and whether it could run less often or in a job.
3. Report GC.Alloc per frame in steady play and the five call stacks that allocate most.
4. Propose changes ranked by expected saving, each with its risk to gameplay. Change nothing yet.
  • Self time and call counts find both wide bars and combs.
  • Asking for allocations separately catches the spikes the averages hide.
  • Ranking by saving with a risk keeps the cheapest safe fix first.
DesignUse when a world needs many more characters
Unreal Engine 5.8, PS5 and Xbox Series S, 60 fps. The open-world town needs 400 AI townsfolk; today 150 cost 6 ms of game thread.

1. Propose significance tiers using the Significance Manager: the score, the tiers and the distances or rules behind them.
2. For each tier give the tick interval, perception rate, animation update rate under the Animation Budget Allocator, and what is switched off.
3. List the gameplay events that must still reach a low-tier character immediately, and how.
4. Say how we measure it: stat game and an Unreal Insights capture before and after, in the busiest square.
  • Tiers with explicit rates make the trade between cost and behaviour reviewable.
  • Listing must-react events protects gameplay from the optimisation.
  • Naming the measurement makes the saving provable on the target.

Questions to ask when reviewing CPU work

  1. Which thread limits, and which markers have the most self time?Inclusive time points at containers, self time at the cost.
  2. How many times a frame does the hot call run?Combs need fewer calls; wide bars need cheaper ones.
  3. How many bytes are allocated per frame in steady play?Every byte feeds the next collection spike.
  4. What is on the critical path, and what waits for it?Moving work off the critical path is the only way more cores help.
  5. Does anything update that the player cannot see or affect?The cheapest update is the one that does not run.

8 Check yourself

  1. A flame chart shows a function called 80 times a frame at 0.05 ms each, and another called once at 2.5 ms. Which costs more, and what kind of fix suits each?

    Show answer

    The first: 80 × 0.05 = 4.0 ms against 2.5 ms. The comb needs fewer calls, through ticking less often, significance tiers or processing all 80 in one batched system. The single wide call needs that call to be cheaper or moved to a job.

  2. A Unity project allocates 60 KB a frame and stutters every few seconds, although incremental collection is on. Why can it still stutter, and what is the real fix?

    Show answer

    Incremental mode spreads the collector's work over frames but does not reduce it, and with a high allocation rate the work can still pile into frames already near the budget. The fix is to stop the steady allocations: cache strings and buffers, pool spawned objects and remove boxing and closures from hot paths, aiming for zero GC.Alloc in steady play.

  3. A frame has 4 ms of gameplay that must run first on the game thread, then 9 ms of animation and culling that splits into small jobs. What is the best frame time on 8 cores, and on 16?

    Show answer

    On 8 cores, 4 + 9 ÷ 8 ≈ 5.1 ms. On 16, 4 + 9 ÷ 16 ≈ 4.6 ms. Doubling the cores saves half a millisecond because the 4 ms of gameplay is the critical path; shortening it is the bigger win.
