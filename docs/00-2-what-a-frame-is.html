0.2 What a frame is

A game is a flip-book drawn on the fly: it shows a new still image dozens of times a second, and each one must be finished within a fixed slice of time. This chapter is about that slice: how long it is, what fills it, and what players notice when it overflows.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A frame is one finished image. A game draws one, shows it, and straight away starts on the next, so the picture seems to move. How many it manages each second is its frame rate, measured in frames per second, or fps. Common targets are 30 and 60 fps, and higher on fast PC monitors and VR headsets.

Turn the number around and you get the one that matters to the people building the game: frame time, how long each frame takes. At 60 fps it is 1000 ÷ 60, about 16.7 milliseconds, often quoted as 16.6. Everything the game does for that frame, from physics to shadows, has to share that time. The share each system gets is its slice of the frame budget.

Everyday comparison

A railway station where a train leaves every few minutes, exactly on time. Each train carries whatever was loaded before it left. A carriage that is not ready does not hold the train back; it waits for the next one, and the platform stays empty in the meantime.

The display is the timetable. Each screen refresh is a departure, and each frame is a carriage that must be loaded before it.

The problem it solves

A fixed rhythm makes motion look smooth. Objects move the same distance between every image, and the player's input shows up at a steady pace.

The price is a hard deadline. Work that runs even slightly late misses its train and waits for the next one.

Why a lead cares

The frame-rate target is a product decision that sets every other budget. Going from 30 to 60 fps halves the time every system gets. Every new effect is paid for in milliseconds out of the same frame, so each request for one is a question about what else gives up time. And players notice the worst frames more than the average: a game that averages 60 fps but freezes for a tenth of a second every few seconds feels rough, whatever its counter says.

3 How it works

Seven steps, from a single image to the rhythm players feel.

1 Frames sample motion

Nothing on screen actually moves. Each frame is a still picture of where things are at one instant. Between frames the world moves on, and the next picture shows it further along. More frames per second means smaller jumps between pictures, which reads as smoother motion, and the game can show the result of the player's input sooner.

2 Milliseconds add up; frame rates do not

Frame rate is how players and store pages talk. Milliseconds are how teams work, for two reasons. The first is that work adds up in milliseconds: 3 ms of physics plus 4 ms of animation is 7 ms. Said as frame rates, those are 333 and 250 fps, and no simple sum turns them into the 143 fps they allow together.

The second is that frame rates hide the size of a change. Dropping from 60 to 30 fps adds 16.7 ms to every frame. Dropping from 120 to 60 fps sounds just as bad and adds 8.3 ms. From 240 to 120 adds 4.2 ms. The higher the frame rate, the less time each frame per second is worth.

Maths Converting between the two

frame time in ms = 1000 ÷ frames per second

frames per second = 1000 ÷ frame time in ms

30 fps is 33.3 ms, 60 fps is 16.7 ms, 90 fps is 11.1 ms, 120 fps is 8.3 ms, 144 fps is 6.9 ms and 240 fps is 4.2 ms.

To average a run, divide the number of frames by the seconds it took, which is the same as 1000 ÷ the average frame time. Averaging fps readings directly gives a number that is too high whenever frame times vary.

3 The game loop

Every game, in every engine, runs the same loop. The game loop reads input, updates the world by the time that has passed, renders the frame and presents it to the display, then goes round again. One trip round the loop is one frame.

The update step uses the frame time. If a car drives at 20 metres per second and the last frame took 16.7 ms, the game moves it 0.33 metres. Scaling every movement by the frame time keeps the game running at the same speed whatever the frame rate. Engines hand this number to game code as the delta time. Physics usually runs on a fixed step of its own, for example 60 times a second, catching up or waiting as frames come and go; 2.2 The game loop and threads covers fixed steps and how engines split the loop across threads.

4 The budget, and what happens when it shrinks

At a given target, every frame gets the same allowance: 33.3 ms at 30 fps, 16.7 ms at 60, 11.1 ms at 90, 8.3 ms at 120. Teams divide that allowance between systems and call the result the frame budget. Raising the target cuts the time for everything at once.

Simplified

The ruler assumes v-sync on a display that refreshes at the target rate, and a CPU and GPU that overlap perfectly on different frames. Real engines also queue frames ahead, and displays with a variable refresh rate show each frame as soon as it is ready. 3.1 Pixels and displays covers displays, and 11.1 Frame budgets covers real budgets.

5 CPU and GPU, side by side

As 0.1 The whole journey showed, the CPU and the GPU work on different frames at the same time. So their times do not add up. Each processor gets the whole budget, and the slower of the two sets the pace. Godot's documentation puts it plainly: your total frame time is determined by the slower of the two.

A game whose GPU is the slower one is called GPU-bound: lowering the resolution speeds it up, and trimming game logic does nothing. A CPU-bound game is the reverse. Finding out which one you have is the first step of any performance work, and 11.1 Frame budgets shows how.

6 The display's clock and missed frames

The display keeps its own time. A 60 Hz screen shows a new image every 16.7 ms whether or not the game has one ready. With v-sync on, the game swaps in its finished image only at that moment, so the picture never changes halfway through a refresh; 3.1 Pixels and displays covers v-sync and what happens without it.

If a frame is not ready in time, the screen shows the previous image again. A frame that needs 25 ms instead of 16.7 misses its refresh by about 8 ms and appears at the next one, a whole refresh later, while the image before it stays up for 33.3 ms. Players see a stall followed by a jump. A frame, or a short run of frames, that takes far longer than its neighbours is a hitch.

7 Steady beats fast: frame pacing

Players notice uneven frames more than slow ones. A game that shows every image for exactly 33.3 ms runs at a steady 30 fps and looks smooth. A game that shows its images for 50, 16.7 and 33.3 ms in turn also averages 30 fps, and it stutters, because objects jump different distances from one image to the next. Android's developer documentation describes this pattern for a 30 fps game on a 60 Hz phone: frame times of 49, 16 and 33 ms when frames are released at the wrong moments.

Keeping every frame the same length is called frame pacing. It is why many games cap their frame rate below what the hardware could reach: a steady 30 can feel better than a 45 that wanders. How the cap is done matters too. Unity's documentation warns that capping with its software frame-rate target and no v-sync is subject to microstuttering.

In proportion

Doubling a frame-rate target halves the time for everything at once: every system and every pass. It is one of the most expensive decisions a project makes, and the easiest one to make late. Decide it early for each platform, and derive every other budget from it in milliseconds.

5 How it breaks

Frame-time problems are easiest to diagnose from a frame-time graph. These five shapes cover most of what you will see.

Regular spikes every few seconds

Cause
Work that runs now and then lands in a single frame: memory clean-up by a scripting language's garbage collector, an autosave, or streaming in the next part of the level.
Ask for
A profiler capture across several spikes, a list of what ran in each spike frame, and a plan to spread that work over many frames.

A long freeze when entering a new area

Cause
The next area is loaded on the game's main thread instead of in the background, so one frame waits for the disk.
Ask for
Background loading for that area, and a check that nothing on the main thread waits for it.

A steady counter, but jerky motion

Cause
Frames are released at uneven intervals. Common causes are a frame-rate cap without v-sync, or a 30 fps game on a 60 Hz screen that shows some images for one refresh and others for three.
Ask for
A frame-time graph instead of an fps counter, and the game's v-sync and frame-cap settings.

A horizontal tear across the image

Cause
With v-sync off, the display switches to a new image partway down the screen, so the top and bottom show different frames. 3.1 Pixels and displays explains why.
Ask for
V-sync, or variable refresh rate where the display supports it, and a check of which one the platform requires.

The game runs faster at higher frame rates

Cause
Movement or timers are counted per frame instead of being scaled by frame time, so a 144 fps machine runs the game almost five times as fast as a 30 fps one.
Ask for
Every movement and timer scaled by the engine's delta time, and a test of the game at both a low and a high frame rate.

A frame-time graph tells you which frames are slow. To see what ran inside one of them, capture it with the engine's profiler or a frame capture tool.

6 In the engines

All three engines measure frames in milliseconds, give game code the frame time, and let you cap the frame rate. The names differ.

Frame time in game code Actors override Tick(float DeltaSeconds), called every frame, where DeltaSeconds is the game time the last frame took. Ticking must be switched on per actor. Update() runs once per frame, with Time.deltaTime as the time since the last frame. Names and defaults checked against the documentation for Unreal Engine 5.7, Unity 6.6 and Godot 4.7 in November 2026. Engines rename settings between versions; if one is missing, search for it in your version's documentation.

Measure outside the editor

Unity's statistics in the editor include time spent updating the editor's own windows, and every engine's editor does extra work. Judge frame times on a build running on the target hardware.

7 Say it to your AI

Good briefs about frame rate speak in milliseconds, separate average speed from hitches, and ask for a way to measure. Adapt these.

Bug reportUse when the game stutters but the counter looks fine
Unity 6.6, URP, Windows build. Target 60 fps with v-sync.
The fps counter shows 58 to 60, but every 6 to 8 seconds the game freezes for roughly 100 to 150 ms. It is worst in the market level.

I have attached a Profiler capture that covers three freezes. For each freeze, list what ran in the slow frame and how long it took, and name the system responsible. Then propose fixes in order of effort, and tell me how to confirm each one in the Profiler. Do not lower quality settings to hide the problem.
  • The counter value and the freeze pattern point to a hitch rather than a low frame rate.
  • Several captured freezes let it look for the common cause.
  • The last line stops it treating a hitch as a general slowdown.
BriefUse when setting frame budgets for a project
Third-person action game, Unreal Engine 5.7, for PC and PlayStation 5. Targets: 60 fps on PlayStation 5; 60 or 120 fps on PC, chosen in settings.

Draft a frame budget in milliseconds for each target: game thread, render thread and GPU, with the GPU split into shadows, geometry, lighting, effects, post-processing and UI. Keep 10% headroom. State your assumptions, mark which numbers are guesses, and list the stat commands and Unreal Insights views we would use to check each line.
  • Platforms and targets fix the size of each budget.
  • Milliseconds per system make the budget something you can measure.
  • Marked guesses show where you need real measurements before committing.

Questions to ask when reviewing work on frame rate

  1. What frame rate and hardware was this measured on?A change that fits at 30 fps may not fit at 60, or on the slowest target.
  2. How many milliseconds does it add, on the CPU and on the GPU?Changes in fps hide the size of the cost.
  3. Does any of this work happen in a single frame, such as loading or building something?One-off work causes hitches even when the average is fine.
  4. Is every movement and timer scaled by the frame time?Otherwise the game's speed changes with the frame rate.
  5. Was it judged from a frame-time graph or only from an fps counter?Counters average away hitches and uneven pacing.

8 Check yourself

  1. One game drops from 60 to 45 fps in a busy scene. Another drops from 120 to 90. Which one lost more time per frame?

    Show answer

    The first. 60 to 45 fps is 16.7 to 22.2 ms, 5.6 ms more per frame. 120 to 90 fps is 8.3 to 11.1 ms, 2.8 ms more.

  2. The CPU needs 9 ms per frame and the GPU needs 14 ms. Without a cap, roughly what frame rate do you get, and what would speed it up?

    Show answer

    About 71 fps, set by the GPU's 14 ms. Making the GPU's work cheaper, for example by lowering the resolution, helps. Speeding up the CPU does not, because it is already waiting.

  3. The fps counter reads a steady 60, yet testers report stutter. What do you look at?

    Show answer

    A frame-time graph. Single long frames and uneven pacing hide inside an average. Then capture one of the slow frames in the profiler to see what ran in it.
