7.4 Shadow maps

A shadow map is a picture of the scene's depth taken from a light. Every lit pixel then asks it one question: is anything nearer the light than I am? The idea is simple and fast, and almost all of its well-known failures come down to two settings, bias and resolution.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A point is in shadow when something stands between it and the light. Testing that directly for every pixel means tracing a line to the light through all the scene's geometry, which is what ray tracing does. A shadow map answers the same question with the tools a GPU is best at: it renders the scene once from the light, keeping only depth, and then looks the answer up.

Anything the light can see is lit; anything hidden behind something else, from the light's point of view, is in shadow. The shadow map is the record of what the light can see, and it is used by every pixel the light reaches.

Everyday comparison

Stand where the lamp is and take a photograph with a camera that records distance instead of colour. To know whether a spot on the floor is lit, find it in the photograph. If the photograph shows something nearer at that spot, a chair perhaps, the floor there is in the chair's shadow.

The catch is that the photograph has a limited number of pixels, and each one records a single distance for a whole patch of the room.

The problem it solves

Finding what blocks the light for millions of pixels, every frame, for objects that move.

A shadow map turns that into one extra render per light plus one texture lookup per pixel, which GPUs have done fast for decades.

Why a lead cares

Shadows ground objects in the world, and they are one of the largest and least predictable costs in a frame: every shadow-casting light renders the scene again. They also produce some of the most visible bugs, stripes on lit walls, objects that float, edges that crawl. Most of those come from a handful of settings artists change per light, so knowing what each setting trades lets you brief and review fixes instead of watching values get nudged at random.

3 How it works

Follow the two passes, then the problems that come from storing one depth per texel and the settings that manage them.

1 Pass one: depth from the light

The shadow pass places a camera at the light and draws every shadow caster, the objects allowed to block light, into a depth buffer. There is usually no colour and no material work, only positions, so it is cheaper per object than the main pass. The result is the shadow map: a render target holding, for each texel, the light-space depth of the first surface the light meets there, its distance along the light's direction.

The light type decides the camera. The sun's rays are parallel, so a directional light uses an orthographic projection (3.4). A spot light is a camera looking down its cone with a perspective projection. A point light shines in every direction, so it needs six 90° views, one per face of a cubemap.

2 Pass two: the shadow test

While lighting a pixel, the shader transforms the pixel's world position with the light's view and projection matrices, the same ones the shadow pass used. That gives a texel coordinate in the shadow map and the pixel's own light-space depth. The shadow test compares the two: if the pixel is further from the light than the stored depth, something nearer blocks it and the light's contribution is dropped. GPUs have comparison samplers that do the test in the texture unit.

The Lantern Courtyard's sun works exactly this way. Its shadow map is 2048 × 2048 texels of 24-bit depth, rendered from an orthographic camera fitted around the courtyard.

3 Why lit surfaces shadow themselves

Each texel stores one depth for a patch of surface, measured at the texel's centre. On a surface tilted against the light, half of every patch lies further from the light than its centre. Those points fail the test against their own surface's stored depth and come out in shadow, in stripes or moiré patterns across surfaces that should be lit. This is shadow acne. Limited depth precision adds to it, because the depth the shader computes and the depth the rasteriser stored are never exactly equal.

4 Bias, and too much of it

The fix is shadow bias: move the test a little towards the light, or the stored depths a little away from it, so a surface no longer shadows itself. Microsoft's guide to shadow maps calls this adding a depth offset.

Too much bias has the opposite problem. Points just behind a caster now pass the test as well, so the shadow starts some distance from the object that casts it and the object seems to hover. That is peter-panning, after the boy whose shadow came loose. It shows first on thin objects, such as walls, planks and legs, where the gap between the caster and what it shadows is small. Microsoft's guide recommends giving narrow objects such as walls real back faces for that reason.

5 Slope-scaled bias and normal offset

The depth change across one texel depends on how steeply a surface is tilted against the light: nothing when it faces the light, a lot at grazing angles, growing with the tangent of the angle. A constant bias that fixes grazing surfaces is far more than surfaces facing the light need, and that excess is what detaches shadows.

Slope-scaled bias adds bias in proportion to that slope, so each surface gets roughly what it needs; GPUs since Direct3D 10 can apply it in hardware while drawing the shadow map. Normal offset moves the point along its surface normal by a fraction of a texel before the lookup, which steps it out of its own texel's shadow on tilted surfaces. Engines differ in the detail: Godot's Normal Bias offsets the lookup by the normal, while Unity's Normal setting shrinks the shadow-casting surfaces along their normals.

7 Resolution and texel density

Shadow map resolution sets how much of the world each texel covers. It is the same idea as texel density for textures (6.1), measured in centimetres of world per shadow texel. A shadow edge cannot be sharper than one texel, so coarse texels give blocky, stair-stepped shadows, and bias, which is usually measured in texels, grows with them.

Two levers help. Fit the light's view as tightly as possible around what the camera can see, so no texels are wasted on empty space; Microsoft's guide also fits the near and far planes tightly, which improves depth precision. And spend texels where the camera is: Unity's URP documentation gives the example of cutting Max Distance from 40 to 10, which lets a 1024 map give better shadows near the camera than a 2048 map did. Splitting the view into several maps by distance, cascades, is the subject of 7.5 Shadows at scale.

8 PCF: soft edges from several tests

A shadow map cannot be blurred like an ordinary texture. Averaging depths invents surfaces that do not exist: a texel at 4.1 m and one at 5.8 m average to 4.95 m, where there may be nothing at all. Percentage-closer filtering, PCF, published by Reeves, Salesin and Cook in 1987, runs the test against several nearby texels and averages the answers instead, so a pixel near an edge can be partly lit.

Hardware helps: a comparison sampler with bilinear filtering already does a 2 × 2 PCF in one fetch. Engines build larger kernels from several such fetches, often in a rotated or random pattern so the steps turn into fine noise that temporal anti-aliasing then smooths. The courtyard uses 12 of those fetches on a Poisson disc rotated per pixel. More taps cost more texture reads for every shadowed pixel.

9 PCSS: softer with distance

Real shadows from lights with size are sharp where an object touches the ground and softer further away, because the soft edge, the penumbra, widens with the distance between the blocker and the surface (7.1). Percentage-closer soft shadows, PCSS, presented by Randima Fernando at NVIDIA in 2005, estimates that per pixel. It searches the shadow map near the pixel for blockers, averages their depths, works out the penumbra width from the light's size, and then runs PCF with a kernel that wide.

PCSS costs a blocker search and a variable, often large, filter for every pixel: Fernando's original sketch used 36 blocker samples and 64 filter samples. HDRP uses it as its High filtering quality and Godot for lights with a Size or Angular Distance; Unreal's virtual shadow maps soften shadows their own way (7.5).

The shadow pass costs vertex work and draw calls for every caster inside each map, regardless of screen resolution. A point light with 300 casters in range can mean up to 1,800 draws a frame before instancing or caching. Sampling costs every lit pixel a few texture reads per shadowed light: at 1080p, a 9-tap filter, HDRP's Medium quality for point and spot lights, is 2,073,600 × 9 ≈ 18.7 million comparisons per light per frame, and PCSS with Fernando's 36 + 64 samples is about 207 million.

What makes the bill grow

  • Shadow-casting lightsEach one renders the scene again; a point light six times.
  • Casters in view of the lightTriangles and draw calls in the shadow pass grow with everything inside the light's range, including objects the camera cannot see.
  • ResolutionMemory grows with the square of the map size: 4096² is four times 2048².
  • Filter tapsEvery extra PCF or PCSS sample is a texture read for every shadowed pixel.
  • Update rateRe-rendering maps every frame for lights and casters that never move wastes the whole pass; HDRP can update shadows only on enable or on demand.
  • Distance coveredA sun shadow that must reach the horizon needs cascades or virtual shadow maps (7.5).

In proportion

Unity's shadow performance guide calls real-time shadows a high performance impact and suggests low-detail meshes, or even primitives, as shadow casters where shadows are expensive. The cheapest shadow is one that is not re-rendered: decide which lights truly need dynamic shadows, which can be baked (8.2), and which small objects need not cast at all.

5 How it breaks

Shadow-map artefacts are among the most recognisable bugs in games. Each has a typical picture and a typical cause.

Stripes on lit surfaces

Cause
Shadow acne: not enough bias for the surface's angle to the light, coarse texels, or low depth precision from loosely fitted near and far planes.
Ask for
Slope-scaled bias and normal offset before more constant bias, a check of the light's depth range, and before-and-after shots at grazing sun angles.

Objects that float

Cause
Peter-panning: too much bias or normal offset, usually raised to hide acne, and worst on thin casters such as walls, legs and planks.
Ask for
The bias values per light, a smaller constant bias with slope bias added, and back faces or thickness on thin casters.

Blocky, stair-stepped shadow edges

Cause
Texels too large for the distance they cover: a small map, a light view fitted loosely, or a shadow distance set far beyond what the camera needs.
Ask for
The texel size in centimetres where the player looks, a tighter shadow distance or fit, then a resolution change if still needed, and filtering for what remains.

Shadow edges that crawl as the camera moves

Cause
The light's view is refitted every frame as the camera moves, so texel boundaries slide across the world. Microsoft's guide describes this shimmering edge effect.
Ask for
Snapping the light's view to whole texels as the camera moves, the fix that guide describes, and a test with a slow camera pan.

Light leaking along the base of walls

Cause
Normal offset or bias larger than a thin wall's thickness, or single-sided walls, so points just behind the wall pass the test.
Ask for
Smaller offsets for that light, thicker or two-sided shadow casters, and a review of interiors at the sun's lowest angle.

To diagnose, look at the shadow map itself. A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc shows each shadow pass, its draw calls and its depth image, and URP's Rendering Debugger can colour the main light's shadow cascades.

Bias units differ

Engines define bias in different units, and Godot even gives spot lights a different default from other lights. Never copy bias values between engines or light types; tune them on the target scene at the sun's lowest angle.

7 Say it to your AI

Shadow briefs need the engine and technique, the light and its settings as numbers, where and at what sun angle the problem appears, and a way to check the fix that covers both acne and floating. Adapt these.

BriefUse when tuning shadows for a level
Engine: Godot 4.7, Forward+. One DirectionalLight3D sun with a day-night cycle; Max Distance 100 m; shadow map 4096, 16-bit; Soft Low filtering. Bias 0.1, Normal Bias 2.0 (defaults).
At sunrise and sunset the terrain shows stripes, and at noon the characters' feet look detached from the ground.

1. Explain which setting causes each problem and why the sun angle matters.
2. Propose values for Bias, Normal Bias and Max Distance that remove the stripes at a 5° sun without detaching feet at noon, and say whether a different split setup would help.
3. Estimate the texel size at the camera with your settings.
4. Give a test route through the level, at three sun angles, with screenshots to compare.

  • Naming both symptoms stops a fix for one that causes the other.
  • Asking for texel size brings resolution into the answer alongside bias.

Find the cause. Check whether the light's shadow view is snapped to whole texels as the camera moves, measure the texel size on the fence at the usual camera distance, and compare fixes: shorter Max Distance, different cascade splits, higher resolution, and temporal anti-aliasing. For each, give the GPU cost on our minimum spec and a video of the same pan.
  • Crawling while panning points at how the light's view is fitted to the camera.
  • Measuring texel size on the problem object turns it into numbers.
  • A repeatable camera pan makes before and after comparable.

Questions to ask when reviewing shadow work

  1. Which lights cast dynamic shadows, and how many times does each render the scene?A point light renders six times; each extra shadowed light adds a pass.
  2. What is the shadow texel size where the player usually looks?It sets the sharpest possible edge and how much bias is needed.
  3. How was the bias chosen, and was it tested at the sun's lowest angle?Acne appears at grazing angles; floating appears on thin casters.
  4. Which objects are shadow casters that do not need to be?Small debris and distant clutter cost shadow-pass draws for no visible gain.
  5. How many filter taps does each shadowed pixel take?PCSS and large PCF kernels multiply texture reads across the screen.
  6. Do static lights re-render their shadows every frame?Caching or baking removes the whole pass for lights that never change.

8 Check yourself

  1. A pixel is 12.0 m from the sun along the light's direction. Its texel in the shadow map stores 9.5 m. Is the pixel lit?

    Show answer

    No. Something 2.5 m nearer the sun covers that texel, so the pixel is in its shadow.

  2. An artist raised a light's bias until the stripes on a wall disappeared. Now a fence's shadow starts 30 cm from the fence. What is this, and what is a better fix?

    Show answer

    Peter-panning, from too much constant bias. Lower it and add slope-scaled bias and a little normal offset, which bias tilted surfaces without pushing every shadow away; smaller texels, from a tighter fit or higher resolution, reduce the bias needed.

  3. A 2048 × 2048 sun shadow map covers 100 m around the camera. How wide is one texel, and what does that mean for shadow edges?

    Show answer

    100 m ÷ 2048 ≈ 4.9 cm. No shadow edge can be sharper than that: smaller details are lost or blocky, and PCF can only soften the steps; it cannot add detail.
