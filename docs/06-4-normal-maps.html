6.4 Normal maps and fake detail

A normal map is a texture that tells the lighting which way the surface faces at every pixel, so a flat wall lights up as if it were covered in bricks. It changes the shading, never the shape, and the other tricks in this chapter buy back a little more of the shape at a price.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

How bright a point looks depends mostly on which way it faces compared with the light. That direction is the surface normal. A mesh stores one normal per vertex, so a wall made of two triangles has four normals and looks perfectly flat.

A normal map stores a normal for every texel instead. The pixel shader reads it and lights each pixel as if the surface there faced that way. The wall stays two triangles, but the light picks out every brick edge, scratch and rivet. Artists usually make the map by baking it from a detailed sculpt.

Everyday comparison

A theatre backdrop is painted with shading so that, from the seats, a flat cloth reads as a street of buildings. A normal map is a backdrop that repaints its own shading for every light and every camera position.

Walk round to the side of the backdrop, though, and it is a flat cloth. Normal maps fail the same way: at the outline and at grazing angles.

The problem it solves

Modelling every brick of a wall, every stitch of a jacket and every pore of a face would take millions of triangles per object, and every one would be processed in every pass, every frame.

A normal map moves that detail into a texture: one extra read per pixel, a few megabytes of memory, and it can tile across every wall in the level.

Why a lead cares

Nearly every surface in a modern game has a normal map, and they are the main reason assets can be low-poly. They are also a pipeline handshake: the baking tool, the mesh's tangents and the engine's settings must agree, or lighting goes subtly wrong across thousands of assets. Getting the conventions fixed in pre-production is cheap; discovering them in a late art review is not.

3 How it works

Start with what a normal map holds, then see how lighting uses it, where it comes from, and the tricks that go further. There are eight steps.

1 What a normal map stores

A normal is a direction of length 1 with three parts, x, y and z, each between −1 and 1. A texture stores numbers from 0 to 255, so each part is moved into that range: add 1, halve it, multiply by 255. Red holds x, green holds y and blue holds z.

In the usual kind of normal map, z points straight out of the surface. A flat texel is (0, 0, 1), stored as (128, 128, 255): a light blue-purple. Tilts move red and green away from 128, but blue stays high, because a surface never faces into itself. That is why normal maps look blue-purple with red and green fringes.

2 Lighting reads it, pixel by pixel

For every pixel, the pixel shader reads the normal map at that pixel's UV coordinates, turns the texel back into a direction, and uses that direction in its lighting maths instead of the smooth normal from the vertices. Slopes facing the light get brighter, slopes facing away get darker, and the eye reads bumps.

Try it. Drag the light around the square. Turn the normal map off and the bricks become a flat print. Switch to rivets for a shinier surface. Lower the light to a grazing angle and the bumps stand out most. Leave the green channel setting for step 4.

Simplified

The interactive lights the square with one simple diffuse model and a small highlight. Real materials also use the normal for reflections and roughness, which 6.5 PBR materials covers, and real lights cast shadows, which a normal map cannot produce from its bumps.

3 Tangent space and object space

The direction straight out of the surface is different at every point of a curved mesh. So the usual normal map is written in tangent space: a small frame of reference that sits on the surface at each point. Its axes are the vertex normal, the tangent, which runs along the texture's horizontal direction, and the bitangent, which runs along its vertical direction. The shader turns each texel from that frame into world directions using the three vectors stored with, or rebuilt from, the mesh.

Because every texel is relative to the surface under it, a tangent-space map works on any shape, tiles across walls, and bends with an animated character. An object-space normal map stores directions relative to the whole model instead. It needs no tangents, but it belongs to one mesh: it cannot tile, mirror or deform, and it looks like a rainbow.

4 The green channel: two conventions

Tools disagree about which way the tangent frame's vertical axis points. In the green channel convention called OpenGL or Y+, green above 128 means the surface tilts up the texture. In the DirectX or Y− convention, it means the surface tilts down. Red and blue mean the same in both. Unity and Godot expect Y+ maps; Unreal expects Y−.

A map made for one and used in the other has its vertical tilts inverted. Lit from the side it looks fine; lit from above, the edges facing the light go dark and bumps read as dents. Go back to the interactive, set the green channel to Flipped, and move the light from the side to the top.

5 Baking from high poly to low poly

Most normal maps are baked. An artist sculpts a detailed high-poly version of the model, then builds the light low-poly mesh the game will use, the retopology step, and unwraps its UVs. The baker casts a ray from each texel's point on the low-poly surface, finds where it meets the high-poly sculpt, and writes the sculpt's normal there into the texel, in the low-poly mesh's tangent space.

Rays start from a cage, a slightly inflated copy of the low-poly mesh that encloses the sculpt, or from a maximum ray distance. Too tight and rays start inside the detail and miss it; too loose or too long and they hit the wrong part of the model.

The baker expresses every normal in the low-poly mesh's tangent frames. If the engine later builds slightly different tangents for that mesh, every texel is decoded in a slightly wrong frame, and the low-poly edges show through as seams and shading bands. MikkTSpace, a tangent-space algorithm by Morten Mikkelsen, exists to prevent this: it produces the same tangents for the same mesh in any tool. Its reference code describes it as a common standard for baking tools; Unity and Godot generate MikkTSpace tangents, and Unreal offers it on import.

Bake on the final mesh

A baked map is only correct for the exact normals, UVs and triangulation it was baked against. Re-triangulating, changing hard edges or recomputing normals on import after the bake changes the tangent frames and breaks the map. MikkTSpace's own notes say the triangulation of quads matters too, so export triangulated meshes.

6 Height maps, bump mapping and parallax

A height map is a greyscale texture where white means high and black means low. Bump mapping, introduced by James Blinn in 1978, tilts the normal using the slopes of such a height field. Unity, for example, converts height maps to normal maps on import, because one normal map read is cheaper than the several height reads needed to find a slope.

Normal maps cannot move anything: the colour at a point stays where it was painted, so deep detail looks printed on when the camera moves. Parallax mapping fixes some of that. It reads the height map once and slides the texture lookup towards the viewer by an amount that grows with depth and with how shallow the view is. Parallax occlusion mapping (POM) goes further: it steps along the view ray through the height field, layer by layer, until it finds where the ray first goes below the surface, so raised parts hide what is behind them.

7 Displacement, tessellation and silhouettes

All of the above happen inside the pixel shader on a flat triangle, so none of them can change the outline of an object. That outline, the silhouette, is where fake detail is caught out: a normal-mapped rock is a perfect circle against the sky, and a brick wall seen along its length has a ruler-straight top.

Displacement mapping moves real vertices along their normals by the height map, so the geometry itself becomes bumpy. It needs enough vertices to carry the detail. Tessellation creates them on the GPU at draw time, splitting triangles more finely where the camera is close; Unreal's Nanite Tessellation does this for Nanite meshes with a displacement map.

8 Detail normal maps

A unique normal map large enough for the pores on a face or the weave of a jacket seen up close would be enormous. A detail normal map solves it with two scales: a unique map for the large shape, and a small tiling map of fine grain repeated many times across the surface. The shader blends the two directions per pixel.

What makes the cost grow

  • Screen coveragePixel tricks are paid per pixel. A POM floor filling the screen at 4K costs far more than the same material on a small prop.
  • Viewing anglePOM takes more steps at grazing angles, exactly where floors and walls are usually seen.
  • Map resolutionEvery doubling of a normal map's width is four times its memory.
  • Extra layersDetail maps, blended materials and decals each add texture reads to every pixel they cover.
  • Tessellation densityDisplacement needs vertices where the detail is; dense tessellation adds geometry work to every pass that draws the mesh.

5 How it breaks

Normal map bugs are lighting bugs, so they hide under some lights and show under others. Check every fix with a light moving round the object.

Bumps look like dents under overhead light

Cause
The map uses the other green channel convention: made for DirectX-style (Y−) and used where Y+ is expected, or the reverse.
Ask for
The engine's flip green setting on the affected textures, and one agreed convention for the baker and texturing tool's export presets.

Seams and bands along the low-poly edges

Cause
The engine's tangents differ from the baker's: one uses MikkTSpace and the other does not, or the mesh's normals, UVs or triangulation changed after baking.
Ask for
MikkTSpace on both sides, tangents imported from the file or computed the same way, and a re-bake against the final triangulated mesh.

Wavy, smeared or missing detail

Cause
Bake rays started inside the sculpt or never reached it: the cage is too tight, too loose or the ray distance too short, so texels record the wrong part of the model or nothing.
Ask for
The cage or ray distance adjusted per part, exploded bakes for parts that sit close together, and the bake checked for missed rays before texturing starts.

Lighting leans one way, bumps look soft

Cause
The normal map was imported as a colour texture: sRGB decoding bends every stored direction, and a colour format such as BC1 loses precision in X and Y.
Ask for
The engine's normal map texture type or compression setting, which turns sRGB off and uses BC5, and an import rule so it cannot happen again.

Bumpy surface, perfectly smooth outline

Cause
Expected behaviour: normal maps, parallax and POM never change the silhouette. It shows most on rounded props against the sky and on long walls.
Ask for
Real geometry where outlines show, such as modelled edges, trims or displacement on hero surfaces, not a stronger normal map.

Slices or swimming at grazing angles

Cause
POM with too few steps for its depth shows stacked layers; parallax mapping with too much height makes the texture slide as the camera moves.
Ask for
A lower height scale, more steps only at grazing angles, and fading the effect out with distance.

To diagnose a normal map, view it on its own. Most engines have a debug view that shows world-space normals as colours; a frame capture tool shows the texture's format and whether sRGB is on.

Changing engines

Moving content between Unreal and Unity or Godot flips the expected green channel. Decide per project which convention source files use, and set the import flag by rule, not by eye.

7 Say it to your AI

Normal map problems need three facts from you: which tool baked the map, which engine and import settings read it, and a description of the light under which it looks wrong. Without the light, an AI cannot tell a convention bug from a tangent bug.

Questions to ask when reviewing normal map work

  1. Which green channel convention do our source maps use, and where is the import flag set?One wrong preset inverts every map it touches.
  2. Do the baker and the engine both use MikkTSpace, on the same triangulated mesh?Different tangents show up as seams that no texture edit can fix.
  3. Are normal maps imported as normal maps: linear, two-channel, BC5 or the mobile equivalent?Colour settings bend the stored directions.
  4. Where does POM or tessellation run, and how much of the screen does it cover?Their cost is per pixel or per triangle, so coverage decides the price.
  5. Which silhouettes matter, and is there real geometry there?No pixel trick fixes an outline.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why do tangent-space normal maps look blue-purple?

    Show answer

    Blue stores the part of the direction pointing out of the surface, which is always positive and usually close to 1, so blue stays near 255. Red and green store the sideways tilts, which are usually small, so they sit near 128.

  2. A rock looks great up close but its outline against the sky is perfectly smooth. Will a stronger normal map fix it?

    Show answer

    No. Normal maps, parallax and POM only change shading inside the outline. The silhouette needs real geometry: more modelled detail, displacement with tessellation, or extra pieces placed along the edge.

  3. Imported bricks look raised when lit from the left and sunken when lit from above. What is wrong?

    Show answer

    The green channel convention. Only the vertical tilt is inverted, so horizontal light looks right and vertical light looks wrong. Flip the green channel on import, and fix the export preset.
