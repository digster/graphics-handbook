3.3 Vectors you can see

A vector is an arrow written as a few numbers. Games use arrows for almost everything that moves, faces or shines somewhere, and two operations on them, the dot product and the cross product, answer most of the questions a renderer asks about angles.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Tell someone in a field to walk 4 steps east and 3 steps north, and you have given them a vector. It says which way to go and how far, written as two numbers, (4, 3). It does not say where to start. In 3D a vector needs a third number for up and down.

A point is different: it is a place, such as the corner of a table or the position of a player. Points and vectors are both written as lists of numbers, which is why they get mixed up, but they behave differently. Subtract two points and you get the vector between them. Add a vector to a point and you get a new point. Adding two points together means nothing.

Everyday comparison

A treasure map says: from the old oak, 20 paces north, then 10 paces east. The oak is a point. The paces are a vector.

Start the same paces from a different tree and you end somewhere else. The instruction travels; the oak stays where it is.

The problem it solves

A game must describe where things are, how they move, which way they face and where light comes from. Vectors give all of these one shape: three numbers.

Two cheap operations then answer most questions about them: how much two directions agree, and which direction is at right angles to both.

Why a lead cares

Most features you will brief, from aim assist and AI vision to lighting and camera rigs, come down to a handful of dot and cross products. Most vector bugs come from three slips: forgetting to make a direction length 1, mixing up points and directions, and getting an order or a sign backwards. Knowing those three lets you read AI-written code and ask the questions that find them.

3 How it works

Seven steps: what a vector is, how it differs from a point, how long it is, then the two products and what games do with each.

1 An arrow is a list of numbers

A 2D vector is two numbers: how far along x and how far along y. A 3D vector adds z. The numbers are called components. The arrow they describe has a direction and a length but no position, so the same numbers drawn anywhere are the same vector.

Engines store each component as a 32-bit floating-point number, so one 3D vector takes 12 bytes. Shader code calls this type float3; engine code calls it FVector (Unreal) or Vector3 (Unity, Godot).

2 Points and directions in a game

A game scene is full of both kinds of thing. Positions of characters, props and the camera are points. Movement, facing and light are vectors. A velocity is a vector measured per second: (4, 3) m/s moves an object 4 m across and 3 m up every second, wherever it happens to be.

The most common operation in gameplay code links the two: subtract one point from another to get the vector from the first to the second. Enemy position minus player position is the arrow from the player to the enemy; its length is the distance between them.

Simplified

Engines usually store points and vectors in the same type: Unity's documentation describes Vector3 as a "representation of 3D vectors and points". The code has to know which one it holds. Matrices treat them differently, moving points but only turning directions, which 3.4 explains.

3 Length, and making it one

The length of a vector, its magnitude, comes from Pythagoras: square each component, add them, take the square root. (4, 3) has length 5. In 3D the same rule applies with three components.

Many questions only care about direction. For those, code scales the vector so its length is exactly 1, making it a unit vector. This is called normalising: divide every component by the length. A normalised (4, 3) is (0.8, 0.6). Directions such as normals and light directions are almost always stored as unit vectors.

Maths Length and normalising as formulas

length of (x, y, z) = √(x² + y² + z²)

normalised v = v ÷ length of v

A vector of length 0 has no direction, so it cannot be normalised: the division is by zero. Engines guard against this and return a zero vector instead (see section 6). Code that compares distances often skips the square root and compares squared lengths, which gives the same answer for "which is closer" and saves work.

4 The dot product: how much two arrows agree

The dot product of two vectors is one number. To compute it, multiply the matching components and add the results: (1, 2, 3) · (4, 5, 6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32.

What makes it useful is what that number means. It equals the two lengths multiplied together and then by the cosine of the angle between the arrows. So for two unit vectors the dot product is the cosine itself: 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles, −1 when they point in opposite directions. Geometrically it is the length of one arrow's shadow on the other.

Maths The dot product two ways

a · b = ax·bx + ay·by + az·bz

a · b = |a| × |b| × cos θ

The first line is how it is computed. The second is what it means, where |a| is the length of a and θ the angle between the arrows. If both are unit vectors the lengths are 1 and the result is cos θ. If they are not, the answer is scaled by both lengths, which is the source of most dot-product bugs. The angle itself, if needed, is the arccosine of the dot product of the two unit vectors.

5 The dot product at work: light, sight and facing

Lighting. A surface lit straight on is brightest. Tilt it away and the same beam of light spreads over more surface, so each part receives less. The amount it receives is the dot product of the surface normal N and the direction towards the light L, both unit vectors. Negative values mean the light is behind the surface, so shaders clamp them to zero: brightness = max(0, N · L). This is the heart of diffuse lighting, covered in 7.2 Shading models, and a pixel shader computes it for every pixel of every lit surface.

Try it below. Drag L around N and watch the readout and the patch. Then drag either arrow longer: the raw dot product grows past 1, while the brightness, computed from normalised copies, stays between 0 and 1. Tick "Normalise both" to see the two agree.

Sight. An AI guard with a 90° vision cone sees the player if the angle between its forward direction and the direction to the player is under 45°. Comparing angles needs an arccosine; comparing dot products does not. Normalise the direction to the player, take its dot product with the guard's forward vector, and compare the result with cos 45°, which is 0.71.

Facing. The sign of a dot product says whether two directions are on the same side. A surface faces the camera when the dot product of its normal and the direction to the camera is positive. Gameplay uses the same test for "is the enemy behind me?". The GPU makes its own front-or-back decision for backface culling a different way, from the order of each triangle's corners on screen.

Code A vision cone in six lines

The same shape works in any engine language. This is C#-like pseudocode.

Vector3 toPlayer = player.position - guard.position;   // point - point = vector
float distance = toPlayer.magnitude;
if (distance < 0.001f || distance > viewRange) return false;  // zero length: cannot normalise
Vector3 d = toPlayer / distance;                       // normalise
float cosHalfAngle = 0.71f;                            // cos 45° for a 90° cone; compute once
return Dot(guard.forward, d) >= cosHalfAngle;        // forward must be unit length too

When reviewing code like this, check that both vectors in the dot product have length 1, that the threshold is a cosine and not an angle in degrees, and that the zero-distance case is handled.

6 The cross product: the arrow that sticks out

The cross product takes two 3D vectors and returns a third, at right angles to both. Its length equals the area of the parallelogram the two inputs span, so it is longest when they are at right angles and zero when they are parallel.

Order matters. b × a points exactly the opposite way to a × b. Which of the two directions counts as "up" also depends on the handedness of the coordinate system: the same numbers point out of the screen in one convention and into it in the other. Unity's documentation describes its Vector3.Cross with a left-hand rule for this reason.

In 2D the third arrow has nowhere to go but straight out of the page, so only its sign is left. That sign is useful on its own: it says whether the second arrow turns left or right from the first, which is how a car's AI decides which way to steer towards a target.

Maths The cross product, component by component

a × b = (ay·bz − az·by, az·bx − ax·bz, ax·by − ay·bx)

length of a × b = |a| × |b| × sin θ

Six multiplies and three subtractions. The result is usually not unit length, so code that wants a direction normalises it afterwards. If a and b are parallel, sin θ is 0 and the result is the zero vector, which cannot be normalised.

7 The cross product at work: normals and camera axes

Normals from triangles. A triangle with corners A, B and C has two edges from A: B − A and C − A. Their cross product is perpendicular to both edges, so it is perpendicular to the triangle. Normalise it and you have the triangle's normal. Tools use this to compute normals for a mesh that has none. Which side it points out of depends on the order of the corners, the triangle's winding order.

Camera axes. A camera needs three directions: forward, right and up, each at right angles to the others. Code usually knows only where the camera looks and which way the world's up is. Two cross products fill in the rest: right from forward and world up, then the camera's own up from right and forward. When the camera looks straight up or down, forward and world up are parallel, the first cross product is zero, and the camera has no idea which way is right.

4 What it costs

Vector maths is close to free. A 3D dot product is three multiplies and two additions, which a GPU does as one multiply and two fused multiply-adds: five floating-point operations. The cost that matters is how many times you do it.

OperationArithmetic for 3D vectorsTypical use
Add, subtract3 additionsMoving a point, the vector between two points
Dot product3 multiplies, 2 additionsLighting, vision cones, facing tests
LengthA dot product with itself, then a square rootDistances, speeds
NormaliseA dot product, a reciprocal square root, 3 multipliesEvery direction before a dot product
Cross product6 multiplies, 3 subtractionsNormals, camera axes, left-or-right tests

Take diffuse lighting at 1080p with four lights: one N · L per light per pixel. That is 2,073,600 × 4 = 8,294,400 dot products per frame, or about 498 million per second at 60 fps. At five operations each, that is about 2.5 billion operations per second. The PlayStation 5's GPU is rated at up to 10.3 trillion.

5floating-point operations in one 3D dot product on a GPU

8.3 MN · L tests per frame at 1920 × 1080 with four lights
0.02%of a PlayStation 5 GPU's peak arithmetic for those tests at 60 fps

What makes the cost grow

  • Per pixel, not per vertexA 1080p frame has about 2 million pixels; a character has tens of thousands of vertices.
  • Per lightEvery light that reaches a pixel adds its own N · L and normalising.
  • Memory, not mathsFetching a vector costs more than the arithmetic on it, so meshes pack normals small (4.2).
  • BugsA missing normalise or a flipped sign can take days to trace. People-time is the real cost.

Simplified

Real shaders do more than one dot product per light, and the peak figure is a ceiling no game reaches. The point stands: vector arithmetic is rarely what makes a frame slow. Memory traffic and the number of pixels and lights are.

5 How it breaks

Vector bugs tend to look like design problems or lighting problems. These six cover most of them.

Faster on the diagonal

Cause
Movement input was not normalised, so right plus forward has length 1.41. The same bug makes strafing and aiming feel uneven.
Ask for
Normalising the input direction, or clamping its length to 1 so analogue sticks keep partial speeds.

Lighting blown out or too dark

Cause
A normal or light direction was not unit length when it reached N · L, often after scaling an object or blending normals. The dot product then exceeds 1 or falls short.
Ask for
Normalising both vectors in the shader just before the dot product, and a test scene with scaled objects.

Lit from the wrong side

Cause
The light vector points from the light to the surface where the formula expects surface to light, so N · L has the wrong sign. A mirrored normal does the same.
Ask for
A statement of which way each vector points, named in the code (toLight, not lightDir), and a check against a single known light.

Black pixels or vanishing objects

Cause
A zero-length vector was normalised, dividing by zero and producing NaN ("not a number"), which spreads through every calculation it touches. Common when two points coincide or a velocity is zero.
Ask for
The engine's safe normalise, or an explicit length check first, and a search for raw divisions by a length.

Camera flips looking straight up

Cause
The camera's right axis is forward × world up, which is zero when the two are parallel. The camera spins or snaps as it passes the vertical.
Ask for
Limiting pitch to just short of ±90°, or keeping the previous right axis when the cross product is near zero.

Normals pointing inwards

Cause
Generated normals used the edges in the wrong order, or a conversion between left- and right-handed coordinates flipped them. Lighting looks inverted.
Ask for
A debug view that draws normals as lines, and a check of the cross product order against the engine's handedness.

Most engines can draw vectors in the viewport for debugging. A few lines that draw a velocity, a normal or a vision cone in the game view settle most of these arguments in minutes.

Every engine has a 3D vector type with the same handful of operations. The names differ, and so does what "forward" means.

Porting caveat

Code moved between engines keeps compiling and quietly breaks: "forward" changes axis and sign, and the same cross product points the other way under a different handedness. When an AI ports gameplay maths from one engine to another, ask it to list every axis, sign and cross product order it changed. 3.4 has the full comparison of axes.

7 Say it to your AI

Good vector briefs say which vectors are points and which are directions, insist on unit length where it matters, and ask for the edge cases: zero length, parallel inputs, the vertical. Adapt these.

BriefUse when adding a vision cone or aim check
Engine: Godot 4.7, GDScript. Enemies need a vision cone: 100° wide, 15 m range, blocked by walls.

1. Implement the check with a dot product against the enemy's forward vector. Normalise the direction to the player first and compare with the cosine of half the cone angle, computed once, not with an angle.
2. Use Godot's conventions for forward and say which axis that is. Name each variable as a point or a direction.
3. Handle the player standing exactly on the enemy (a zero-length vector) and players above or below the enemy.
4. Add a debug draw of the cone and tests with players at 49°, 51° and directly behind.
  • Naming the dot product and the cosine stops a slower, error-prone angle comparison.
  • Asking for the engine's forward axis catches the most common porting mistake.
  • The 49° and 51° tests sit either side of the 50° edge, so they prove the threshold is right.
Bug reportUse when lighting changes with an object's scale
Unity 6, URP, custom HLSL shader. Objects we stretch unevenly, such as a crate scaled 3 × 1 × 1, look too bright on some faces and too dark on others. With even scaling they look correct.

Find where the shader transforms and normalises the normal and the light direction. Check that both are unit length at the moment of the N · L dot product, after transformation and after interpolation between vertices. Explain why uneven scale changes the normal, show the fix, and give me a test scene that would have caught it.
  • The pattern (only uneven scale) points at normals losing unit length or direction.
  • Naming the two moments where length is lost keeps the search narrow.
  • Asking for a test scene turns one fix into a guard.

Questions to ask when reviewing vector code

  1. Are both vectors in this dot product unit length, and where were they normalised?Otherwise the result is not a cosine, and thresholds and lighting drift.
  2. Does this light vector point towards the light or away from it?The wrong one flips the sign of N · L and lights the back of everything.
  3. What happens when this vector is zero?Normalising it divides by zero, and NaN spreads.
  4. Is this variable a point or a direction, and which space is it in?Mixing them compiles and runs, and is wrong. Spaces are the subject of 3.4.
  5. Which order is this cross product in, and does it match the engine's handedness?Swapping the inputs reverses the result.
  6. Is it comparing cosines or angles?Comparing dot products with a precomputed cosine avoids an arccosine per check.

8 Check yourself

  1. Movement code multiplies the stick input by a speed of 5 m/s without normalising it. How fast does the character run when the stick is pushed fully up and right, giving (1, 1)? What should it be?

    Show answer

    The length of (1, 1) is √2 ≈ 1.41, so the character runs at about 7.07 m/s instead of 5 m/s. Normalising the input to (0.71, 0.71) fixes it.

  2. A guard faces (0, 1). The normalised direction to the player is (0.6, 0.8). The guard's vision cone is 90° wide. Can it see the player?

    Show answer

    Yes. The dot product is 0 × 0.6 + 1 × 0.8 = 0.8, which is above cos 45° ≈ 0.71. The player is about 37° off centre, inside the 45° half-angle.

  3. Normals computed with a cross product point into the model instead of out of it. Name two likely causes.

    Show answer

    The edges went into the cross product in the wrong order, or the triangle's corners are listed in the opposite winding order. A conversion between left- and right-handed coordinates can cause either.
