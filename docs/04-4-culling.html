4.4 Culling

The cheapest object to draw is the one you never send. Culling finds what the camera cannot see, because it is behind the camera, hidden behind a wall, or too small to matter, and skips it before it costs a draw call or any GPU work.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A level holds far more than any one view shows. Behind the camera, around the corner, inside the next building, over the hill: from most viewpoints, most of the world is out of sight. Drawing it anyway costs a draw call on the CPU and vertex and pixel work on the GPU, for nothing on screen.

Culling is the family of tests that find this waste and skip it. Each test asks one question of every object. Is it inside the camera's view? Is it big enough on screen to matter? Is something solid in front of it? Then, on the GPU, one last question of every triangle: is it facing the camera? Every test must be conservative. It may keep something that turns out to be invisible, which wastes a little time, but it must never remove something that should be seen.

Everyday comparison

A theatre set is built for the audience's view. Nothing is built offstage, out of the audience's sightlines. Rooms behind the set's walls are left empty. The backs of the flats are bare wood. Props at the very back of the stage get less detail than those at the front.

Culling makes the same decisions every frame, for a camera that can walk anywhere.

The problem it solves

Without culling, the cost of a frame would grow with the size of the level instead of with what the camera sees. A city level would cost the same whether you face a wall or a skyline.

Culling lets a level be large, because only the part in view costs anything.

Why a lead cares

Culling is where level design meets performance. A layout with walls, corners and rooms hides most of itself and runs fast; a wide-open vista with long sightlines gives culling little to remove. Some culling needs authoring and build steps, such as marking occluders or baking visibility data. And when culling goes wrong, it is visible: objects pop in late, vanish at the screen edge, or shadows disappear.

3 How it works

Follow one frame's culling from the cheapest tests on the CPU to the last test on the GPU. There are eight steps.

1 Test the bounds, not the triangles

Testing every triangle of every object would cost more than drawing them. Instead each object carries a bounding volume: a simple shape that encloses all of it. The two common ones are a box aligned with the world's axes, called an AABB, and a sphere. If the shape is outside the view, everything inside it is too.

A sphere is the cheapest to test: one point and one radius. A box fits tall or flat objects much more tightly, so it culls more often. Engines usually store both, compute them when the mesh is imported, and update them as the object moves.

2 Frustum culling: is it in view at all?

The camera sees the inside of its view frustum, the cut-off pyramid of 3.4 Spaces and transforms, bounded by six planes: left, right, top, bottom, near and far. Frustum culling tests each object's bounding volume against those planes and skips anything wholly outside one of them. For a camera with a 90° view, it typically removes everything behind and beside the camera: often more than half the level.

Testing tens of thousands of objects one by one adds up, so engines group objects in space, into grids, trees of boxes or bounding volume hierarchies, and test the groups first. A group wholly outside the view rejects everything in it with one test. A group wholly inside accepts everything in it with no further tests. Only the groups crossing an edge need their objects tested one by one.

Maths The sphere against plane test

Each frustum plane is stored as a unit normal n pointing into the frustum and a distance d. For a sphere with centre c and radius r:

signed distance = n · c + d

outside if signed distance < −r for any of the six planes

That is one dot product and one comparison per plane. A box test does the same with the box corner that lies farthest along the plane's normal.

3 Distance and small-object culling

An object in view can still be too small to matter. A bottle 80 m away covers a few pixels; drawing it costs a draw call and gains almost nothing. Distance culling skips objects beyond a set distance, usually shorter for smaller objects. Small-object culling asks the same question directly: how many pixels would it cover? Below a threshold, it is skipped.

The two are the same idea from two directions, because an object's size on screen falls in proportion to its distance. Unreal's Cull Distance Volume makes the link explicit: a table of object sizes and the distance at which each size is culled.

Simplified

Distant objects are usually swapped for simpler versions before they are culled, which is level of detail, the subject of 4.5. Culling is the last step of that chain: the version with nothing in it.

4 Occlusion culling: is something in front of it?

Frustum culling keeps everything in the view's direction, including the street behind the building in front of you. Occlusion culling removes objects that are in view but completely hidden. The objects that do the hiding are occluders: large, solid things such as walls, buildings and terrain. The objects being tested are occludees. Most objects are occludees only; a barrel hides too little to be worth using as an occluder.

An object counts as hidden only if it is hidden everywhere. A tower whose top shows above a wall must be drawn whole, because the GPU draws whole objects.

The oldest way to ask the question uses the GPU itself. The renderer draws an object's bounding box against the depth buffer without writing any colour, and an occlusion query reports how many pixels passed the depth test. Zero means hidden. The catch is timing: the answer arrives after the GPU has done the work, and waiting for it stalls the CPU. Dean Sekulic's chapter in GPU Gems describes the usual fix, reading each result a frame later, which can make objects appear a frame late as the camera moves.

5 Hi-Z: testing against a depth pyramid

Many modern engines test many objects at once against a depth pyramid instead. First they render the best occluders into a depth buffer. Then they build a chain of ever smaller copies of it, each texel keeping the farthest depth of the four texels below it. That chain is the hierarchical depth buffer, and testing against it is Hi-Z occlusion culling, also called HZB.

To test an object, take the rectangle it covers on screen and pick the pyramid level where that rectangle spans only a texel or two. Read those few texels. If the object's nearest point is farther than the farthest depth stored there, every pixel it could cover is already hidden. Big objects and small ones cost the same: a handful of reads.

Try it on a whole town below. The strip under the map is the occlusion depth buffer for one row of the screen, with its pyramid. Turn the camera into the buildings, then away from them, and switch the stages on and off to see what each one saves.

6 Precomputed visibility: PVS and portals

For levels that do not change, visibility can be worked out before the game ships. The level is divided into cells, and for each cell a tool computes every object that could be seen from anywhere inside it: its potentially visible set. At runtime the engine finds the camera's cell and looks up the list. The idea goes back to Teller and Séquin's 1991 work on architectural walkthroughs.

Indoors, rooms and doorways make the structure explicit. In portal culling, each room is a cell and each doorway or window is a portal. The renderer draws the camera's room, then looks into the next room only through the portals it can see, narrowing its view to each opening as it goes.

Both techniques shine in buildings, corridors and dense streets, and do little in open landscapes. Both depend on geometry that does not move: if a wall in the bake is destroyed in play, the baked data still says the rooms behind it are hidden.

7 Culling on the GPU

The renderers of 4.3 that keep the scene in GPU memory cull there too. In GPU culling, a compute shader tests every instance, or every small cluster of triangles, against the frustum and the depth pyramid, then writes the survivors for indirect draws. Thousands of GPU threads test thousands of objects at once, and the CPU never sees the list.

There is a chicken-and-egg problem. Hi-Z needs a depth buffer, but the depth buffer comes from drawing the scene. A common answer is to cull in two passes, using last frame's depth first and this frame's depth to catch the rest. Unity's documentation for its URP GPU occlusion culling describes the same principle: it tests against depth from the current and previous frames and renders objects found unoccluded in either.

Ubisoft's Assassin's Creed Unity renderer took this down to clusters of 64 triangles, culling by cluster bounds and triangle facing on the GPU. Its authors report 20 to 40% of triangles culled that way, and 30 to 80% of shadow triangles.

8 The last test: back faces

Everything so far skips whole objects. The GPU adds one more test, for every triangle. 4.1 Meshes showed that the order of a triangle's corners, its winding order, tells its front from its back. Once the vertices are on screen, a front-facing triangle's corners go one way round and a back-facing triangle's go the other. Backface culling skips the back-facing ones. On a closed mesh they are always hidden behind the front, and they are about half its triangles.

Surfaces with no back to hide, such as leaves, flags and cloth, turn it off and draw both sides. Which corner order counts as the front is a convention: glTF uses anticlockwise, and an import that flips it turns a mesh inside out.

4 What it costs

Culling is work, and it only pays when it removes more than it costs. Each stage has its own bill.

StageRuns onWhat it costsWhat it saves
FrustumCPU, or GPU in GPU-driven renderersA few plane tests per object or group, every frameDraw calls and GPU work for everything out of view, often more than half the level

Published numbers show the scale. On a PlayStation 4, the Assassin's Creed Unity pipeline spent about 600 µs drawing its 300 best occluders, 100 µs shrinking that depth to 512 × 256, 50 µs combining it with last frame's depth and 50 µs building the pyramid. That is about 0.8 ms of GPU time each frame before a single object has been tested.

The other side of the ledger is just as real. Unity's documentation warns that its built-in occlusion culling runs on the CPU at runtime and can offset the time it saves, so it helps most when a project is GPU-bound because of overdraw. Godot's warns that detailed occluders can overload the CPU, and that moving an occluder forces its acceleration structure to be rebuilt.

  • Object countEvery object is tested every frame. Grouping objects in space keeps the cost growing slower than the level.
  • Open viewsWith few occluders, occlusion culling costs time and removes little. Long sightlines make every stage less effective.
  • Extra viewsEvery shadow cascade, reflection capture and split-screen view needs its own culling, from its own viewpoint.
  • Moving occludersBaked data cannot follow them, and runtime occluders that move cost rebuilds.
  • Precomputed detailSmaller visibility cells cull more precisely but take more memory and longer bakes.

In proportion

Frustum and backface culling are always worth having and every engine does them for you. Occlusion culling and baked visibility are choices: measure them on your level, from the viewpoints players actually use, with them on and off.

5 How it breaks

Culling bugs are among the most visible in rendering, because the symptom is something appearing or vanishing. These are the common ones.

Objects vanish at the screen edge

Cause
The bounding volume is smaller than what is drawn. Shaders that move vertices, such as wind on trees, or animation that reaches outside the bind pose, push geometry beyond its bounds, so the object is culled while part of it is still on screen.
Ask for
The bounds drawn in a debug view, and the engine's bounds scale or extension set for animated and shader-deformed meshes.

Things pop in a frame late

Cause
Occlusion results from the previous frame. An object hidden last frame is skipped this frame, even though it has just come into view round a corner.
Ask for
A two-pass occlusion test or a conservative margin, and a test at full speed on the target device, where frame rates and camera speeds are real.

Shadows disappear

Cause
A shadow caster was culled against the camera's view instead of the light's. The object is off screen, but its shadow falls into view.
Ask for
Shadow casters culled against each light's own frustum or cascade, and the setting that controls shadow-caster culling distance checked.

Something visible is missing

Cause
Stale or over-simplified visibility data. A level was edited after the occlusion bake, or simplified occluders poke out beyond the real walls and hide things that should show.
Ask for
The bake run as part of every build, a check that fails when baked data is older than the level, and simpler occluders kept inside the real geometry.

Thin surfaces vanish from behind

Cause
Backface culling on a surface with no back, such as a leaf, a flag or a sheet of paper, or a winding order flipped by mirroring or import.
Ask for
Two-sided materials on exactly those surfaces, and a check of the import's handedness and any negative scales.

Occlusion culling made it slower

Cause
An open scene with few occluders. The tests and the occluder rendering cost more than the little they remove.
Ask for
Frame timings with occlusion on and off from the views players use, and occlusion limited to the areas where it pays.

Every engine has a way to freeze culling and fly the camera out to see what was kept. It is the fastest way to confirm any of these.

Static only

Baked visibility in every engine covers geometry that never moves. Unity's documentation is explicit that moving objects can be occludees but cannot occlude, and that its built-in occlusion culling is not suitable for scenes generated at runtime. Destructible walls and procedural levels need runtime occlusion instead.

7 Say it to your AI

Good briefs about culling name the viewpoints that matter, give the counts before and after each stage, and ask for the cost of culling as well as its savings. Adapt these.

BriefUse when a dense level sends too much to the GPU
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7, no Nanite on props. Target: PlayStation 5 at 60 fps.
Our old-town level has 38,000 actors. From the market square, stat InitViews shows about 9,000 visible primitives, and draw calls run near 5,000. The square is ringed by tall buildings, so most of the town should be hidden from it.

1. Explain what each culling stage is doing from the square, using the stat InitViews numbers, and where the visible count is higher than the view suggests.
2. Propose changes: cull distances by size with a Cull Distance Volume, HZB occlusion, and whether precomputed visibility suits this level. Give the expected effect and the cost of each.
3. List any props whose bounds look too large or too small, and how to check them.
4. Tell us how to measure the result from five fixed camera positions, with culling frozen to verify what is sent.
  • Fixed viewpoints and the stat numbers make the problem measurable.
  • Asking for the cost of each stage stops a blanket "turn everything on".
  • Freezing culling gives a visual check of what is actually sent.
Bug reportUse when objects pop in or vanish
Unity 6.6, URP, GPU Resident Drawer with GPU occlusion on. Two problems since we enabled them:
1. Enemies rounding a corner at sprint speed appear one frame late.
2. Trees at the right edge of the screen lose their canopies on windy days. Our foliage shader moves vertices up to 1.5 m.

For each, tell me which culling stage removes the object and why. Check the trees' bounds against the maximum wind offset, and say how to extend them. For the enemies, check which depth the occlusion test uses and whether a second test this frame is happening. Give me a repeatable test scene for both.
  • Separating the two symptoms points at two different stages.
  • Giving the wind offset lets the AI compute the bounds needed.
  • A test scene turns a visual glitch into something that can be retested.

Questions to ask when reviewing culling work

  1. Which stages run, in what order, and what does each cost on the target device?Culling that costs more than it saves makes the frame slower.
  2. What are the bounds of anything animated or moved in a shader?Bounds that are too small make things vanish at the screen edge.
  3. Are shadow casters culled against each light's own view?Otherwise shadows disappear when their casters leave the screen.
  4. When is baked visibility rebuilt, and what checks that it is current?Stale data hides things that should be seen.
  5. How does it behave in the most open view in the game?Occlusion culling saves least where sightlines are longest.

8 Check yourself

  1. A lamp post's bounding sphere crosses the edge of the view, but the post itself is just off screen. It is drawn anyway. Is that a bug?

    Show answer

    No. Culling tests the bounding volume, and is conservative on purpose: anything whose bounds touch the view is kept. It costs one wasted draw. A tighter box would cull it, which is why engines often test boxes for tall objects.

  2. A team turns on occlusion culling in an open desert level and the frame gets slower. Why?

    Show answer

    There is almost nothing to hide behind. The occluder rendering and the tests still cost time, but they remove few objects, so the cost outweighs the saving.

  3. Backface culling skips about half the triangles of a closed mesh. Why does it not halve the vertex shader's work?

    Show answer

    The GPU can only tell which way a triangle faces once its corners are on screen, which is after the vertex shader has run. What it saves is rasterisation and pixel shading of the back faces.
