5.3 Rasterization

Rasterization turns each triangle into the list of pixels it covers and gives every one of them a blend of the values at the triangle's three corners. It is fixed, fast hardware, and it decides how much pixel work each triangle creates.

Triangle rasterizer

1 The picture

2 In plain words

After the vertex shader has run, the GPU knows where the three corners of a triangle land on screen. The screen, though, is a grid of pixels. Rasterization is the step that bridges the two. It finds every pixel the triangle covers and works out, for each one, a blend of whatever the vertex shader attached to the corners: colours, texture coordinates, normals, depth.

The coverage rule is strict and simple. Every pixel has one test point, its centre. If the centre is inside the triangle, the pixel is in; if not, it is out, even if most of the pixel is under the triangle. Each pixel that is in becomes a fragment, a candidate for that pixel's colour, and goes on to the pixel shader.

Everyday comparison

Lay a paper triangle on squared paper and fill in every square whose middle is under the triangle. Then colour each filled square by mixing three paints, using more of a corner's paint the closer the square is to that corner.

That is rasterization: the squares are pixels, the middle of each square is the test point, and the paint mix is the blend of corner values.

The problem it solves

Triangles are exact shapes with corners anywhere; a screen is a fixed grid. Something has to decide, millions of times a frame, which squares a shape fills, with no gaps and no square filled twice where two triangles meet.

The rasterizer makes that decision in fixed hardware, the same way on every GPU that follows the API's rules.

Why a lead cares

This is where geometry cost turns into pixel cost. How many pixels a frame shades depends on resolution, on how big the triangles are on screen and on how many surfaces overlap. Tiny or very thin triangles can make the GPU do two to four times the shading work for the same picture. Jagged, crawling edges and textures that bend across a floor also start here, so you will meet this chapter again when you review LOD budgets, resolution targets and anti-aliasing choices.

3 How it works

Follow one triangle from the end of the vertex shader to the list of fragments the pixel shader receives. There are eight steps.

1 Corners become pixel coordinates

The vertex shader hands over each corner in clip space (5.2 Vertex shaders). The GPU divides by the fourth coordinate, w, to apply perspective, then scales the result to the render target: x from 0 to its width in pixels, y from 0 to its height, with y running down the screen.

In these coordinates pixel (2, 1) is the square from x 2 to 3 and y 1 to 2. Its sample point is the centre, (2.5, 1.5). Direct3D 10 and later put pixel centres at these half-way positions; Direct3D 9 put them on whole numbers, which is why old code is full of half-pixel offsets.

Finally the corners are snapped to a fixed grid inside each pixel, so all later maths is exact integer arithmetic. Vulkan requires at least 4 bits of this sub-pixel precision, 16 steps per pixel, and its 2026 roadmap profile asks for 8 bits, 256 steps.

2 Coverage: is the centre inside?

Pixel coverage is the yes-or-no answer for each pixel. Each edge of the triangle is a straight line, and a straight line splits the screen in two. A small formula, the edge function, tells which side of a line a point is on: positive on the inside, negative on the outside. A pixel centre is covered when it is on the inside of all three edges.

Maths The edge function

For an edge from corner a to corner b and a point p, all in pixel coordinates:

E(p) = (b.x − a.x) × (p.y − a.y) − (b.y − a.y) × (p.x − a.x)

E is zero on the line, positive on one side and negative on the other. Its size is twice the area of the triangle a, b, p. Moving p one pixel to the right changes E by a fixed amount, so hardware can step across a row of pixels with one addition per pixel.

3 Working in blocks, not pixel by pixel

A 1080p screen has 2,073,600 pixels, and testing all of them for every triangle would be hopeless. The rasterizer only looks inside the triangle's bounding box, and it tests blocks of pixels before single pixels. A block that one edge rules out completely is skipped. A block entirely inside is accepted whole. Only blocks that the edge crosses are tested pixel by pixel.

4 Barycentric weights

The same three edge values do a second job. Divide each one by the triangle's total and you get the pixel's barycentric coordinates: three weights, one per corner, that always add up to 1. A pixel at corner A has weights 1, 0, 0. A pixel in the middle has about a third each. A weight below zero means the pixel is outside, which is the coverage test again in another form.

5 Blending what the vertex shader wrote

Every value the vertex shader outputs for a corner is blended with these weights: colour, UV coordinates, normal, position, depth. This attribute interpolation is how a texture spreads smoothly across a triangle and how lighting varies across a surface that only has values at its corners. The pixel shader never sees the corners; it only sees the blended values at its own pixel.

Try it below. Drag the corners and watch which pixels turn on: only those whose centre dot is inside. Point at pixels near an edge and read their weights; outside ones have a weight below zero. Then pick the tiny triangle and turn on the 2 × 2 quads to see step 8 coming.

6 Perspective-correct interpolation

There is a catch. Equal steps across the screen are not equal steps across a surface that leans away from the camera. Halfway up the image of a floor is much less than halfway along the floor, because the far half is squeezed into a smaller part of the screen. Blending with the plain screen-space weights, called affine interpolation, puts the texture in the wrong place, and the error changes direction at the diagonal where two triangles meet. That is the bend you saw in the tilted square.

Perspective-correct interpolation fixes this by blending each value divided by its corner's depth, then dividing by the blended 1 / depth. Modern GPUs do this in hardware for every vertex output, and it is the default in every shading language. Affine texture mapping is the reason textures swam and bent on the original PlayStation.

Maths The perspective-correct blend

With screen-space weights wA, wB, wC, a value u at the corners and each corner's clip-space w (its distance from the camera):

affine: u = wA uA + wB uB + wC uC

correct: u = (wA uA/w'A + wB uB/w'B + wC uC/w'C) ÷ (wA/w'A + wB/w'B + wC/w'C)

Here w' is the corner's clip-space w. For the floor above, the halfway screen point gets (0.5 × 1/3) ÷ (0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 1/3) = 0.25 of the way along. In HLSL a vertex output marked noperspective switches this off; nointerpolation skips blending entirely and copies one corner's value.

7 The fill rule: shared edges drawn once

Meshes are made of triangles that share edges, and sooner or later an edge passes exactly through a pixel centre. If both triangles drew that pixel, it would be shaded twice, which shows as a darker line through anything transparent. If neither drew it, a crack of background would show through.

The fill rule settles it. Direct3D and OpenGL use the top-left rule: a centre exactly on an edge belongs to the triangle only if that edge is a top edge (exactly horizontal, above the rest of the triangle) or a left edge (on the triangle's left side). Vulkan requires the same outcome without naming the rule. Every shared edge is a left or top edge of exactly one of its two triangles, so every pixel is drawn exactly once. The snapped integer positions from step 1 are what make "exactly on the edge" a clear yes or no.

Simplified

The fill rule only works when neighbouring triangles share exactly the same corner positions. A corner that sits in the middle of a neighbour's edge (a T-junction), or two meshes whose seam vertices differ by a rounding error, can still leave single-pixel cracks that sparkle as the camera moves. That is a modelling or export problem, and the fix is in the mesh.

8 Stair-step edges and small triangles

One test point per pixel has two side effects. The first is edge aliasing: a pixel is either fully in or fully out, so a smooth edge becomes a staircase, a wire thinner than a pixel breaks into dashes, and slow movement makes pixels flip on and off along every edge. Fixing it means testing more points per pixel or blending over several frames, which is 9.2 Anti-aliasing.

The second is the small-triangle cost. The rasterizer hands covered pixels to the pixel shader in 2 × 2 groups called quads, because shaders need neighbouring pixels to work out how fast values change (5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw explains why). A quad with only one covered pixel still runs all four. A triangle that covers one pixel therefore costs four shader runs, and a long thin sliver touches a new quad at almost every pixel. On top of that, each triangle costs setup time however few pixels it has: Epic's Nanite team reported that GPUs set up at most four triangles per clock.

4 What it costs

The rasterizer is fixed-function hardware and is rarely the bottleneck by itself. Its cost shows up in the work it creates: one pixel shader run for every covered pixel, rounded up to whole quads. Two numbers drive that work, how many pixels the screen has and how big triangles are on it.

Triangle size matters almost as much. The table comes from a simulation of 20,000 randomly placed and rotated triangles of each size, counting the 2 × 2 quads each one touches.

What makes the bill grow

  • ResolutionPixel work grows with width × height. An 83% screen percentage at 1080p renders about 1600 × 900, 69% of the pixels.
  • Triangles under a few pixelsDense meshes seen from far away shade two to four lanes per pixel and hit the triangle setup limit.
  • Long, thin trianglesSlivers touch a new quad at almost every pixel along their length, whatever their area.
  • Overlapping surfacesEvery surface that covers a pixel is rasterized there, even if it ends up hidden. That is overdraw, the subject of 5.4.
  • More samples per pixelMSAA tests coverage and depth at 2, 4 or 8 points per pixel, though the pixel shader still runs once per pixel (9.2).

In proportion

For most content, triangle setup and coverage are cheap next to the pixel shading that follows. The cases that hurt are the ones above: dense meshes far away, grass and hair made of thin strips, and high resolutions. Epic built Nanite's software rasterizer because hardware rasterizers, built to run wide over pixels, are slow for triangles about the size of a pixel. Its talk reports about three times the speed of their fastest hardware path for that case.

5 How it breaks

Rasterization problems show up as edge and texture artefacts, or as GPU time that does not match what is on screen. These five are the ones you will see.

Jagged, crawling edges

Cause
One coverage sample per pixel. Every edge is a staircase, and the steps slide along the edge as things move.
Ask for
The anti-aliasing method and its settings on each platform, and a check that it is actually enabled in the build (9.2).

Textures bend along a diagonal

Cause
UVs are blended in screen space instead of with perspective correction: a noperspective output, or UVs computed after the divide by w.
Ask for
How UVs travel from the vertex shader to the pixel shader in that material, and a test scene with a large floor seen at a grazing angle.

Cables, fences and grass break into dashes

Cause
The geometry is thinner than a pixel at that distance, so it only covers the pixel centres it happens to cross.
Ask for
A thicker or textured LOD for distant thin geometry, a minimum on-screen width, or an anti-aliasing method that handles it.

Sparkling cracks along seams

Cause
Neighbouring triangles do not share exact corner positions: T-junctions, or two meshes whose seam vertices differ slightly. The fill rule cannot close a gap in the geometry.
Ask for
A check of the seam in the mesh: welded vertices, no T-junctions, and matching positions where modular pieces meet.

Distant dense meshes cost more than near ones

Cause
Full-detail meshes far away: triangles of a pixel or less waste most of each 2 × 2 quad and hit the triangle setup limit.
Ask for
A quad overdraw view of the shot, then LODs or Nanite for the bright assets, with before and after GPU timings.

To confirm any of these, take a frame capture with a frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX. Both can show a draw's triangles over the rendered image, and the pixel history of any single pixel: every triangle that covered it and what each one did.

Good briefs about rasterization name the view or capture that shows the problem, give on-screen sizes in pixels, and ask for a way to verify the fix. Adapt these.

Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7, deferred, targets PS5 and PC at 60 fps with a 1440p internal resolution.
Our forest and cliff meshes are non-Nanite static meshes of 80,000 to 200,000 triangles. In wide shots the base pass takes 9 ms and the Quad Overdraw view lights up the distant hills and trees.

Questions to ask when reviewing geometry and shader work

  1. Where do triangles get smaller than a few pixels, and what keeps them from doing so?Tiny triangles cost two to four shader runs per pixel; LODs, Nanite or culling should stop them.
  2. Does any vertex output use nointerpolation, noperspective or flat? Why?The wrong modifier causes bending textures or faceted shading.
  3. Do neighbouring meshes share exact vertex positions along their seams?T-junctions and near-miss positions leave cracks the fill rule cannot close.
  4. Is any geometry thinner than a pixel at normal viewing distance?Cables, railings, hair and grass will break up and shimmer unless something handles them.
  5. At what resolution does this run on each target, and was the cost measured there?Pixel work scales with pixel count: a 4K target is four times the work of 1080p.

8 Check yourself

  1. A pixel centre lies exactly on the edge two triangles share. Which triangle draws it, and what goes wrong without a rule?

    Show answer

    Exactly one: the triangle for which that edge is a top or left edge, under the top-left rule. Without a rule the pixel is drawn twice, which shows through glass or smoke as a darker line, or not at all, which leaves a crack.

  2. A triangle covers one pixel. How many pixel shader runs does the GPU start for it, and why?

    Show answer

    Four. Pixels are shaded in 2 × 2 quads so the shader can compare neighbours, so the quad holding that pixel runs all four lanes. Three are helper lanes whose results are thrown away: 25% of the work is kept.

  3. A large floor's tiled texture bends along a diagonal line when you look along it, and the bend grows at grazing angles. What is happening?

    Show answer

    The UVs are being blended with screen-space weights, affine interpolation, instead of perspective-correct ones. The error flips direction at the diagonal where the floor's two triangles meet. Look for noperspective or UVs computed after the divide by w.
