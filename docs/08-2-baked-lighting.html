8.2 Baked lighting

The oldest and still the most common way to get bounced light into a game is to compute it in the editor, store it in textures and probes, and read it back for almost nothing at run time. This chapter shows what is stored where, what that costs in memory and in artists' time, and how bakes go wrong.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Baked lighting is lighting worked out before the game runs. A tool in the editor traces light around the level, bounce after bounce, as slowly and as carefully as it likes, and saves the answer. The game then reads it back instead of computing it. Bounced light that would cost milliseconds every frame costs a texture read.

The answer is stored in two places. A lightmap holds light for surfaces that never move: floors, walls, buildings, rocks. Light probes, points scattered through the air, hold light for everything else to pick up as it passes: characters, vehicles, props that get knocked over.

Everyday comparison

A theatre set painter paints light and shadow straight onto the scenery: a warm glow below a painted window, dark streaks in the corners. Under plain stage lighting the set looks lit by a sunset. It costs nothing during the show, and it looks as good as the painter is patient.

It cannot follow an actor who carries a lamp across the stage, and it is wrong the moment someone moves a flat.

The problem it solves

Bounced light, global illumination, needs light from every direction at every point (8.1). Computing that every frame is expensive enough that real-time methods are a chapter of their own. Baking does it once, at film quality, and makes it nearly free to display, which is why phones, handhelds and most games with fixed lighting still use it.

Why a lead cares

Baking moves the cost of lighting from the player's GPU to your team's time. Every lighting change needs a new bake, which can take minutes or hours, so bake time sets how many times a day an artist can try an idea. Lightmaps also take memory and disk space, demand a second set of UVs on every static mesh, and rule out moving the lights or walls they were baked with. Whether to bake is a production decision as much as a rendering one.

3 How it works

A bake has three parts: what is computed, where it is stored, and how the game reads it back. The steps follow that order, from the lightmapper to the probes that light moving objects.

1 What the bake computes

The lightmapper is the editor tool that does the work: Lightmass and GPU Lightmass in Unreal, the Progressive Lightmapper and the newer Unity Compute Light Baker in Unity, the LightmapGI node in Godot. For each texel of the lightmap, and each probe, it traces light paths: rays to the lights to find direct light, and rays into the scene that collect light which has already bounced. Each path is one sample. Hundreds or thousands of samples per texel are averaged, and a denoiser smooths what noise is left.

The settings that matter are the number of bounces, the number of samples per texel and the area to bake carefully. Unreal's Lightmass emits photons across the whole level, so a Lightmass Importance Volume around the playable area keeps the effort there; outside it, surfaces get one lower-quality bounce. Its Build, Lighting Quality presets run from Preview ("gets the point across, but at least it is fast") to Production ("looks awesome, takes a while"). Unity sets Max Bounces (default 2) and direct and indirect sample counts in the Lighting window; Godot's LightmapGI has Bounces (default 3) and quality presets from Low to Ultra.

2 Lightmap UVs: a second, unique layout

A lightmap is a texture, so the mesh needs UV coordinates to find its texels (6.1). The UVs used for the material usually cannot serve. Material UVs are free to overlap and repeat: every plank of a floor can use the same wood texture, and both sides of a symmetrical prop can share one half of its texture. Light is different on every plank and on each side of the prop, so each spot needs its own texel.

Lightmap UVs are a second set of coordinates in which every face has its own space, nothing overlaps, and each UV island has a margin of empty texels around it. Unity stores them in the mesh's second UV channel, Mesh.uv2, which its documentation also calls UV1. Unreal reads the channel named by the mesh's Lightmap Coordinate Index; Godot calls it UV2. All three engines can generate them on import.

The gap, or padding, is a real requirement. Epic's lightmapping guide says a minimum of four texels between islands is usually needed to avoid all bleeding, and that lightmap UVs should be split where there are deep recesses or crevices, accepting that every split risks a visible seam. Unity's default Lightmap Padding is 2 texels; Godot generates UV2 when a mesh is imported with Light Baking set to Static Lightmaps.

3 Lightmap resolution: texels per metre

Lightmap resolution is the lightmap's texel density: how many texels cover each metre of surface. It decides how sharp baked shadows can be and how much memory the lightmaps take. Because surfaces are two-dimensional, memory grows with the square: double the density and the texel count quadruples, as Unity's documentation warns.

Each engine sets density differently. Unity's Lighting window has Lightmap Resolution in texels per unit, scaled per object by Scale In Lightmap. Unreal sets a Light Map Resolution per static mesh, the size of the texture area its whole UV layout gets, so the density on screen depends on the mesh's size; the Lightmap Density view mode colours every mesh by its actual density so mismatches stand out. Godot's import option Lightmap Texel Size defaults to 0.2 m per texel, five texels per metre, which its documentation suits to small and medium scenes and suggests raising to 0.5 or more for large ones.

Unity also stores, by default, a second texture with the main direction light arrives from at each texel, called Directional mode. Normal maps can then catch baked light from the right side. Unity says it takes about twice the memory of non-directional lightmaps; Godot's LightmapGI offers the same as Directional, off by default.

4 Light mobility: how much of each light is baked

Chapter 7.1 introduced static and dynamic lights. Light mobility is the setting on each light that picks between them, with a middle option that bakes some of the light and computes the rest every frame. It is the main switch between quality, speed and freedom.

The middle setting stores baked shadows from static objects separately from the light itself, in a shadow mask: a texture that records, per texel, how much each light is blocked. The light's direct contribution is computed every frame and multiplied by it. Unreal's Stationary lights bake distance field shadow maps for static objects and give moving objects per-object dynamic shadows. Unity's Mixed lights take one of three Lighting Modes chosen in the Lighting window: Baked Indirect (only the bounce baked, real-time shadows), Shadowmask (baked shadows from static objects, with a Distance Shadowmask variant) and Subtractive (direct light baked too, cheapest, and not available in HDRP). Unity's own comparison rates their iteration time as slowest for Baked, slow for Mixed and fast for Realtime.

5 Light probes: baked light for things that move

A character cannot use a lightmap: it moves, and its shape changes. Instead the bake records light at points in the air. A light probe stores the light arriving at one point from every direction. At run time, a moving object finds the probes around it and blends them, weighted by how close each one is.

Engines differ in how probes are placed and blended. Unity's Light Probe Group is placed by hand; Unity connects the probes into tetrahedra and blends the four corners of the one containing the object, once per object, a method Robert Cupisz presented at GDC 2012. The probes must form a volume, at least two layers high, and Unity advises placing them densely where light changes quickly and sparsely where it does not. Godot's LightmapGI generates probes automatically, at Gen Probes Subdiv 8 by default, and adds hand-placed LightmapProbe nodes. Per-object blending has a limit: a large object gets one blend for its whole body, so half of it can be wrong when it straddles a shadow line.

6 Spherical harmonics: light from every direction in nine numbers

A probe needs to hold light from every direction, compactly, so it can be stored by the thousand and blended cheaply. Most engines use spherical harmonics, SH: a set of fixed shapes over the sphere, ordered from smooth to detailed, that are added together in different amounts. The probe stores only the amounts. The first shape is the same in every direction and holds the average. The next three, band 1, tilt that average towards one side along each axis. The five of band 2 add a little more shape.

That is why three bands, nine numbers per colour channel, became the standard. Ravi Ramamoorthi and Pat Hanrahan showed in 2001 that nine coefficients describe the irradiance from a distant environment with average errors of around 1%. Unity's probes store exactly that: third-order SH, 27 floating-point values per probe, 9 for each of red, green and blue. Unreal's volumetric lightmap stores a third-order SH per sample too. The same trick is the irradiance map of 7.6 squeezed into nine numbers.

Maths What the nine numbers are

Each SH basis function Ylm(ω) is a polynomial in the direction ω = (x, y, z): a constant for band 0; x, y and z for band 1; xy, yz, xz, x² − y² and 3z² − 1 for band 2, each times a fixed constant. The bake projects the incoming light L(ω) onto each one:

clm = ∫ L(ω) Ylm(ω) dω

and the shader rebuilds the light a surface with normal n receives as a short sum, with one weight per band for the cosine falloff:

E(n) ≈ Σ Al clm Ylm(n),   A0 = π, A1 = 2π⁄3, A2 = π⁄4

Because Al shrinks quickly for higher bands, higher-detail shapes barely affect a matte surface, which is why stopping at band 2 loses so little. Blending probes is just blending their coefficients. Sharp light, like a reflection, would need many more bands, which is why reflections use cubemaps instead. Truncating a sharp light can also undershoot on the opposite side, called ringing; Unity's Light Probe Group has a Remove Ringing option, at some cost in accuracy and contrast, and Unreal has a de-ringing smoothing setting for its volumetric lightmap.

7 Irradiance volumes: probes everywhere, automatically

Placing probes by hand does not scale to a large level. An irradiance volume fills the level with a 3D grid of probes instead, and a point in the air reads the eight probes at the corners of its cell. Grids that adapt put small cells near surfaces, where indirect light changes quickly, and large ones in open air.

Unreal's version is the volumetric lightmap, which replaced its older Indirect Lighting Cache. It places samples in bricks of 4 × 4 × 4 cells, densely around static geometry and over the whole Lightmass Importance Volume, and interpolates them for every pixel of a moving object. Volumetric Lightmap Detail Cell Size sets the finest spacing; Epic warns that halving it can raise memory up to eight times.

Unity's is Adaptive Probe Volumes, APV, in URP and HDRP. It fills each volume with bricks of 64 probes in a 4 × 4 × 4 grid, uses smaller bricks where there is more geometry, with default probe spacings of 1, 3, 9 or 27 m, and lets each pixel sample the eight closest probes. APV can also blend between several baked Lighting Scenarios, for example day and night or lights on and off, stream probe data for large worlds, and update sky light at run time with sky occlusion.

Godot's LightmapGI node bakes both lightmaps and an automatically generated set of probes on the GPU, inside the editor only. Its documentation recommends it for low-end hardware, including integrated graphics and phones, and notes that it is the only Godot GI method that supports more than two bounces.

8 Bake time and the cost of iteration

A bake's quality is mostly a matter of samples. More samples per texel means less noise, but the noise falls slowly: to halve it, the bake needs four times the samples, and so about four times the time. Denoisers, OptiX or Open Image Denoise in Unity, Open Image Denoise by default in GPU Lightmass, JNLM or OIDN in Godot, let bakes stop earlier, at the risk of smearing detail.

The engines attack bake time in three ways. Faster hardware: GPU Lightmass, a Beta feature in Unreal 5.8 that needs DirectX 12 and a ray-tracing GPU, is described by Epic as comparable in speed to CPU Lightmass spread over many machines, and Godot bakes on the GPU. Previews: Unity can bake interactively while you work, GPU Lightmass has a Bake What You See mode, and Godot recommends low quality while iterating and a high-quality final bake before export. Distribution: CPU Lightmass can spread a build across machines with Swarm, which GPU Lightmass does not support.

Baked data is also a build artefact. Lightmaps and probe data are generated files that must be rebuilt whenever lights or static geometry change, kept in version control or regenerated on build machines, and checked so nobody ships a level with stale lighting. Unreal's viewport warns when lighting needs to be rebuilt; the review list in section 7 has the questions to ask.

4 What it costs

At run time baked lighting costs almost nothing: a lightmap read per pixel of static geometry, and a probe blend per moving object or per pixel. The bill arrives as memory, disk space and bake time. A worked example: 20,000 m² of lightmapped surface at 8 texels per metre is 20,000 × 64 = 1,280,000 texels. In BC6H, at 1 byte per texel, that is about 1.2 MB; directional lightmaps double it; uncompressed half-float RGBA, at 8 bytes per texel, would be about 9.8 MB. Raise the density to 32 texels per metre and every one of those numbers grows sixteen times.

  • Texel densityMemory and bake time grow with its square. Most of the budget goes to the few surfaces that need sharp shadows.
  • Surface areaEvery lightmapped square metre costs texels, including backs of buildings nobody sees. Exclude what the player cannot reach, or give it low density.
  • Samples and bouncesSamples set noise; bounces matter most in bright, closed interiors. Both lengthen bakes far more than they change memory.
  • Lighting scenariosDay and night, or lights on and off, each need their own full set of baked data.
  • Probe densityProbe volumes grow with the cube of their resolution: halving the spacing can mean eight times the probes.
  • IterationEvery change to lights or static geometry means another bake. Slow bakes mean fewer ideas tried per day.

Budget both kinds of cost

Set a lightmap memory budget per level and platform, a target texel density per surface type, and a bake time target for preview and final builds. If a bake takes all night, artists get one try a day; if a preview bake takes two minutes, they get dozens. Measuring bake time per level is as important as measuring frame time.

5 How it breaks

Baked lighting fails in the bake, in the UVs, or at the boundary between baked and moving things.

Hard lines across smooth surfaces

Cause
A lightmap seam: two neighbouring faces whose UV islands sit apart in the lightmap, so their edge texels were baked and filtered separately and disagree.
Ask for
Fewer splits in the lightmap UVs on smooth surfaces, matched texel density across the seam, and the engine's seam-stitching option where it has one.

Light or shadow bleeding at edges

Cause
Texels bigger than the wall, so one texel spans inside and outside; or too little padding, so filtering blends neighbouring islands. Epic advises at least four texels of padding.
Ask for
More padding, higher density on thin walls, and thick geometry for walls between bright and dark spaces. Probe and voxel light leaking is covered in 8.5.

Blotchy or noisy baked light

Cause
Too few samples, a denoiser smoothing noise into blobs, or a lightmap resolution too low for the light's detail.
Ask for
A final bake at higher sample counts, denoiser settings checked on a test scene, and density raised only where the detail is visible.

Patches of light in the wrong places

Cause
Overlapping lightmap UVs, often the material UVs used by mistake: two faces share texels, so each shows the other's light.
Ask for
Generated or hand-made lightmap UVs on every static mesh, and the engine's overlap warnings treated as errors in the asset pipeline.

Moving objects lit wrongly for where they stand

Cause
Probes too sparse, missing from an area, or buried inside walls, so a character picks up light from the wrong side; or a large object blending one set of probes for its whole body.
Ask for
Probe coverage checked in the probe debug view, probes added where light changes, per-pixel probe volumes for large objects, and leak controls such as APV's Probe Adjustment Volume.

Shadows and light that do not match the level

Cause
Stale bake: static geometry or lights changed after the last bake, or an object marked static is moved at run time.
Ask for
A rebake step in the build pipeline, a check that fails builds with stale lighting, and objects that move at run time marked as movable.

To find the cause, look at the lighting on its own. Unreal's Lighting Only and Lightmap Density view modes, and Unity's Baked Lightmap and UV Overlap scene views, separate a bake problem from a material problem.

7 Say it to your AI

Baking briefs need the engine and lightmapper, what moves in the level, the memory and bake-time budgets, and the platforms. Ask for settings with numbers, and for checks you can run.

BriefUse when setting up baked lighting for a level
Unity 6.6, URP, targets: Nintendo Switch 2 and PC. Indoor level, about 6,000 m² of floor and walls, fixed lights, doors that open, NPCs walking through.
Budgets: lightmaps 24 MB on Switch 2; preview bake under 5 minutes on our workstations; final bake overnight on the build machine.

1. Recommend light modes per light type and a Mixed Lighting Mode, with reasons.
2. Propose texel densities per surface type (floors, walls, small props) and show the lightmap memory they imply, with and without Directional mode.
3. Recommend Adaptive Probe Volumes or Light Probe Groups for the NPCs, with probe spacing, and how to avoid leaks through the walls.
4. Give preview and final Lighting settings (samples, bounces, denoiser) and how to measure bake time per level.
  • Saying what moves, doors and NPCs, decides what can be baked and where probes are needed.
  • Memory and bake-time budgets turn density and sample counts into numbers that can be checked.
  • Asking for both preview and final settings protects the artists' iteration time.
Bug reportUse when a bake shows seams and bright edges
Unreal Engine 5.8, Lumen off, static lighting with CPU Lightmass, Production quality.
After the last bake: hard lines across the curved concrete wall in the atrium, and bright strips along the floor at the base of the walls that face the sunlit courtyard. Walls are 10 cm thick single meshes. Light Map Resolution 64 on the wall mesh.

1. For each problem, list the likely causes in order, using our settings: UV islands, padding, resolution, wall thickness.
2. Tell us what to check in the Lightmap Density view and in the mesh's lightmap UV channel, and what "good" looks like.
3. Propose fixes, starting with the cheapest, and say what each one costs in memory or bake time.
  • Naming the lightmapper, quality and resolution rules out guesses about settings.
  • Describing where each fault appears separates the seam from the bleeding.
  • Asking what to look for in the debug views lets you verify the diagnosis yourself.

Questions to ask when reviewing baked-lighting work

  1. Which lights are baked, which mixed and which real time, and why?Mobility decides the run-time cost and what can change in the game.
  2. What texel density does each kind of surface get, and what is the lightmap memory per level?Density sets both sharpness and memory, which grows with its square.
  3. Do all static meshes have non-overlapping lightmap UVs with enough padding?Overlaps scramble light; thin padding bleeds it across islands.
  4. How do moving objects get baked light, and has probe coverage been checked where they go?Missing or buried probes light characters from the wrong side.
  5. How long do preview and final bakes take, and who runs the final bake?Bake time sets how fast artists can iterate, and stale bakes ship bugs.
  6. What happens when a door opens, a wall breaks or the time of day changes?Baked light cannot follow; it needs lighting scenarios, mixed lights or real-time GI.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why can't a static mesh use its material UVs for its lightmap?

    Show answer

    Material UVs may overlap or repeat, so several faces share the same texels of the texture. Each spot on a surface receives different light, so the lightmap needs every face to have its own texels, with padding so filtering does not blend neighbouring islands.

  2. A level's lightmaps are 6 MB at 10 texels per metre. The art director wants shadows twice as sharp. What happens to memory and bake time?

    Show answer

    Twice as sharp means twice the density, 20 texels per metre, which is four times the texels: about 24 MB, and roughly four times the bake time. Raising density only on the surfaces where the shadows matter keeps most of the saving.

  3. A character crossing from a sunlit courtyard into a dark corridor is lit brightly inside the corridor. The walls are lightmapped correctly. Where do you look?

    Show answer

    At the probes: their density and placement near the doorway, whether any are buried in walls or missing inside the corridor, and whether the character blends probes per object or per pixel. Moving objects get baked light from probes, not lightmaps.
