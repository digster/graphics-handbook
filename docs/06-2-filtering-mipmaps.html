6.2 Filtering and mipmaps

A texture almost never reaches the screen at its own size. Up close one texel covers many pixels; in the distance one pixel covers hundreds of texels. Filtering decides what colour each pixel gets in both cases, and mipmaps, pre-shrunk copies of every texture, keep the distant case sharp, calm and cheap.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

When the GPU reads a texture for a pixel, the UV it has rarely lands on the centre of one texel, and the pixel rarely covers exactly one texel. Texture filtering is the rule for turning the texels around that point into one value.

Up close there are too few texels. A 512-texel texture across a wall that fills a 4K screen is stretched over 3,840 pixels, 7.5 per texel, and the GPU has to invent smooth colour between texels. In the distance there are too many. One pixel covers dozens of texels but reads only a handful, so it picks up an arbitrary one, and the result flickers as the camera moves. The cure for the distance is a mipmap: a chain of copies of the texture, each half the size of the last, made ahead of time. A distant pixel reads a small copy whose texels already average everything it covers.

Everyday comparison

A map app does not draw every street of a country when you zoom out to see the whole of it. It switches to a simpler map drawn in advance for that zoom level, and switches again as you zoom, each level showing about as much detail as the screen can hold.

Mipmaps are those zoom levels for every texture, and the GPU picks the level for every pixel, every frame.

The problem it solves

Without them, distant textures turn to noise that crawls when the camera moves, and regular patterns such as bricks, fences and tiles grow false curved stripes. Reading scattered texels from a large texture is also slow, because neighbouring pixels read texels far apart, which defeats the GPU's texture cache.

With them, each pixel reads a few neighbouring texels from a copy of the right size, which looks calm and is fast.

Why a lead cares

Filtering and mip settings are a few checkboxes per texture and per quality preset, and they decide whether floors shimmer, roads blur at a distance and pixel art stays crisp. They also spend memory (mipmaps add a third to every texture) and GPU time (anisotropic filtering can read many times more texels). Most of the mistakes are one setting wrong on a whole class of textures, which is exactly the kind of rule worth putting in an import preset and checking automatically.

3 How it works

Follow the GPU through one texture read, from the simplest filter to the most careful. There are eight steps.

1 Two cases: magnified and minified

Every texture read falls into one of two cases. When the texture is magnified, one texel spreads over several pixels, as on a wall right in front of the camera. When it is minified, one pixel spans several texels, as on that same wall seen from across a courtyard. Most surfaces in a frame are minified somewhere, and floors are both at once: magnified at your feet, heavily minified at the horizon.

2 Nearest and bilinear

Nearest filtering, also called point filtering, reads the one texel whose square the sample point falls in. It is the cheapest read and keeps hard edges, which is why pixel-art games use it on purpose. Magnified, every texel shows as a sharp block.

Bilinear filtering reads the four texels around the sample point and blends them, each weighted by how close the point is to its centre. Magnified textures become smooth gradients instead of blocks. GPUs do this blend in dedicated texture hardware, and it is the starting point for every filter that follows.

Maths The bilinear weights

Measure the sample point from the centre of the texel up and to its left, in texels: fx across and fy down, each between 0 and 1. The four weights are:

top left (1 − fx)(1 − fy) · top right fx(1 − fy) · bottom left (1 − fx)fy · bottom right fx·fy

They always add up to 1. In the figure fx = 0.3 and fy = 0.6, so the weights are 0.28, 0.12, 0.42 and 0.18.

3 Why distant textures shimmer

Bilinear fixes magnification and does nothing for minification. A pixel that spans 40 texels still blends only the 4 around its centre and ignores the other 36. Which 4 it lands on depends on exactly where the pixel centre falls, so neighbouring pixels pick unrelated values, and a tiny camera move changes them all. This is texture aliasing: noise in a still frame, a crawling shimmer in motion, and for regular patterns, moiré, broad false stripes that are not in the texture at all.

4 Mipmaps: the texture, pre-shrunk

A mip chain starts with the full texture, level 0, and halves the width and height at each level until it reaches 1 × 1. Each texel of a level is the average of 2 × 2 texels of the level above, so a 2048 × 2048 texture has 12 levels: 2048, 1024, 512 and so on down to 1. Engines build the chain at import or cook time, so it costs nothing per frame.

Each level has a quarter of the texels of the one before. The whole chain therefore adds a quarter plus a sixteenth plus a sixty-fourth and so on, which approaches one third. For a 2048 × 2048 texture at 4 bytes per texel, level 0 is 16 MiB and the 11 smaller levels add 5.33 MiB, 33.3% more. Unity's and Godot's documentation both quote the same figure.

Simplified

A plain 2 × 2 average is the simplest way to build a level. Engines also offer sharper filters, and special handling for normal maps and alpha-tested textures, where averaging changes what the values mean. Compressed formats store texels in 4 × 4 blocks, so their smallest levels round up to one block; the chain still adds about a third.

5 Choosing a level for each pixel

Mip level selection happens per pixel, in hardware. GPUs shade pixels in 2 × 2 blocks, so for every pixel they know how far its UV moves to the pixel on the right and the pixel below. Converted to texels, the longer of those two steps is how many texels the pixel spans. The level is the base-2 logarithm of that span: a pixel spanning 1 texel reads level 0, 2 texels level 1, 4 texels level 2, 8 texels level 3.

Spans between powers of two give fractional levels. A span of 6.1 texels gives level 2.6, which falls between level 2 and level 3. What the GPU does with the fraction is the difference between the next two filters.

Simplified

The step sizes are the screen-space derivatives that pixel shaders can also ask for directly, which 5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw covers. Hardware may approximate the lengths, and the graphics APIs allow some freedom in exactly how the span is measured.

6 Trilinear: blending two levels

The cheapest use of mipmaps rounds the level to the nearest whole number and does a bilinear read there. In the floor above, that is bilinear + mipmaps. It calms the distance, but where the rounding flips from one level to the next, the sharpness jumps, and floors show lines across them that slide along as the camera moves.

Trilinear filtering does a bilinear read in both neighbouring levels and blends them by the fraction: at level 2.6, 40% of level 2 and 60% of level 3. The lines disappear. The cost is 8 texel reads instead of 4, from two levels.

7 Anisotropic: long, thin footprints

Trilinear treats every pixel's footprint as a square. On a floor or road seen at a low angle it is nothing like one: a pixel near the horizon may span 2 texels across and 16 along the direction you are looking. Trilinear has to pick its level from the longer side, 16 texels, so it reads mip 4 and blurs the 2-texel direction eight times more than it needs to. That is why trilinear floors go to grey mush in the distance.

Anisotropic filtering measures both sides. It picks the level from the shorter side divided into the longer, then takes several trilinear reads, or probes, spread along the long side and averages them. For the 2 × 16 footprint with 8× anisotropy, that is 8 probes at mip 1 instead of one at mip 4, sharp along both axes. The setting, 2× to 16×, caps the number of probes; Direct3D 12 allows up to 16. Only pixels with stretched footprints pay for extra probes, which in practice means floors, roads, terrain and walls seen side-on.

Simplified

The probe pattern here follows the reference method in the OpenGL anisotropic filtering extension: probes = longer side ÷ shorter side, capped at the maximum, and level = log2(longer side ÷ probes). The specification leaves the exact method to each GPU, and vendors use their own, adaptive versions.

8 LOD bias

The LOD bias is a number added to the chosen level before the read. A bias of −1 reads one level sharper than the pixel size calls for; +1 reads one level blurrier. Negative bias brings back detail, along with some of the shimmer mipmaps were removing; positive bias saves bandwidth and hides noise at the price of blur.

The main legitimate use is with temporal anti-aliasing and upscaling (9.2, 9.3). When the frame is rendered at a lower internal resolution, every pixel is bigger, so the GPU picks blurrier mips; and TAA recovers detail over several frames anyway. Engines therefore add a negative bias. Godot documents both: −0.5 when TAA is on, and log2 of the 3D scale when rendering below full resolution, so half resolution on each axis gives −1. Unity's scripting reference advises against more than −0.5 on a texture, and suggests anisotropic filtering instead.

4 What it costs

Filtering costs texel reads, and through them memory bandwidth and texture-unit time. Mipmaps cost memory. Both depend on the setting, as this table shows for one texture read.

Nearest1None neededPixel art, data lookups, UI drawn at exact size
Bilinear4None neededUI, sprites, screen-sized effects
Bilinear + mipmaps4OneLow-end and mobile presets
Trilinear8TwoDefault for most 3D textures
Anisotropic 16×8 to 128TwoGround, roads, walls, anything seen at an angle

Texel reads are not free, but they are cheap when they hit the cache, and mipmaps are what make them hit it. A pixel reading level 0 of a large texture in the distance pulls texels from all over the image; reading the right level keeps neighbouring pixels on neighbouring texels. Godot's documentation names this as a benefit of mipmaps: distant textures render faster and use less memory bandwidth. Epic's scalability reference notes the other side: limiting anisotropic samples reduces texture bandwidth, though not texture memory.

  • ResolutionMore pixels mean more reads; 4K output has four times the reads of 1080p.

5 How it breaks

Filtering faults are easiest to see in motion. Record a slow camera move across a floor or a fence, and most of these show at once.

Shimmer and moiré in the distance

Cause
The texture has no mipmaps, so distant pixels read a few texels out of many. Common with textures imported with 2D or UI settings and then used in 3D.
Ask for
Mipmaps generated for every texture used in 3D, enforced by an import preset, and a mip level debug view to find the ones without.

Lines across floors that slide with the camera

Cause
Bilinear filtering with mipmaps rounds to one level per pixel, so the sharpness jumps where the level changes. Set per texture, or project-wide for cheaper presets.
Ask for
Trilinear filtering on 3D textures, and a check of any project setting that forces nearest-mip filtering, such as Godot's use_nearest_mipmap_filter.

Ground and roads go to mush in the distance

Cause
No anisotropic filtering, or too low a level, so stretched footprints are read with one square, blurry sample.
Ask for
Anisotropic filtering of 8× or 16× on ground, road and wall textures, set per quality preset, with its cost measured on the lowest target.

Dark or coloured lines at seams, only far away

Cause
The smaller mip levels average each island with the empty space or the neighbour beside it. The gap between islands halves at every level, so it runs out.
Ask for
Wider gaps between islands and atlas regions, and the space around islands filled with the island's own edge colour, a step usually called dilation.

Foliage and fences thin out with distance

Cause
Alpha-tested textures average their alpha in the mips, so it falls below the cut-off and whole blades and wires vanish at a distance.
Ask for
Mipmaps built to preserve alpha coverage, such as Unity's Preserve Coverage and Alpha Cutoff import settings matched to the material's cut-off, and a check at several distances.

Pixel art looks smeared

Cause
Bilinear filtering and mipmaps are on for textures meant to show hard-edged pixels, usually from a project-wide default.
Ask for
Nearest filtering and no mipmaps for pixel-art textures and UI drawn at exact size, as an import preset, with the camera snapped to whole pixels.

Most engines have a debug view that colours surfaces by mip level, like the colour option in the floor above. It shows at a glance which textures have no mipmaps and where levels change.

Two meanings of LOD bias

Unreal's texture LOD Bias and Unity's Global Mipmap Limit remove the largest mip levels, which saves memory and lowers resolution for every pixel. A sampler LOD bias, such as Unity's mipMapBias or Godot's texture_mipmap_bias, keeps every level and only shifts which one each pixel reads. Briefs and reviews should say which one they mean.

7 Say it to your AI

Good filtering briefs name the texture types involved, the platforms and their quality presets, and ask for a way to see the result in motion, since most filtering faults hide in still images.

BriefUse when setting texture filtering defaults
Engine: Unity 6.6, URP. Platforms: PC (high and medium presets) and Nintendo Switch-class mobile hardware (low preset).

Propose texture import presets for these types: environment albedo, normal and mask maps; terrain layers; foliage with alpha cutout; UI sprites; pixel-art sprites; lookup tables.
For each: Filter Mode, Generate Mipmap, Mipmap Filtering, Preserve Coverage, Aniso Level, Wrap Mode.
For each quality preset: Anisotropic Textures and Global Mipmap Limit.
Explain the visual risk of each choice, estimate the memory effect of the mipmap settings, and give a test scene and camera path that would show shimmer, mip lines, blur at grazing angles and thinning foliage.
  • Listing texture types gets a rule per class instead of one setting for everything.
  • Naming the presets separates per-texture settings from per-platform overrides.
  • A camera path makes the result checkable in motion, where these faults show.
Bug reportUse when floors shimmer or show lines
Godot 4.7, Forward+ renderer. In our town level the cobblestone streets shimmer when the camera moves, and on some streets there are faint lines across the road that slide along with the camera. Other textures look fine.

Check, for the cobblestone textures and their materials: whether mipmaps were generated on import (and whether Detect 3D changed the import settings), the material's Texture Filter mode, the project's use_nearest_mipmap_filter and anisotropic_filtering_level settings, and texture_mipmap_bias. Explain which setting causes which symptom, give the fix, and tell me how to confirm it with a mip level debug view.
  • Two symptoms, shimmer and sliding lines, point to two different settings.
  • Saying that other textures look fine points at per-texture import or material settings.
  • Naming the exact settings stops a generic answer about anti-aliasing.

Questions to ask when reviewing filtering changes

  1. Which textures have no mipmaps, and is each of them only ever shown at its own size?Any 3D texture without mips will shimmer at a distance.
  2. Is trilinear or bilinear + mipmaps in use, and on which platforms?The cheaper mode shows sliding lines across floors.
  3. What anisotropy level does each quality preset use, and was the cost measured?It is often the cheapest big quality win on ground textures, but not free on mobile.
  4. Is any negative LOD bias applied, by whom, and why?Engines and upscalers already apply one; stacking another brings shimmer back.
  5. How are alpha-tested textures' mips built?Plain averaging makes foliage and fences thin out and vanish with distance.
  6. Was the change checked in motion, not only in screenshots?Shimmer and mip lines are hard to see in still images.

8 Check yourself

  1. How much memory does a full mip chain add to a texture, and where does the number come from?

    Show answer

    About a third. Each level has a quarter of the texels of the one above, so the extra is a quarter plus a sixteenth plus a sixty-fourth and so on, which approaches one third. A 2048 × 2048 texture at 4 bytes per texel goes from 16 MiB to 21.33 MiB.

  2. A pixel on a distant road spans 2 texels across and 16 along. Which mip level does trilinear read, and what does 8× anisotropic filtering do instead?

    Show answer

    Trilinear uses the longer side: log2 of 16 is 4, so mip 4, blurring the road eight times more across than needed. 8× anisotropic takes 8 probes along the long side at log2(16 ÷ 8) = mip 1, which stays sharp in both directions.

  3. Textures look soft after enabling TAA, and someone proposes a LOD bias of −2 on all textures. What do you ask?

    Show answer

    Whether the engine already applies a bias for TAA or upscaling (Godot adds −0.5 for TAA, for example), and why −2: it reads levels four times sharper than the pixel size in each direction, which brings back shimmer and costs bandwidth. Better to match the documented rule, such as log2 of the render scale, and use anisotropic filtering for angled surfaces.
