6.3 Texture memory

Textures are usually the biggest thing in GPU memory. Games fit them by storing them in compressed formats the GPU can read directly, and by keeping only the detail the camera can currently see.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A texture is a grid of colour values. A 4096 × 4096 texture has 16.7 million of them, and a modern scene uses hundreds of textures: a colour map, a normal map and a mask for nearly every material. Stored plainly, they would not fit in the VRAM of any graphics card, let alone a phone.

Games solve this twice. First, block compression stores each small square of pixels in a fixed, tiny number of bytes that the GPU decodes for free as it reads. That cuts memory by four to eight times. Second, texture streaming keeps only the sharpness the camera needs right now in memory, and fetches more from disk as the player moves.

Everyday comparison

A tiler laying a mosaic does not get a list of 16 exact colours for every small square. For each square they get two tins of paint and, for each tile, a number from 0 to 3 that says how far to mix from the first tin towards the second. The instructions for every square are the same short length, so they can open the book at any square without reading the ones before.

Streaming is the tiler's van. It carries the detailed instructions only for the wall they are working on today, and a rough sketch of the rest.

The problem it solves

Image files such as PNG and JPEG are small on disk, but the GPU cannot read them: to find one pixel you have to decode the file from the start. Decoded, they take 4 bytes per pixel. Block compression is small and still lets the GPU jump straight to any pixel.

Streaming solves the rest: a level may reference more texture data than the card holds, but the camera can only ever see a fraction of it at full sharpness.

Why a lead cares

Texture memory is usually the largest line in a game's memory budget, and the smallest GPU you ship on decides how much of it you get. Texture formats are import settings that artists set, often by default, on thousands of files. The symptoms of getting them wrong are some of the most common visual bug reports: blurry textures after loading, smeared normal maps, blocky gradients, and crashes on low-memory devices.

3 How it works

Follow a texture from the artist's file to the pixels on screen. There are seven steps.

1 Why textures are big

An uncompressed format such as RGBA8 stores every texel in full: red, green, blue and alpha, one byte each, so 32 bits per pixel. Memory is then width × height × 4 bytes. A 4096 × 4096 texture is 64 MB. Its mipmaps, the half-size, quarter-size and smaller copies that 6.2 Filtering and mipmaps explains, add one third more: 85.3 MB.

The file the artist saved is not what the GPU uses. When the engine cooks a texture for a platform, it decodes the PNG or TGA and re-encodes it in a GPU format. The figure compares the three ways the same image can exist.

Simplified

Cooked game packages usually add general-purpose compression on top of the GPU formats, to make downloads and installs smaller. That layer is undone as the data loads, so it saves disk space and download time, not VRAM. Unreal's texture editor reports both sizes separately.

2 How block compression works

Every block-compressed format cuts the texture into small blocks, almost always 4 × 4 texels, and stores each block in the same number of bytes. Inside a block, neighbouring texels usually have similar colours. So instead of 16 colours, the block stores two endpoint colours and, for each texel, a short index that says where on the line between the endpoints its colour sits.

BC1, the oldest common format, does exactly that. It stores two endpoints in 16 bits each and a 2-bit index per texel. The decoder makes two extra colours at one third and two thirds of the way along, so every texel picks from four. That is 64 bits for 16 texels, 4 bits per pixel, an eighth of RGBA8.

Because every block has the same size, the GPU finds the block for any texel by arithmetic, reads its 8 or 16 bytes and decodes only that block, inside the texture unit, at full speed. That is why block compression saves more than memory: the GPU also moves four to eight times fewer bytes every time it samples, which matters most on phones, where memory traffic costs battery and heat.

Maths Working out any texture's size

For one mip level of a block-compressed texture:

bytes = ⌈width ÷ block width⌉ × ⌈height ÷ block height⌉ × bytes per block

For an uncompressed one:

bytes = width × height × bits per pixel ÷ 8

A full mip chain adds about one third: 1 + ¼ + ¹⁄₁₆ + … ≈ 4⁄3. Every BCn and ETC2 block is 4 × 4 texels; ASTC blocks range from 4 × 4 to 12 × 12. Sizes that are not a whole number of blocks are rounded up, which is why tiny mip levels of a compressed texture still take one full block.

3 The BCn family

PCs and the current Xbox and PlayStation consoles use the BCn formats, numbered BC1 to BC7. They differ in how many channels they store and how carefully. Each was designed for a kind of data, so choose by what the texture holds first and by quality second.

Compared with RGBA8 at 32 bits per pixel, BC1 and BC4 are one eighth of the size and the rest are one quarter. Against 16-bit floating-point HDR at 64 bits per pixel, BC6H is one eighth. BC6H and BC7 need Direct3D 11 class hardware; everything sold as a gaming PC or console for well over a decade has it.

Simplified

BC7 picks one of eight modes for every block: some split the block into two or three regions with their own endpoint pairs, some trade colour precision for finer indices, some code alpha separately. That flexibility is why it looks so much better than BC1, and why it is slow to encode: the compressor searches the modes for each block. The sizes in the table never change, whatever the mode.

4 Phones: ASTC and ETC2

Mobile GPUs mostly do not support BCn. Their two formats are ETC2 and ASTC. ETC2 also works on 4 × 4 blocks: 4 bits per pixel for RGB, and 8 bits per pixel for RGBA, where an extra EAC block carries the alpha. Single- and two-channel EAC versions, R11 and RG11, play the roles of BC4 and BC5. Google reports that more than 95% of Google Play devices support ETC2.

ASTC, designed by Arm and AMD, always stores a block in 128 bits but lets each texture choose the block's footprint, from 4 × 4 up to 12 × 12 texels. The same bits spread over more texels give a smaller, softer texture. It stores one to four channels and has an HDR profile. More than 80% of Google Play devices and Apple devices since the A8 chip of 2014 support it, and Arm's measurements put ASTC at 3.56 bits per pixel ahead of BC1 and ETC2 at 4.

5 Which format for which map

A material's textures hold different kinds of data, and each kind has a natural format. Colour that people look at is stored in sRGB, the gamma-encoded form 3.2 Colour describes, and decoded to linear values when sampled. Everything else is data: normals, roughness, masks. Data must be stored linear, with sRGB off, or the decoding bends the numbers.

Packing several masks into the channels of one texture is channel packing. A common layout puts ambient occlusion, roughness and metalness in red, green and blue. It saves a texture sample and a file, but formats that share endpoints between channels, such as BC1 and BC7, can let one channel's pattern leak into another.

6 Texture streaming: only the mips you need

A texture on a wall 8 m away covers a quarter of the screen width it covers at 2 m, so the GPU samples a mip level two steps smaller. The full-size levels would sit in memory unused. Mip streaming exploits this: the engine works out, for every texture, the sharpest mip any visible object needs, keeps that and everything smaller in memory, and loads or drops the large levels as the camera moves.

The memory set aside for this is the texture streaming pool. When the mips the camera wants fit, everything looks sharp. When they do not, the streamer drops the top mip from some textures until they fit, so the scene looks softer than it should. After a load or a camera cut, the pool briefly holds only the small mips while the large ones are read from disk, which is the familiar blur that sharpens a moment later.

Each engine sets the pool in its own way, and the names matter when you brief someone. Unreal calls it the texture streaming pool and sizes it in MB; Unity's Mipmap Streaming works to a single Memory Budget that also counts textures that do not stream. Textures that do not stream, which usually includes UI, stay at full size and take their share out of the same memory. In Unity, textures on objects the streamer cannot measure fall back to low-resolution mips until their level is set by hand.

7 Virtual texturing: only the pages you need

Mip streaming works per texture: if any part of a huge texture is close, all of its top mip must load. Virtual texturing goes finer. It cuts every mip of a texture into small square pages and loads only the pages that pixels on screen actually touch, into a fixed cache texture. A small lookup texture, the page table, tells the shader where each page currently lives in the cache.

Software virtual texturing finds out which pages are needed by having the GPU write page requests during rendering, which the CPU reads back a frame or more later and loads. Until a page arrives, the shader falls back to a coarser mip, so new areas can pop sharp a moment after they appear. Sparse textures are the hardware version: the GPU's own memory mapping lets a texture have only some of its pages backed by real memory.

Simplified

Real systems add a border of a few texels around every page, so filtering across page edges has the data it needs. Unreal's streaming virtual textures default to a border of 4 texels, and wider borders cost more memory. Pages from several textures sampled with the same UVs are often loaded together, which Unity calls a Texture Stack.

4 What it costs

Every game works to a texture memory budget: the share of the weakest target's memory that textures may use, after render targets, geometry, audio and code have taken theirs. Here is an illustrative scene: 100 environment materials with 2048² textures, 4 hero characters at 4096², 8 terrain layers, an HDR sky and the UI, in the formats from section 3.

5.3 MBone 2048² BC7 or BC5 texture with mips
2.7 MBthe same in BC1 or BC4
21.3 MBthe same uncompressed, RGBA8
×4memory each time a texture doubles in width and height

The last number is the one to watch. Raising a texture from 2048² to 4096² quadruples it: a hero character's three 4096² maps cost 53.3 MB, as much as four environment materials at 2048². Lowering the maximum size of textures the player never sees up close is usually the cheapest saving in any project.

What makes the bill grow

  • ResolutionMemory grows with the square of the width. One step up in size is four times the bytes.
  • Unique texturesEvery material with its own maps adds a full set. Tiling textures and shared trim sheets are reused for free.
  • Wrong formatsA mask stored as a colour texture, or a normal map stored uncompressed, costs two to eight times what it should.
  • Fully loaded texturesUI, textures that cannot stream and anything marked never to stream sit at full size, and in Unity they eat into the same budget.
  • BandwidthCompressed textures are also cheaper to sample. Uncompressed ones move four to eight times more bytes per frame, which costs battery and heat on phones.

The usual trade

Disk size, download size and VRAM are three different budgets. Cooked packages compress textures again for download, so a texture can be small to download and still large in memory. Always ask which of the three a number refers to.

5 How it breaks

Texture memory problems show up as a handful of recognisable pictures. Each points at a setting.

Blurry for a moment, then sharp

Cause
Streaming is catching up after a load, a teleport or a camera cut. Only small mips are in memory until the large ones arrive from disk.
Ask for
Preloading the textures around spawn points and cutscene cameras before the cut, and a check of how many requests are queued at that moment.

Textures stay soft everywhere

Cause
The mips the camera wants do not fit the streaming pool or budget, so the streamer keeps dropping top mips. Often a few oversized textures are crowding out everything else.
Ask for
The streaming stats at that spot, a list of the largest textures resident there, and a plan to cut their size or format before anyone raises the pool.

Blocky steps in smooth gradients

Cause
A sky, UI panel or soft gradient compressed with BC1 or a large ASTC block. Four shades per 4 × 4 block cannot follow a slow gradient, and 5:6:5 endpoints add banding.
Ask for
BC7 or a smaller ASTC block for that texture, uncompressed for UI, and a side-by-side capture to confirm the fix is worth its memory.

Blocky, speckled normal maps

Cause
A normal map compressed as colour: BC1 or BC3 instead of BC5, often with sRGB still on. The X and Y precision is too low and the gamma curve bends the vectors.
Ask for
An import rule that detects normal maps by name or folder and forces the engine's normal map setting, plus a scan for existing offenders.

One mask leaks into another

Cause
Unrelated masks packed into one BC1 or BC7 texture. Those formats share endpoints across channels, so a strong pattern in one channel bends the values of the others.
Ask for
BC7 at higher encoder quality, moving the most important mask to its own BC4 texture, or a different packing; compare the decoded channels, not the source file.

Crashes or long loads on older phones

Cause
The device does not support the format the build shipped, so the engine decompresses textures on load. Unity, for example, unpacks ASTC to uncompressed RGBA32 on devices without it: four times the memory or more.
Ask for
The supported formats of the low-end test devices, the build's per-platform texture format settings, and a fallback build or ETC2 default where needed.

To check what a texture really is in memory, take a frame capture with a frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX. Both list every texture with its format, size and mip count.

Same name, different meaning

"Compression" can mean three things in an engine: the GPU format (VRAM), packaging compression (disk and download) and Unity's Crunch, which shrinks files on disk on top of the GPU format. When someone reports a saving, ask which one changed.

7 Say it to your AI

Texture work is mostly audit and settings, which suits an AI with access to the project. Give it the platform, the budget in numbers and the measurement you trust, and ask for a list it can justify, not a blanket change.

BriefUse when textures do not fit the budget
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7. Targets: PS5 and a minimum-spec PC with a 6 GB graphics card.
The texture streaming pool is over budget in our two biggest levels on the PC target, and textures look soft there.

1. List the 50 largest textures resident in each level, with size, format, compression setting, sRGB flag and maximum in-game size.
2. Flag every texture whose format does not match its role: normal maps not on Normalmap, masks with sRGB on, uncompressed textures that are not UI.
3. Propose new maximum sizes per texture group, keeping hero characters and anything seen close at full size. Estimate the saving per change.
4. Write an asset validation rule that catches these mistakes on import. Do not change any assets yet.
  • Two targets and a card size give the AI the real constraint.
  • Listing what to report keeps the audit concrete and checkable.
  • "Do not change any assets yet" keeps the decisions with the art team.

Questions to ask when reviewing texture work

  1. Which format does each texture type end up in, on each platform?A setting that is right on PC may be decompressed on an older phone.
  2. Are normal maps, masks and other data textures stored with sRGB off?Gamma decoding bends data, and nothing crashes to warn you.
  3. Which textures are excluded from streaming, and why?Every never-stream texture sits in memory at full size for the whole level.
  4. What is the largest texture size, and who can see it up close?Each doubling of size is four times the memory.
  5. Is the saving measured in VRAM, on disk or in the download?The three are separate budgets and change for different reasons.

8 Check yourself

  1. How much memory does a 2048 × 2048 BC7 texture take with its mips? How much uncompressed?

    Show answer

    BC7 is 8 bits, 1 byte, per pixel: 2048 × 2048 = 4 MB, plus a third for mips, about 5.3 MB. Uncompressed RGBA8 is 4 bytes per pixel: 16 MB, about 21.3 MB with mips.

  2. An artist imported a normal map with the default colour settings: BC1 and sRGB on. What will you see, and what should it be?

    Show answer

    Blocky, slightly wrong lighting: BC1 has little precision for X and Y and sRGB decoding bends the stored vectors. It should use the engine's normal map setting: BC5 on PC and console, linear, X and Y only.

  3. After a camera cut, textures are blurry for half a second and then sharpen. Should you raise the streaming pool?

    Show answer

    Probably not. Textures that sharpen on their own mean the pool is big enough and the mips are still loading. Preloading at the destination helps. Textures that stay soft point at the pool or budget.
