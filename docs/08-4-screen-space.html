8.4 Screen-space tricks and their blind spots

Screen-space reflections, screen-space global illumination and contact shadows all do the same thing: they march rays through the depth buffer the frame has already drawn. That makes them cheap, sharp and fully dynamic. It also gives all of them the same four blind spots, and every engine pairs them with something else to answer when the screen cannot.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

By the middle of a frame the renderer has already drawn every visible surface. It knows, for each pixel, how far away that surface is, which way it faces and, a little later, what colour it is once lit. Screen-space effects reuse that picture. To find a reflection, a bounce of light or a small shadow, they follow a ray across the screen and look up the depth buffer at each step, instead of testing the ray against the real triangles of the scene.

That is a huge saving. Testing a ray against millions of triangles needs special data structures and, for speed, ray-tracing hardware. Looking up a depth value is one texture read. The price is that the depth buffer is only a photograph. It holds one surface per pixel, seen from one place, inside one frame.

Everyday comparison

Imagine finding your way around a town using only a single photograph taken from one street corner. You can trace a line across the picture and see which building front it reaches. You cannot see the yard behind the first row of houses, anything outside the edges of the photo, or the back of any building.

For most questions the photo is good enough, and it is far quicker than walking every street.

The problem it solves

Reflections, bounced light and fine contact shadows all need to know what a ray from each pixel meets. Answering that against the real scene is the job of ray tracing, which is expensive. Screen-space tracing answers it from data the frame already has, for a few milliseconds, on any GPU.

Why a lead cares

Screen-space effects are in nearly every shipped game, often on top of baked or real-time GI. Their failures are among the most familiar visual bugs: reflections that vanish when the camera tilts down, smears behind characters, lighting that changes as the camera moves. All of them come from four blind spots. Knowing them lets you tell an unavoidable limit from a fixable bug, and decide what the fallback should be.

3 How it works

The steps below follow one ray from start to finish: marching, deciding what counts as a hit, going faster with Hi-Z, failing, and handing over to something that can answer instead.

1 Marching a ray through the depth buffer

Ray marching means following a ray in steps and testing each step. In screen space each test has three parts. Move the point a little further along the ray. Project it onto the screen to find which pixel it falls in and how deep it is. Compare that depth with the one the depth buffer stores for the pixel. Nearer than the stored surface means the ray is still in open air; further means it has gone behind something.

The answer at a hit is the pixel itself. Its colour in the lit image becomes the reflection or the bounce, and its position tells the effect how far the ray went. SSR, introduced in 7.6 Reflections, is this march along the mirror direction. The rest of this chapter is about what goes wrong with it, and what else uses it.

2 Step length and thickness: two guesses

Two settings decide whether a march finds the right surface. The step length decides how often the ray is tested. The thickness decides how far behind a stored surface a step may land and still count as hitting it. Both are guesses about the scene, because the depth buffer records where each surface starts and nothing about how deep it is.

The guess about depth is the same thickness heuristic that screen-space AO uses to avoid dark halos (8.3). Engines expose it under different names: HDRP's SSR has Object Thickness, its SSGI has Depth Tolerance, Godot's SSR has Depth Tolerance (default 0.5). McGuire and Mara's widely used tracer gives every depth-buffer sample a thickness and recommends matching it to the objects in the scene.

3 Hi-Z: skipping empty space

A ray across a 1080p screen can cross hundreds of pixels. Reading every one is slow, and skipping some misses thin things. Hi-Z tracing gets both speed and accuracy from a depth pyramid: the chain of ever smaller copies of the depth buffer you met in Hi-Z occlusion culling (4.4), built so that each texel keeps the nearest depth of the texels below it. Culling keeps the farthest, because it asks whether an object is hidden; tracing keeps the nearest, because it asks whether a ray could hit anything.

The walk is simple. If the ray stays nearer than a cell's nearest surface all the way across the cell, nothing in that cell can stop it, so the ray jumps to the cell's far edge and moves up to a coarser mip, with bigger cells. If the ray reaches that nearest depth inside the cell, it moves to that point and drops to a finer mip. At the finest mip, one pixel, it applies the thickness test. Epic describes Lumen's screen traces as "a stackless walk of the Closest HZB mips", citing Uludag's 2014 method; AMD's FidelityFX SSSR walks a mip chain the same way.

Simplified

The lab's depth buffer is one column of 64 pixels, so each pyramid level halves one dimension. A real pyramid halves both, and a real screen is over a thousand pixels tall, so the saving grows: each extra level doubles the stride in both directions. Hi-Z cannot help once a ray is behind a surface, though; there it has to go pixel by pixel until the ray comes out again or gives up.

5 The four blind spots

Every failure of a screen-space effect comes down to one of four gaps in the depth buffer. The lab shows all four.

A ray that leaves the frame is an off-screen miss. It is the most common of the four for reflections, because the mirror of a downward-looking camera points up, out of the top of the screen, and the mirror of a surface facing the camera points back behind it. To hide the moment a reflection loses its data, tracers apply a screen edge fade: hits close to the border are blended away. HDRP calls it Screen Edge Fade Distance. It turns a hard pop into a soft fade, but the information is still gone.

Rays that pass behind objects, or into the hidden area under a thin object, sometimes reappear when the camera moves and the space becomes visible. That makes the effect view-dependent: the same spot reflects or bounces differently depending on where the camera is.

6 SSGI: the same march for bounced light

SSGI, screen-space global illumination, uses the march for diffuse GI. Each pixel sends rays over the half-sphere above its surface, takes the lit colour wherever they hit, and averages it, weighting rays near the normal more, as in 8.3. The average is one bounce of light from what is on screen, including colour bleeding from a red wall onto a white floor and light from emissive screens and signs. Godot's version is called SSIL, screen-space indirect lighting, and works the same way.

A full hemisphere needs many directions, and a frame can afford very few per pixel. HDRP's SSGI with Full Resolution on traces one ray per pixel per frame, and one ray per four pixels with it off. Unreal's standalone SSGI quality levels 1 to 4 use 8 to 12 ray steps and 4 to 32 rays. The result is noisy, so SSGI always comes with a denoiser and with reuse of earlier frames, the subject of step 8.

The colour it picks up is the lit image itself, so a second bounce appears only if last frame's image already contained the first. And it inherits all four blind spots, which matter more for diffuse light than for reflections: a bounce needs rays in every direction, and most of those leave the screen or go behind something. Epic recommends its SSGI as a way to improve indirect lighting but not as a sole indirect lighting method; Godot says SSIL is meant to be used with a proper GI method such as SDFGI or VoxelGI.

7 Screen-space shadows

The same march aimed at a light gives contact shadows, introduced in 7.5 Shadows at scale. The ray is short, a few centimetres to a few tens of centimetres, and the question is only whether anything is in the way. Short rays rarely leave the screen and rarely pass far behind anything, so contact shadows suffer least from the blind spots. Their typical failure is the thin-object one: a pole or a finger can cast a shadow that is too long behind it, because the tracer cannot tell how thick it is.

Unreal sets them per light with Contact Shadow Length; HDRP has a Contact Shadows override; URP in Unity 6.6 and Godot 4.7 have none.

8 Few rays, many frames: denoising and reprojection

One ray per pixel, or one per four, gives a speckled result. Two things clean it up. A spatial denoiser blends each pixel with neighbours at a similar depth and normal, like the bilateral filter of 8.3. Temporal reprojection reuses the traced results of earlier frames: each pixel looks up where it was last frame using motion vectors, fetches the result stored there, and blends in a little of this frame's new rays. Over a dozen frames, a dozen rays per pixel add up. This is temporal accumulation, which chapter 9.2 covers in full.

HDRP's SSGI exposes its denoiser directly: Denoise, Half Resolution Denoiser, Denoiser Radius and a Second Denoiser Pass. Its SSR's PBR Accumulation algorithm accumulates several frames, with an Accumulation Factor for how fast. Faster accumulation reacts sooner to change and shows more noise.

9 Fallbacks: who answers when the screen cannot

A ray that leaves the screen or runs out of steps still needs an answer. A ray-miss fallback supplies it from data that does not depend on the camera: a reflection probe, a light probe or lightmap baked into the level, the sky, or a world-space GI method that can trace the whole scene. It is the GI version of the reflection fallback order in 7.6. The screen answers what it can; everything else is handed down the chain.

The fallback's quality decides what failures look like. If the probe that answers off-screen reflection rays was captured in a different room, the floor reflects the wrong room. If the world-space GI behind SSGI is much darker than the on-screen bounce, lighting visibly jumps when the camera turns and the bright surface leaves the screen. A good pairing makes the two agree, so the screen-space layer adds detail without changing the overall level. The lab's Misses use buttons show how much a fallback has to cover.

4 What it costs

Screen-space tracing costs pixels times rays per pixel times reads per ray. A 1080p frame has 2,073,600 pixels. SSR at half resolution, a common setting, traces 518,400 rays; at 32 reads each that is about 16.6 million depth reads, plus a colour read for each hit. SSGI multiplies by the number of rays per pixel, which is why it runs at low ray counts and leans on denoising and reuse.

  • ResolutionEvery pixel that traces costs rays. Half resolution is a quarter of the work, and the usual default for SSGI.
  • Rays per pixelSSGI needs many directions; each extra ray per pixel is another full march.
  • Reads per rayLong rays and linear steps read more. Hi-Z cuts reads in open space but not once a ray is behind a surface.
  • Reflective areaSSR only runs on smooth enough surfaces. A wet street that fills the screen costs far more than a few puddles, and rough surfaces need more rays to avoid noise.
  • Denoising and historySpatial and temporal passes cost their own time, and temporal reuse needs correct motion vectors for everything that moves.
  • Grazing anglesRays travelling almost along a surface cross many pixels before they resolve. Lumen caps iterations for those rays.

Cheap is relative

Screen-space effects are cheap compared with tracing the whole scene, but a stack of them is not free: SSR, SSGI, contact shadows and SSAO together can take several milliseconds of a 16.7 ms frame budget. Ask for each one's cost on the target platform, and whether two of them are doing the same job, for example SSGI's occlusion on top of SSAO.

5 How it breaks

Each of these is one of the four blind spots, or the shortcuts taken to make the march affordable.

Reflections fade or vanish as the camera tilts

Cause
Off-screen misses. Tilting down pushes what the floor should reflect out of the frame; the edge fade then hides the hits that are left.
Ask for
A fallback that matches: box-projected reflection probes captured in each area, or ray-traced or Lumen reflections where it matters.

Smears and streaks behind objects

Cause
A thickness setting too large for thin objects: rays passing behind a character or pole are reported as hitting it, so its colour streaks along the reflection.
Ask for
A smaller thickness or depth tolerance, tested against the thinnest common objects, and a fallback for the rays that then pass behind.

Holes in reflections or bounced light

Cause
Rays passing behind an object, into space the camera never saw, find nothing and fall back. The gap moves as the camera moves.
Ask for
A fallback that covers the gap plausibly, and an accepted list of views where it shows. Only world-space tracing removes it.

Bounced light changes as the camera turns

Cause
SSGI only bounces light from what is on screen. Epic warns that Lumen's screen traces also cause view-dependent lighting when indirect lighting is scaled up.
Ask for
A world-space GI method or bake underneath that already holds most of the bounce, so the screen-space layer only adds detail.

Noise, boiling and shimmer

Cause
Too few rays for the denoiser to clean, often after dropping to half resolution or a lower quality preset, or a temporal filter that cannot accumulate because the camera never stops.
Ask for
The ray budget per pixel, denoiser settings and a capture of the raw and denoised passes side by side.

Grainy patches behind moving objects

Cause
Disocclusion: the newly uncovered pixels have no history, so they show one frame's worth of rays until accumulation catches up.
Ask for
A stronger spatial filter on pixels with little history, and checks that moving objects write motion vectors.

To find which layer is at fault, turn the screen-space effect off for a moment. If the problem disappears and a different, softer problem appears, the fault is in the screen-space layer; if it stays, look at the fallback underneath.

Questions to ask when reviewing screen-space work

  1. What answers a ray that leaves the screen or runs out of steps?The fallback decides what every failure looks like.
  2. What thickness or depth tolerance is set, and was it tested on the thinnest common objects?Too large smears thin objects; too small lets rays tunnel through solid ones.
  3. Does the march use linear steps or a depth pyramid, and how many reads at most?Long linear steps skip thin objects; the read limit bounds the cost.
  4. How many rays per pixel, at what resolution, and how is the result denoised?This sets both the cost and how much noise or lag to expect.
  5. Does the result change when the camera turns, and is that acceptable?View-dependent lighting is built into screen space; a world-space base keeps it small.
  6. Do all moving objects write motion vectors?Without them, temporal reuse smears history across moving things.

8 Check yourself

  1. A puddle reflects a street lamp perfectly until the camera looks down at the puddle, when the reflection disappears. Which blind spot is this, and what should the reflection show instead?

    Show answer

    An off-screen miss. Looking down pushes the lamp out of the top of the frame, so the reflected ray leaves the screen with nothing to find. The fallback should answer, typically a reflection probe captured nearby, so the puddle still shows a plausible, if less exact, lamp.

  2. Raising SSR's thickness removed holes in a reflection, but now thin railings leave long streaks. Why do both happen, and what is the trade?

    Show answer

    The depth buffer stores where each surface starts but not how deep it is, so the tracer guesses. A larger guess stops rays that were tunnelling through solid objects, which fills the holes, but it also stops rays passing behind thin railings, which are reported as hitting them and smear their colour. The fix is a thickness tuned to the scene plus a fallback for the rays that pass behind.

  3. Why does Hi-Z make a march both faster and more accurate than long linear steps?

    Show answer

    Its pyramid keeps the nearest depth of each region, so while the ray is in front of that depth it can skip the whole region in one read. Near surfaces it drops to single pixels, so it never steps past a surface and cannot skip a thin object, which long linear steps can.
