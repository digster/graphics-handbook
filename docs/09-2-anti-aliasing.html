9.2 Anti-aliasing

A pixel that tests one point gets every edge either fully right or fully wrong, so straight lines turn into stairs and thin things break up. Anti-aliasing gives each pixel a better idea of what it covers: more samples in the same frame, a guess from the finished image, or samples gathered over many frames. This chapter explains each family, from SSAA to TAA, what it costs and the artifacts it leaves behind.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The GPU decides whether a triangle covers a pixel by testing one point, the pixel's sample point (5.3). The answer is yes or no. A pixel that is 40% covered by a dark rock shows either all rock or no rock, so a straight edge becomes a staircase: edge aliasing, the jaggies.

The general problem is aliasing: any detail finer than the pixel grid comes out wrong when each pixel looks at only one point. Edges become stairs, thin wires become dashes, fine texture patterns become false bands (the texture aliasing that mipmaps fight, 6.2), and highlights smaller than a pixel sparkle. That last one has its own name: specular aliasing. In a still screenshot aliasing looks rough. In motion it is worse, because each pixel's answer flips as things move, so edges crawl and details flicker.

Anti-aliasing, AA, is any technique that gets closer to the true coverage. There are three families. Take more samples inside each pixel in the same frame (SSAA, MSAA). Look at the finished image and smooth the stair-steps you can see (FXAA, SMAA). Or take one slightly different sample each frame and blend frames together (TAA). Almost every current game uses the third, often inside an upscaler (9.3), and the first two survive where TAA's side effects are not acceptable.

An everyday comparison

You want to know how much of each floor tile a rug covers, and you may look at one spot per tile. Look at the centre of every tile and each one is rug or no rug. Look at four spots per tile and you can say a quarter, a half, three quarters. Or come back every day, look at a different spot each time, and keep a running average: after a week you know almost as well, for the cost of one look a day. That works until someone moves the rug.

The problem it solves

Without anti-aliasing, edges stair-step, wires and fences break up, shiny surfaces sparkle and the whole image shimmers when the camera moves. Players read that as cheap or noisy, and it gets worse at lower resolutions and on large screens.

Anti-aliasing removes most of it, at the price of some memory and milliseconds, and in TAA's case some softness and the risk of ghosting.

Why a lead cares

The anti-aliasing method shapes the whole look of the game: crisp or soft, stable or shimmering. It costs from a fraction of a millisecond to several. TAA is also a foundation that other effects lean on: ambient occlusion, reflections, hair and dithered transparency are often built noisy on purpose and rely on TAA to clean them up, so switching it off can break them. And the upscalers in 9.3 are TAA with extra steps, so the same bugs, ghosting and smearing, follow you there.

3 How it works

Every method below answers the same question: how much of this pixel does each thing cover? They differ in where they find the extra information: more samples now, the finished image, or earlier frames.

1 Why one sample per pixel aliases

Follow one pixel while an edge slides across it. Its true coverage rises smoothly from 0 to 1. A single sample at the centre reads 0 until the edge reaches the centre, then jumps to 1. Every pixel along an edge makes that jump at a different moment, which is the crawl you see on moving edges. With four samples the pixel climbs in quarters, and the steps are small enough to look smooth.

Shading aliases the same way. A shiny, bumpy surface has highlights smaller than a pixel; whether a pixel catches one depends on the single normal its sample happens to read. The fixes there work on the material as well as the samples: mipmaps for textures, and for highlights, raising roughness where the normal map or the geometry bends a lot within a pixel, so the highlight becomes wider and dimmer instead of flickering. HDRP calls this Geometric Specular AA; Godot runs a screen-space roughness limiter by default.

2 SSAA: render more pixels, then shrink

SSAA, supersample anti-aliasing, is the brute-force answer: render the whole frame at a higher resolution and average it down. At 2 × 2 the GPU shades four samples for every final pixel, so edges, thin geometry, textures and highlights are all anti-aliased together. It is the reference everything else is compared with, and it is too expensive for most games: four times the pixel shading and four times the render target memory. It survives as a quality option, often called resolution scale above 100%, and in photo modes.

3 MSAA: test many points, shade once

MSAA, multisample anti-aliasing, is a hardware feature that keeps SSAA's extra coverage tests and drops most of its extra shading. At 4× the rasterizer tests coverage and depth at four points per pixel, but the pixel shader runs once for each triangle that touches the pixel, and its colour is copied to the samples that triangle covers. At the end a resolve step averages the samples into one colour per pixel.

MSAA fits a forward renderer, which shades as it draws. A deferred renderer writes a G-buffer first and lights it later, so with MSAA every G-buffer layer is stored once per sample and lighting must run per sample along edges. Brian Karis, explaining why Unreal Engine 4 moved to temporal AA, gives exactly that reason, and adds that most of their aliasing was inside triangles, where MSAA does nothing. Unreal and HDRP offer MSAA only with forward rendering for this reason. On tile-based mobile GPUs the extra samples can stay in on-chip memory and be resolved before they are written out, which makes MSAA cheap there: Unity's URP documentation says MSAA can be the cheaper option on tiled GPUs. Leaves and fences drawn with alpha testing need an extra trick, alpha to coverage, which turns the texture's alpha into a sample mask.

4 FXAA and SMAA: smooth the finished image

Post-process anti-aliasing takes another route: let the frame render aliased, then look for stair-steps in the finished image and blend across them. It needs no extra samples and works with any renderer. The idea goes back to Alexander Reshetov's morphological anti-aliasing (MLAA) in 2009; two descendants are in every engine.

FXAA, by Timothy Lottes at NVIDIA, is a single full-screen pass. For each pixel it compares brightness with its four neighbours; if the contrast is high it finds the edge's direction, walks along it to find where the step ends, and blends the pixel with its neighbour across the edge by an amount that depends on its position along the step. Lottes's paper gives its cost as under 1 ms for a 1920 × 1200 frame on a GeForce GTX 480, and says to run it after tone mapping, on the display-ready image, and before the interface.

SMAA, by Jorge Jimenez and colleagues (2012), works in three passes: detect edges, measure the shape of each stair pattern and look up how much each pixel should blend, then blend. It recognises more shapes than FXAA, including diagonals and sharp corners, so it blurs less, and it has variants that add MSAA samples (S2x) or a second, jittered frame (T2x).

That last sentence is the limit of the whole family. A wire that broke into dashes has no stair pattern to read; the missing pieces were never rendered. And because each frame is processed alone, a crawling edge is smoothed into a crawling soft edge: post-process AA reduces stair-steps without making motion stable.

6 TAA: one jittered sample a frame, blended over time

TAA, temporal anti-aliasing, spreads supersampling over time. Each frame it moves the whole camera by a tiny offset, less than a pixel, so every pixel samples a slightly different point than last frame. That offset is subpixel jitter. It costs nothing to render: the offset is added to the projection matrix (3.4), and the rest of the renderer does not need to know.

The offsets come from a short repeating list. Karis used the Halton sequence with bases 2 and 3, which places each new point in the biggest gap the earlier ones left, so after any number of frames the samples cover the pixel evenly. NVIDIA, AMD and Intel all recommend Halton jitter for their upscalers too.

Each frame, TAA blends the new image into the result it kept from last frame, its history buffer. Storing sixteen frames would be expensive, so it keeps one image and updates it: new result = w × this frame + (1 − w) × history. With w around 0.1, the most recent frame counts for 10%, the one before for 9%, the one before that for 8.1%, and so on: an exponential moving average that behaves like an average of many frames for the cost of one stored image. This is temporal accumulation, and TAA is its best-known use.

  • Switch the object to Moving: a grey trail follows it. Turn on Neighbourhood clamp and the trail shrinks to about one pixel; the readout's ghost count drops from 23 to 7 at frame 8.
  • Raise the minimum weight: the trail fades faster, and in the still scene the edge stays rougher. Lower it and the opposite happens. That trade is the core tuning decision of every TAA.
  • Back in the still scene, turn the clamp on and watch the mast. Clamping also throws away good history for features thinner than a pixel, which is where TAA flicker comes from.

7 Following motion: motion vectors and reprojection

Averaging only works if each pixel blends history that belongs to the same surface. When the camera or an object moves, the surface that was under pixel (100, 50) last frame may now be under (104, 50). So before blending, TAA does reprojection: for each pixel it works out where its surface was last frame and reads the history there.

For camera movement the renderer can compute that from the depth buffer and last frame's camera. For objects that move by themselves, characters, vehicles, doors, each object writes its own screen-space movement into a buffer while it is drawn. That buffer holds motion vectors, also called the velocity buffer: two numbers per pixel, usually 16-bit each, so 4 bytes per pixel and 8.3 MB at 1080p.

Accuracy matters. Karis notes that a small error in motion vectors streaks an otherwise static image, and that an anti-aliased edge lies partly outside the object that made it, so TAA reads the motion vector of the nearest surface in a small neighbourhood to keep edges attached to moving objects. Motion vectors are shared with motion blur (9.4) and every temporal upscaler (9.3), so their quality pays off three times.

8 When history is wrong: disocclusion and ghosting

Some pixels have no valid past. When an object moves, the surface it uncovers was hidden last frame. This is disocclusion. The wall's motion vector says it did not move, so reprojection reads the history at the same place, which still shows the object. Blended in at 90%, that becomes ghosting: a fading copy trailing behind anything that moves.

Every TAA therefore needs history rejection: ways to detect bad history and trust it less. Comparing depth, or the speed stored with the history against the current speed, catches some cases; HDRP exposes the latter as Speed rejection. The method that made TAA practical is neighbourhood clamping, which Karis credits to Lottes and to Hugh Malan. A correct anti-aliased pixel is a blend of the colours around it, so before blending, TAA limits the history to the range of colours found in this frame's 3 × 3 neighbourhood of the pixel.

Production TAA refines the box. Karis clamps in YCoCg colour space, where brightness and colour are separate, and clips the history towards the neighbourhood's average instead of clamping each channel, which avoids colours collecting in the corners of the box. Unity's Variance Clamp Scale sets the size of that colour range. Clamping has a price. A feature thinner than a pixel may be absent from all nine neighbours in a given frame, so its correct history is clamped away, and it flickers. Anti-flicker heuristics, exposed for example as HDRP's Anti-flickering slider and Unreal's TSR flicker rejection, trade that against ghosting.

Simplified

The time machine uses one darkness value per pixel instead of colour, motion vectors that are exact for the one moving object, and a plain min-max clamp. Real TAA also filters each sample with a small kernel that weights it by distance from the pixel centre, handles colour and HDR brightness, and resamples history with sharper filters than bilinear.

9 The price of TAA: blur, sharpening and mip bias

TAA trades stair-steps for softness. Reading history at a position that falls between pixels blends neighbours every frame, and the error builds up while things move. The reconstruction filter that weights samples spans more than one pixel. Clamping pulls fine detail towards its neighbours. The result is TAA blur: a softer image than the same frame without AA, and softer still in motion.

Engines counter the blur in three ways. A sharpening filter after TAA, such as AMD's Contrast Adaptive Sharpening, which URP and HDRP both offer. Sharper history resampling: HDRP's History Sharpening reads history with a bicubic filter. And a negative mip bias (6.2), so textures are sampled from slightly sharper mip levels on the grounds that TAA's averaging will remove the extra shimmer; URP exposes this as Mip Bias on the camera. Karis lists the same tools, and the upscalers in 9.3 make the mip bias mandatory.

10 Where anti-aliasing sits in the frame

Each family runs at a different point. MSAA happens while geometry is rasterized. TAA runs after lighting, on HDR values and before the tone map (9.1), so that effects after it, such as bloom, receive a stable image. Very bright pixels would dominate an average of HDR values, so Karis weights each sample by its brightness to tame them. FXAA and SMAA run on the tone-mapped image, before the interface is drawn.

The firewall is why TAA became the default. Renderers now compute ambient occlusion, reflections and soft shadows with a few rotated samples per pixel and let TAA's accumulation average them across frames, as the GTAO paper did (8.3). It is also why removing TAA from a modern renderer is rarely a simple switch.

The usual trade

On PC and console, TAA or a temporal upscaler is the default because nothing else handles shading and sub-pixel detail for the price. Offer a sharpening slider and, for players who hate the softness, FXAA or SMAA as an alternative. On mobile, 2× or 4× MSAA with a forward renderer is often cheaper and sharper, and FXAA is the fallback. Competitive games sometimes prefer sharp and slightly aliased over soft and stable.

5 How it breaks

Most anti-aliasing bugs are TAA bugs, and most TAA bugs are history from the wrong place or something the history cannot follow.

Ghost trails behind moving objects

Cause
Disoccluded pixels blend history that still shows the object, and rejection or clamping is too weak to remove it; or the object writes no motion vectors.
Ask for
A check that every moving thing writes motion vectors, tighter neighbourhood clamping or speed rejection, and a higher minimum weight for the new frame where ghosting shows.

A soft image, smeared in motion

Cause
TAA blur from history resampling and clamping, with no sharpening or mip bias to compensate; worse at low frame rates, where each frame moves further.
Ask for
A sharpening pass with an exposed strength, a negative mip bias tied to the AA mode, and sharper history resampling where the engine offers it.

Flicker on thin detail

Cause
Wires, fences, grass blades and bright highlights thinner than a pixel are missing from the neighbourhood some frames, so their history is clamped away, and they blink.
Ask for
Anti-flicker settings in the TAA, geometric specular AA or roughness limiting for shiny detail, and LODs or thicker cards so distant thin geometry stays at least a pixel wide.

Wobbling interface or effects

Cause
Something drawn after TAA still uses the jittered camera: interface elements in world space, or effects that read the jittered depth buffer. They shake by the jitter each frame.
Ask for
The interface drawn after TAA with an unjittered projection, and a list of passes after TAA that read depth, so each can use the unjittered matrix.

Smeared particles and animated materials

Cause
Particles, scrolling textures and transparent effects write no motion vectors and have no single history, so TAA drags old frames along with them.
Ask for
Velocity output for vertex animation, a responsive or reactive mask that lowers history weight under small transparent effects, as Karis's Responsive AA flag does, or drawing them after TAA.

7 Say it to your AI

Anti-aliasing briefs work when they name the engine and the exact method in use, describe the artifact with where and when it appears, and ask how to verify the fix with a capture or a debug view.

Questions to ask when reviewing anti-aliasing work

  1. Which method runs on each platform, and at which point in the frame?TAA belongs before tone mapping, FXAA and SMAA after it, and the interface after all of them.
  2. Does everything that moves write motion vectors, including skinned meshes, vertex animation and scrolling materials?Missing vectors are the most common cause of smearing.
  3. How is stale history rejected: clamping, speed or depth checks, masks for transparent effects?Without rejection every moving object ghosts.
  4. Is there a sharpening pass and a negative mip bias, and are they exposed to players?TAA's softness is a common complaint; these are the standard answers.
  5. Which effects depend on TAA to look clean?Turning TAA off or swapping it for FXAA can expose noisy AO, shadows and dithered transparency.

8 Check yourself

  1. With MSAA 4× on, a game's geometry edges look smooth but a shiny, bumpy floor still sparkles. Why?

    Show answer

    MSAA tests coverage at four points but runs the pixel shader about once per pixel, so anything decided inside the shader, here a highlight from a bumpy normal map, is still sampled once. That is specular aliasing. It needs roughness filtering (such as geometric specular AA), supersampling or a temporal method.

  2. A character walks across a bright wall and leaves a dark fading trail. The camera is still. What is happening, and what removes most of it?

    Show answer

    Disocclusion ghosting. The wall the character uncovers has a zero motion vector, so TAA reads its own history, which still shows the character, and blends it in. Neighbourhood clamping limits history to the colours around the pixel this frame, which removes the trail except right next to the character; speed or depth rejection and a higher new-frame weight help further.

  3. Why can FXAA not fix a power line that has broken into dashes in the distance, while TAA can?

    Show answer

    FXAA only sees the finished image, where the missing pieces of the line were never rendered. TAA jitters the sample point each frame, so over several frames some samples land on the line in every pixel it crosses, and the average recovers it at partial brightness.
