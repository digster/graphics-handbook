5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw

The pixel shader is the small program that colours every covered pixel. A frame usually runs it more times than the screen has pixels, and the gap between the two, overdraw, is one of the most common reasons a game misses its frame budget.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The rasterizer from 5.3 turns each triangle into fragments, one per covered pixel. The pixel shader then runs once for every fragment and decides its colour: it reads textures, works out lighting and returns a result. If three surfaces cover the same pixel, the shader may run three times there and only one answer survives.

Shading the same pixel more than once is overdraw. Some of it is wasted work that the GPU can avoid by drawing near things first or by working out what is visible before shading anything. Some of it cannot be avoided: you see through smoke, glass and foliage edges, so every layer must be shaded and mixed.

Everyday comparison

A decorator paints a detailed mural on a wall, and then the client hangs posters over half of it. All the careful painting under the posters is wasted. A sensible decorator asks where the posters go first and paints only the bare wall.

Painting is the pixel shader. Knowing where the posters go is the depth test. Tracing paper laid over the mural is transparency: you can see through it, so every sheet has to be drawn.

The problem it solves

A 1080p screen has about 2 million pixels, and a modern pixel shader can be hundreds of instructions long. Shading every surface that covers a pixel, hidden or not, would multiply that cost by however many surfaces overlap.

Early depth testing, sensible draw order and pre-passes keep opaque scenes close to one run per pixel, so the budget goes on the layers you can actually see.

Why a lead cares

Pixel shading cost is resolution × overdraw × shader cost. Two of those three are content decisions: how many particles an effect stacks, how much of a foliage card is empty, how expensive a material is. Every engine has a view that shows overdraw and shader cost as a heat map, and reading one is a skill worth having before you sign off an effect, a forest or a new material.

3 How it works

Follow the fragments from the rasterizer to the screen. There are nine steps.

1 What a pixel shader reads and writes

A pixel shader, called a fragment shader in OpenGL and Vulkan, is a shader that runs once for each fragment: one triangle's claim on one pixel, with the values blended from the triangle's corners. It reads those blended values, samples textures and reads constants such as light positions. It writes a colour, sometimes several to different images at once, and can optionally write its own depth or discard the fragment entirely.

2 Overdraw: more runs than pixels

Every surface that covers a pixel produces a fragment there. Without help, each fragment is shaded, and the ones further back are simply overwritten by the ones in front. Overdraw is that extra shading, usually given as an average: an overdraw of 2.5 means 2.5 pixel shader runs for every pixel on screen.

3 Early-Z: skip what is already hidden

The depth buffer stores, for every pixel, how far away the nearest surface drawn so far is (5.5 covers it in full). The depth test compares each new fragment against it and throws away fragments that are further away. Logically this test happens after the pixel shader. Early-Z is the GPU running it before the shader instead, whenever the result cannot change, so hidden fragments cost almost nothing.

Early-Z only helps if near things are drawn before far things, so renderers try to draw opaque objects roughly front to back. It also needs the shader to behave. A shader that can discard pixels, such as a leaf cut-out, means the GPU cannot write depth until the shader has decided; it can still test early, but its own pixels block nothing until they are shaded. A shader that writes its own depth turns early testing off completely.

4 The depth pre-pass

Sorting is never perfect: objects interlock, and large meshes are both near and far. A depth pre-pass removes the guesswork. The engine first draws every opaque object with depth only and no colour, which is cheap. Then it draws them again with full shading, and the depth test only lets through the fragment whose depth matches the stored one. Each visible pixel is shaded exactly once, in any order.

The price is drawing the geometry twice: every vertex is processed again and every fragment is depth-tested twice. With expensive shaders and lots of overlap that is a bargain. With cheap shaders, little overlap or very heavy geometry, it can cost more than it saves, which is why engines make it a setting.

5 Try it: layers, order and transparency

The heat map below counts pixel shader runs at every pixel of a pile of layers. It opens with opaque layers drawn back to front, the worst case. Switch to front to back and watch the heat drop to one run everywhere. Then switch to transparent smoke. Transparent layers do not write depth, because you must see what is behind them, so early-Z and the pre-pass cannot remove any of them. Every layer is shaded wherever it covers.

Simplified

The demo tests one sample per pixel and treats early-Z as perfect. Real GPUs reject hidden fragments a block at a time and shade in 2 × 2 quads (step 7), and tile-based mobile GPUs order the work differently (3.5). The pattern is the same: order and pre-passes help opaque surfaces, and nothing removes visible layers.

6 Alpha-tested foliage

Trees and grass are usually built from flat cards with a leaf texture, and the texture's alpha channel says which pixels are leaf and which are empty. This is alpha-tested foliage, also called cut-out or masked. The rasterizer covers the whole card, so the pixel shader runs on the whole card, reads the alpha, and discards the empty pixels. Layer a few cards and the empty corners of each one are shaded again and again.

The usual fixes are content fixes: cards cut closer to the leaf shape with a few more vertices, fewer and larger leaves on distant LODs, and impostors far away. Engines also put cut-out materials in the depth pre-pass so the full material runs once per visible pixel; Unreal's Early Z-pass setting and Godot's Alpha Scissor mode do exactly that.

7 Pixels are shaded in 2 × 2 quads

GPUs never shade a single pixel on its own. They shade a pixel quad, a 2 × 2 block, and pack 8 or 16 quads into one group of 32 or 64 lanes that run the same instructions together. Pixels of the quad that the triangle does not cover still run, as helper lanes, and their results are thrown away. The reason is step 8: the shader needs its neighbours' values.

A quad touched by two triangles is shaded twice, once for each. That is quad overdraw. It is small for big triangles and large for small or thin ones, because almost every quad they touch is shared with a neighbour.

8 Derivatives choose the mip level

Within a quad, every pixel has a neighbour to its right or left and one above or below. Subtracting their values gives screen-space derivatives: how fast a value changes from one pixel to the next. HLSL calls them ddx and ddy, GLSL dFdx and dFdy.

Texture sampling uses them automatically. If neighbouring pixels land four texels apart in a texture, reading it at full size would skip detail and sparkle, so the sampler reads a smaller copy, a mip level, where they land about one texel apart. 6.2 Filtering and mipmaps covers mips properly. For now, the point is that derivatives are why quads exist, and why a texture read inside a branch that only some pixels of a quad take can pick the wrong mip.

9 Seeing it: overdraw and shader complexity views

Every engine can draw the frame as a heat map. A shader complexity view colours each pixel by how much shading it costs, from cheap to expensive; Unreal's Shader Complexity runs green through red to white. Overdraw views add up the surfaces covering each pixel. Quad overdraw views count how many times each 2 × 2 quad is shaded. They answer different questions, so learn which one you are looking at.

4 What it costs

Pixel shading time follows one product:

pixel shader time ≈ pixels on screen × average runs per pixel × cost of one run

At 1080p, one screen of shading is 2,073,600 runs. Here is how a typical frame builds up, counted in screens of shading so the arithmetic works at any resolution.

What makes the bill grow

  • ResolutionEvery row above scales with pixel count. 4K is four times 1080p.
  • Transparent layersSmoke, fire, glass, water surfaces and UI panels are all shaded at every layer they cover.
  • Empty card areaFoliage and particle cards pay for their whole rectangle, empty parts included.
  • Shader costTexture reads, lights evaluated and instruction count set the cost of each run.
  • Draw orderOpaque objects drawn back to front defeat early-Z; a pre-pass removes the dependency.
  • Small and thin trianglesQuad overdraw adds helper lanes and shades shared quads twice.

In proportion

Vertex work grows with triangle count and pixel work grows with resolution and overdraw. When a frame is too slow on the GPU, lowering the internal resolution is usually the first lever engines pull, which is why upscalers exist (9.3). The second lever is content: fewer, smaller and cheaper transparent layers, and foliage cards that fit their leaves.

5 How it breaks

Pixel shading problems rarely look wrong on screen. They show up as frame time that jumps with the camera, the effect or the resolution. These five patterns cover most of them.

Frame rate drops inside smoke and explosions

Cause
Many large transparent particles stacked in front of the camera. Every layer is shaded at every pixel it covers, and close-up particles cover a lot of pixels.
Ask for
An overdraw or shader complexity view of the worst moment, then fewer or smaller particles, sprites trimmed to their shape, cheaper materials or lower-resolution rendering for the effect.

Forests cost far more than their triangle count suggests

Cause
Alpha-tested leaf cards: the shader runs across every card, and discarded pixels block nothing behind them, so the empty corners are shaded layer after layer.
Ask for
The ratio of card area to leaf area, cards trimmed closer to the leaves, cut-outs in the depth pre-pass, and simpler distant tree LODs or impostors.

GPU time follows resolution, not what is on screen

Cause
The frame is limited by pixel work: expensive materials, full-screen passes or heavy overdraw. Pixel work scales with pixel count, so 4K costs about four times 1080p.
Ask for
A GPU capture with per-pass timings at two resolutions, the most expensive materials by screen area, and a resolution-scaling plan for the target hardware.

Turning on the depth pre-pass made it slower

Cause
Little overdraw, cheap shaders or very heavy geometry: drawing everything twice costs more than the shading it saves. Unity, for one, does not use depth priming on tile-based mobile GPUs at all.
Ask for
Timings with and without the pre-pass in typical and worst-case views on each target, and a per-platform setting if they disagree.

A thin line of blur or noise along a texture seam

Cause
UVs jump inside a 2 × 2 quad, for example where a shader wraps them with frac(), or a texture is read inside a branch only some of the quad takes. The derivatives are wrong, so the sampler picks the wrong mip.
Ask for
Explicit gradients or an explicit mip level at the seam (SampleGrad or SampleLevel in HLSL), or texture reads moved out of divergent branches.

To confirm any of these, use the engine's heat map views first, then a frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX. Their pixel history shows every fragment that touched one pixel, including the ones that were shaded and then overwritten.

Read the view's rules

Additive overdraw views count every surface, including hidden ones that early-Z would reject, so they overstate opaque overdraw and are most useful for transparent layers. Godot's ignores custom shaders. Confirm anything important in a frame capture.

7 Say it to your AI

Good briefs about pixel cost give the frame time and where it goes, say what the heat map views show, and ask for estimates and a way to measure. Adapt these.

BriefUse when an effect drops the frame rate close up
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7, deferred. Target: PS5 at 60 fps, 1440p internal resolution.
When the camera is close to our large explosion, GPU frame time rises from 14 ms to 23 ms. The effect has 6 emitters and up to 400 particles, mostly large soft smoke sprites with a lit translucent material. Shader Complexity shows the explosion white, and Quad Overdraw shows 10 or more layers at its centre.

1. Estimate how many layers cover the worst pixels and what one layer of each material costs.
2. Propose changes that cut overdraw with little change to the look: fewer or smaller sprites, sprite geometry trimmed to the visible shape, a cheaper material for background layers, lower-resolution rendering for the effect, fading particles near the camera.
3. For each change, estimate the GPU saving and the visual risk.
4. Tell me how to measure before and after in a GPU capture.
  • The two frame times and the views give the AI the size of the problem and its cause.
  • Listing options keeps it on overdraw instead of generic optimisation advice.
  • Asking for visual risk protects the art direction.
InvestigationUse when foliage-heavy views are GPU-bound
Engine: Godot 4.7, Forward+ renderer. Our forest runs at 45 fps on our minimum-spec PC while an open field runs at 90. Trees use StandardMaterial3D with Transparency set to Alpha Scissor on leaf cards; Display Overdraw shows the canopy as the brightest part of the screen.

Find where the GPU time goes. Report: whether the depth pre-pass is on, the average ratio of card area to leaf area per tree type, how many card layers cover a typical canopy pixel, and the cost of the leaf material. Then propose fixes in order of saving per hour of art time, and how to verify each one.
  • Two frame rates for two scenes isolate the forest as the cost.
  • Asking for card-to-leaf ratios points the AI at the usual waste.
  • Ranking by saving per hour of art time makes the answer plannable.

Questions to ask when reviewing shader and effects work

  1. Which materials are transparent, and how much of the screen can they cover at once?Every transparent layer is shaded wherever it covers; nothing removes it.
  2. Is a depth pre-pass on for this platform, and was it measured on and off?It saves shading in busy scenes and costs time in simple ones.
  3. Does this shader discard pixels or write its own depth?Discard delays depth writes; writing depth turns early-Z off.
  4. How much of each foliage or particle card is empty?The shader runs on the whole card, so trimming the card cuts the waste.
  5. Does any texture read happen inside a branch, or on UVs that jump?Broken derivatives pick the wrong mip and leave lines along seams.

8 Check yourself

  1. Three opaque walls cover the same pixel. How many times does the pixel shader run there if they are drawn back to front, and front to back with early-Z?

    Show answer

    Back to front, three times: each wall is nearer than the last and passes the depth test, and two results are overwritten. Front to back, once: the nearest wall writes its depth and the other two are rejected before shading.

  2. An explosion has ten layers of smoke. Why does sorting it or adding a depth pre-pass not reduce its shading cost?

    Show answer

    Transparent layers do not write depth, because you must see what is behind them, so nothing can reject one layer for another. Every layer is shaded wherever it covers. Only fewer or smaller layers, or a cheaper material, reduce the cost.

  3. A leaf card covers 64 × 64 pixels on screen and the leaf fills 40% of it. How many pixel shader runs does it cost, and how many are thrown away?

    Show answer

    4,096 runs, one per covered pixel of the card. About 2,460 of them, 60%, are discarded. Cutting the card closer to the leaf shape is the usual fix.
