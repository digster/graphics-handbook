6.5 PBR materials

A material tells the renderer how a surface treats light: what colour it is, whether it is metal, how rough it is. Physically based materials state those things as a few measurable numbers, so the same asset looks right in a sunlit street, a dark cave and every scene in between.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Everything you see is light that bounced off something. A material is the set of instructions that tells the renderer how one kind of surface bounces it: painted wood, wet stone, brushed steel. In practice it is a shader plus the textures and numbers that feed it.

Physically based rendering, or PBR, means those instructions follow how light really behaves. Instead of an artist painting in a highlight and tuning it for one scene, the material states properties of the surface itself. Its albedo, or base colour, is how much of each colour of light it sends back. A second number says whether it is a metal, a third how rough it is at a microscopic scale. The renderer works out the rest for whatever light arrives.

Everyday comparison

A recipe written in grams works in any kitchen. A recipe that says "add flour until it looks right" works only for the cook who wrote it, in their own kitchen.

Older game materials were the second kind. They were tuned by eye under one level's lighting. Moved to a brighter level they glowed; moved to a darker one they went flat.

The problem it solves

PBR materials are the first kind. They describe the surface, so they hold up under any light, at any time of day, in any level.

That is what lets a studio share one material library across teams, buy materials from outsourcers, scan them from real surfaces, and still have everything match in the same scene.

Why a lead cares

Materials are where art direction meets the renderer. A consistent PBR setup lets dozens of artists, outsourcers and AI tools make assets that sit together in one scene. Inconsistent values are one of the most common reasons a game looks cheap: glowing white props, black metal, gold that looks like plastic. They are cheap to prevent with validation on day one and expensive to fix once thousands of assets exist.

3 How it works

A PBR material is built from a handful of physical ideas. Each one below adds one, and the interactive in step 5 puts them together.

1 Two things light does at a surface

When light reaches a surface, some of it bounces straight off, the way it does off a mirror. That is specular reflection. The rest goes into the material.

In a non-metal such as plastic, wood, stone or skin, the light that goes in bumps into pigment and particles. Some colours are absorbed, and the rest comes back out in all directions. That is diffuse reflection, and it is where most of a non-metal's visible colour comes from.

Metals behave differently. Light that goes into a metal is absorbed almost at once, so metals have no diffuse light at all. They reflect much more at the surface, and that reflection is coloured: gold reflects nearly all red light and about a third of blue, so everything it reflects looks yellow.

That one physical difference is the metallic input. It switches between the two behaviours: 0 means a non-metal, 1 means a metal. Real surfaces are one or the other, so the value should be 0 or 1 almost everywhere. In-between values are for places where two materials blend, such as the edge where paint chips off steel or a patch of rust.

2 Roughness and microfacets

Up close, no surface is flat. Even polished plastic is a landscape of tiny slopes, far too small to see. Renderers model this with microfacets: the surface is treated as countless tiny flat mirrors, each tilted a little, all much smaller than a pixel. Roughness says how much those mirrors disagree about which way to face.

On a smooth surface the facets line up, so reflected light stays together. You see a small, sharp, bright highlight and clear reflections. On a rough surface the facets point every which way, so the same reflected light spreads over many directions. The highlight grows wider and dimmer and reflections blur. Nothing is lost; the same light covers a bigger area.

The formula that describes how the facets are tilted is called the normal distribution. Almost every engine now uses GGX, also called Trowbridge-Reitz: Trowbridge and Reitz published it in 1975, and Walter and colleagues derived it again in 2007 and named it GGX. Its long, soft tail around the highlight matches measured materials better than older formulas, and artists liked the natural glow it gives.

Simplified

Most engines, and glTF, square the roughness you type before it reaches GGX (α = roughness²), a remapping from Disney that makes the slider feel even. Some tools store smoothness or glossiness instead, which runs the other way: Unity's Lit shaders use smoothness, which is 1 minus roughness. The physics is the same; only the direction of the slider differs.

3 Fresnel: reflections grow at grazing angles

Stand at the edge of a lake and look down: you see the stones on the bottom. Look across to the far shore: the water is a mirror of the sky and hills. Same water, different angle. This is the Fresnel effect, named after the physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel: every surface reflects more light the closer to edge-on you look at it.

The starting point of that curve is F0, the reflectance at normal incidence: how much light a surface reflects when you look at it straight on. Water's F0 is about 2%. Most non-metals sit between 2% and 5%, which is why engines simply use 4% for all of them. Metals start far higher, from about 56% for iron to over 90% for silver and aluminium. At grazing angles every surface heads towards 100%.

Real-time renderers draw this curve with Schlick's approximation, a short formula that needs only F0 and the angle. Fresnel is why almost every object in a PBR renderer has a faint bright rim, and why a material with the wrong F0 can look right head-on and wrong at its edges.

4 The metal/roughness inputs

Put those ideas together and you have the metal/roughness workflow, the standard in Unreal, Unity, Godot and the glTF file format: base colour, metallic and roughness, with a normal map for fine surface shape (chapter 6.4).

The shader turns those inputs into physical quantities. For a non-metal, the base colour is the diffuse colour and F0 is fixed at 4%. For a metal, the base colour becomes F0 and the diffuse colour is black. Metallic blends between the two.

The older specular/glossiness workflow stores the derived quantities directly: a diffuse colour map, a specular colour map holding F0, and a glossiness map. It gives artists direct control over F0 for non-metals, at the price of an extra colour texture and an easy route to values no real surface has. Both describe the same physics. Most engines, tools and asset stores have settled on metal/roughness, and Unity still offers both.

Unreal's extra input

Unreal's classic material adds a Specular input, default 0.5, which means 4%. Epic's guidance is to leave it alone; it is mostly used for small-scale shadowing in cracks. Since Unreal Engine 5.7, new projects use Substrate materials, whose Slab node takes Diffuse Albedo, F0 and F90 directly. Classic materials still work and are converted when they compile.

5 The BRDF: one formula for the whole surface

The function that combines these pieces is the BRDF, the bidirectional reflectance distribution function. Give it the direction light arrives from and the direction you look from, and it returns how much of that light leaves towards you. The renderer evaluates it for every pixel and every light, usually in the pixel shader.

Most real-time PBR uses the same one, set out for games in Epic's Unreal Engine 4 course notes in 2013. A diffuse part, Lambert, sends the same amount of light in every direction. A specular part, the Cook-Torrance microfacet model, is made of three terms: one for the facets, one for Fresnel and one for facets that hide each other.

Karis computes Schlick's power with a faster exponential that gives the same result, and uses k = α ÷ 2 instead for reflections of the surroundings. Engines differ in small ways: glTF also scales the diffuse part by (1 − F), and Godot uses Burley's diffuse model by default instead of Lambert. Chapter 7.2 compares shading models.

6 Energy conservation

A surface cannot send out more light than reaches it. That rule is energy conservation, and PBR keeps it in two places. Inside the specular part, GGX is normalised: when roughness spreads a highlight it also dims it, so the total stays the same. Between the two parts, light reflected at the surface is light that never went in to become diffuse.

With metal/roughness no value from 0 to 1 can break the rule, which is a large part of why it won. With specular/glossiness it is easy: pair a white diffuse map with a bright specular map and the surface returns more light than it receives. It glows in every scene, and no light setting fixes it.

7 Plausible base colours

Real non-metals cover a narrow range. Fresh snow, one of the brightest natural surfaces, reflects about 81% of light. Charcoal and fresh asphalt reflect about 2%. Very little is darker, and few common surfaces are brighter. Base colour textures store these values with the sRGB curve (3.2 Colour done right), so in a paint program the range looks wider: 2% is stored as 39 out of 255, and 81% as 232.

Studios turn this into a plausible albedo range that their validation tools check. Adobe's Substance PBR guide recommends that non-metal base colours go no darker than 30 to 50 in sRGB (about 1.3% to 3.2% reflectance) and no brighter than 240 (about 87%). A value outside the range is not always wrong, but it should be a choice someone made.

No light in the base colour

Base colour must hold the surface's colour only: no shadows, no highlights, no darkening in crevices. Photos and hand-painted textures often contain all three. The renderer adds its own light and shadow on top, so painted ones appear twice and stay put when the light moves. Crevice darkening belongs in its own ambient occlusion channel.

8 Textures feed the inputs

The inputs rarely stay single numbers. Each one comes from a texture, painted or scanned, so a strap can be smooth metal while the planks beside it are rough wood. A typical set has three textures.

  • Base colour: colour in RGB, stored with the sRGB curve.
  • Normal map: the direction the surface faces at each texel, for detail too fine to model (normal map, chapter 6.4). Data, read as linear numbers.
  • ORM: occlusion, roughness and metallic, one per colour channel of a single texture. Also data, also linear.

Packing three greyscale maps into one texture, channel packing, is common practice; the exact layout is not. glTF and Godot read roughness from green and metallic from blue. Unity HDRP's mask map puts metallic in red and smoothness in alpha. When a texture set moves between tools, check the layout before anything else.

9 Materials, instances and graphs

Most engines split a material in two. A parent material, usually built in a material graph (a shader graph for materials), defines the shader: boxes called nodes that read textures, combine them and feed the outputs. Unreal calls its editor the Material Editor, Unity has Shader Graph and Godot has VisualShader. Saving a parent material compiles shader code, often several shader variants of it.

A material instance reuses that compiled shader with different parameter values: another tint, another roughness, other textures. Instances are cheap to make and need no recompiling, so a game may have a few dozen parent materials and thousands of instances. The exception is any parameter that changes the shader's structure, such as Unreal's static switches: every combination that an instance uses is compiled as a separate shader.

4 What it costs

The BRDF itself is a few dozen arithmetic operations per light per pixel, which modern GPUs run quickly. The real costs of materials are texture memory, the number of shader variants, and the people-time spent keeping values consistent.

Take one material with a 2048 × 2048 texture set in typical desktop formats, each with its full mip chain, which adds a third. The block compression formats in the table, such as BC7, are chapter 6.3.

Shader variants cost compile time, disk and memory. A parent material with five on/off static switches can need up to 2⁵ = 32 shaders, each compiled for every pass and platform that uses it. Unreal's Shader Complexity view colours each pixel by its instruction count, which is the quickest way to spot an expensive graph.

What makes the bill grow

  • Texture resolutionDoubling width and height quadruples memory: 13.3 MB becomes 53.3 MB.
  • Layers and blendsEach blended layer, such as moss over stone, brings its own textures and texture reads.
  • Switches and keywordsUp to 2ⁿ shaders for n on/off switches, each one compiled and loaded.
  • Many parent materialsMore shaders, and more render state changes between draw calls.
  • Special shadingClear coat, cloth, hair and skin add instructions and often extra data per pixel.
  • Inconsistent valuesEvery asset outside the plausible range costs an artist time later, usually when lighting changes.

In proportion

A 4096 × 4096 metal/roughness set is 53 MB, about as much as 44 of the full-precision character meshes in chapter 4.2. Texture resolution, not the BRDF, is the material cost a lead most often has to manage, and texture streaming (chapter 6.3) decides how much of it must be in memory at once.

5 How it breaks

Material bugs rarely crash anything. They make a game look slightly wrong in ways that are hard to name. These six account for most of them.

Props that glow or vanish into black

Cause
Base colours outside the plausible range: near-white values that bounce too much light and look lit from inside, or near-black values that leave nothing but a highlight.
Ask for
A validation pass that lists every material whose non-metal base colour falls outside sRGB 30 to 240, with a screenshot of each in the engine's material validation view.

Muddy, half-metal surfaces

Cause
Metallic set to values such as 0.5 or 0.8 across whole surfaces. The result has some diffuse colour and some tinted reflection, which no real material has.
Ask for
Metallic maps that are 0 or 1 except at blends, and a check that flags large areas of in-between values.

Shadows that ignore the light

Cause
Lighting painted or photographed into the base colour: shadows, highlights or crevice darkening that stay fixed when the light moves.
Ask for
De-lit base colours, with crevice darkening moved into the occlusion channel, and a look at the asset under a light from the opposite side.

Wet-looking stone, matte chrome

Cause
Roughness and glossiness mixed up, or a map exported for the wrong channel layout, so rough areas render smooth and smooth ones rough.
Ask for
The engine's roughness or smoothness buffer view next to the source map, and a check of the export preset's channel layout.

Sparkling, crawling highlights

Cause
Smooth materials with fine normal-map detail seen at a distance. Each pixel covers many tiny bumps, but shading samples only one, so the highlight flickers from frame to frame: specular aliasing (chapter 9.2).
Ask for
Roughness that rises in the smaller mip levels where the normal map averages out, or the engine's specular anti-aliasing option, compared in motion.

Metal that turns black indoors

Cause
Metals have no diffuse light, so they only show what they reflect. Where nothing nearby has been captured for reflections, there is nothing to show (chapter 7.6).
Ask for
A check of reflection sources in that area before anyone edits the material. Brightening the base colour or adding emissive hides the cause.

Every engine can show each input on its own, which settles most arguments in seconds: Unreal's Buffer Visualization view modes, the Material Validation Mode of Unity URP's Rendering Debugger, the Material Validator in HDRP's Rendering Debugger, and Godot's Unshaded and Lighting debug draw modes.

Godot's extra Specular

Godot's Specular property, default 0.5, scales reflections and is not energy conserving. Godot's documentation says to leave it at 0.5 in most cases and to use roughness to change how reflective a surface looks.

7 Say it to your AI

Material briefs work best with real values in them: the engine and its version, the inputs as numbers, the range you consider plausible, and what must not change. Adapt these.

BriefUse when assets from several sources must match
Engine: Unity 6.6, HDRP. Assets arrive from two outsourcers as Substance Painter exports. Some props glow under our night lighting and some metals look like grey plastic.

1. Write an import check for materials: non-metal base colour between sRGB 30 and 240 by luminance, metallic 0 or 1 except at blends, mask maps imported with sRGB off and laid out R metallic, G occlusion, B detail mask, A smoothness.
2. Run it on our 1,200 materials and list the failures, grouped by outsourcer, with a screenshot of each in the Rendering Debugger's Material Validator.
3. Propose fixes that keep each asset's look, and flag any that need an artist's decision.
4. Show how to run the check on every new import, and how to override it for deliberate exceptions.
  • Numbers for the plausible range stop the AI inventing its own.
  • Naming the mask map layout catches exports packed for a different engine.
  • An override path keeps stylised exceptions possible without silencing the check.
Bug reportUse when a material looks right in one place and wrong in another
Our gold trophies look right in the daytime level but almost black inside the vault level. Unreal Engine 5.8, Lumen on. The material instance: Base Color 1.0, 0.77, 0.34; Metallic 1; Roughness 0.2. It is the same asset in both levels.

Find the cause before changing the material. Check what the trophies can reflect in the vault (sky light, reflection captures, Lumen reflections) and whether any instance or level setting differs. Show the cause in a viewport mode or a frame capture. Do not fix it by raising the base colour or adding emissive.
  • A metal has no diffuse light, so "black in one place" points at what it reflects.
  • Giving the exact values shows the material itself is plausible.
  • Ruling out the cosmetic fixes keeps the asset correct for every other level.

Questions to ask when reviewing material work

  1. Are metallic values 0 or 1 everywhere except at blends?In-between values make surfaces no real material has.
  2. Which base colours fall outside the plausible range, and who decided each one?Exceptions are fine when they are choices; most are accidents.
  3. Which textures are marked sRGB, and does that match what they hold?Data read through the sRGB curve gives wrong roughness and lumpy normals.
  4. Does the base colour contain any painted light, shadow or crevice darkening?The renderer adds its own, so it appears twice.
  5. Is this a new parent material, or an instance of one we have? How many shader variants does it add?Instances are free; new parents and switches cost compile time and memory.
  6. Does the texture packing match the channels this engine expects?Layouts differ between glTF, HDRP and in-house conventions.

8 Check yourself

  1. An iron railing has base colour 0.1, metallic 0 and roughness 0.3. What will it look like, and what should change?

    Show answer

    Dark grey plastic: a dark diffuse colour with a faint 4% white shine. Iron is a metal, so set metallic to 1 and the base colour to iron's measured value, about 0.56. Roughness then sets the finish.

  2. A polished floor looks like a mirror at the far end of a hallway but not under your feet. Is the renderer wrong?

    Show answer

    No. That is the Fresnel effect: reflectance rises from about 4% when you look straight down to nearly 100% at grazing angles, so the far floor, seen almost edge-on, reflects far more.

  3. Two artists make the same rock. One base colour stores sRGB 243 (0.90 reflectance), the other sRGB 137 (0.25). Which is plausible?

    Show answer

    The second. 0.90 is brighter than fresh snow (0.81) and above the usual limit of 240 in sRGB, so that rock would glow and bounce too much light onto its surroundings. Most soils and rocks sit well below 0.5.
