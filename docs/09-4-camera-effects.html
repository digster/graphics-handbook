9.4 Camera effects

A game camera is perfect: no glass, an instant shutter, a flawless sensor. Players read that as artificial, so renderers add back the flaws of real cameras on purpose. This chapter covers bloom, lens flare, depth of field, motion blur and the smaller lens effects: how each is computed from the finished image, in what order, at what cost, and which of them players need to be able to turn off.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A rendered frame is a pinhole photograph with an infinitely short exposure. Everything is in focus, nothing moves during the exposure, and light goes exactly where the geometry says. Real cameras and real eyes are messier. Glass scatters and reflects light, an aperture lets only one distance be sharp, a shutter stays open while things move, and film has grain. We have spent a century watching films shot through such lenses, so their flaws read as cinematic and as physically real.

All of these effects are post-processing: full-screen passes that run after the scene is lit, reading the lit image and the buffers made with it, such as depth and motion vectors, and writing a new image. Artists usually set them through a post-process volume: a box in the level, or a global setting, holding values for bloom, exposure, depth of field and the rest, blended as the camera moves from one volume into another. That is how a cave can have stronger bloom and a different grade from the valley outside.

An everyday comparison

A phone photo of a street at night: street lamps glow into the haze, the camera focused on your friend so the shop signs behind are soft discs, a passing car is a streak. Nobody calls those mistakes; they are how the scene looked through that camera. Remove them and the photo looks like a render.

The problem it solves

A screen cannot show how bright a lamp is, so bloom borrows the eye's own reaction to glare to say it. Depth of field steers attention in cutscenes. Motion blur makes 30 or 60 frames a second look smoother. Grain hides colour banding.

The cost is detail, milliseconds, and for some players nausea or eye strain.

Why a lead cares

Camera effects set much of a game's look and are cheap to change late, which makes them a frequent source of argument between art direction, performance and accessibility. Together they cost a few milliseconds a frame, mostly in depth of field and motion blur. They interact with the upscaler, since most run at full display resolution after it. And motion blur, camera shake and flashing effects are accessibility issues: Microsoft's Xbox Accessibility Guidelines ask games to let players turn several of them off.

3 How it works

Every effect here is a filter on the finished image. They differ in what extra information they read (brightness, depth, motion) and in how far each pixel reaches across the screen, which is what makes some cheap and others expensive.

1 Bloom: very bright light spills over

Bloom spreads very bright light into the pixels around it, as glare does in an eye or a lens. It runs on the scene-referred image, before the tone curve (9.1), because only there is a flame at 20 still twenty times brighter than a wall at 1. After tone mapping both would be near white, and bloom could not tell them apart.

A blur wide enough to look like glare would be far too slow at full resolution, so bloom uses a pyramid. Keep only the light above a threshold. Halve the image several times, blurring at each step. Then come back up, adding each level to the one above it, so that tight and wide glows sum together. Finally add a small fraction of the result to the scene.

The threshold is a choice. A hard threshold makes only lights and hot highlights bloom, which is easy to control. Jorge Jimenez's bloom for Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare used no threshold at all, because scattering in the eye affects all light, and HDRP's bloom is energy-conserving, which its documentation says any threshold above 0 breaks. Small, very bright pixels are the other trap: a highlight one pixel wide may land on a different downsampled texel each frame, so the bloom around it flickers. Jimenez's fix was a wider, 13-tap downsample filter plus reducing the brightest pixels' weight when averaging; HDRP's High Quality Prefiltering likewise uses 13 samples instead of 4. A lens dirt texture, multiplied into the bloom, adds smudges that light up when a bright source is in view.

2 Lens flare: reflections inside the lens

A real lens is a stack of glass elements, and a little light reflects between them, producing ghost images of a bright source on a line through the image centre, plus streaks. Games draw lens flares in two ways. Sprite flares attach a set of shapes to a specific light, the sun or a lamp, placed along the line from the light through the screen centre and faded when the light is hidden. Screen-space flares take the bright pixels the bloom pass already found and draw them again flipped and stretched, so every bright spot can flare, including emissive surfaces. Unity offers both: the Lens Flare (SRP) component for specific lights, including off-screen ones, and the Screen Space Lens Flare override, which reuses the bloom buffer and works only for what is on screen.

3 Depth of field and the circle of confusion

A lens focuses light from one distance onto the sensor. Light from a point nearer or farther converges in front of or behind the sensor and lands as a disc instead of a point. Depth of field is the range of distances that look sharp; the disc is the circle of confusion, CoC, and its size for every pixel follows from the pixel's depth with the thin-lens formula.

In the renderer, a pass computes the CoC of every pixel from the depth buffer; then each pixel gathers colour from a disc of that radius around it. Two problems make it harder than a blur. A blurred object in the foreground must spread over sharp pixels behind it, which a simple gather around each pixel cannot do, so renderers split the image into a near and a far field, blur each, and blend; Unreal's documentation describes near, focal and far layers composited together. And a disc 45 pixels across holds about 1,600 pixels, far too many to read, so the gather takes a few dozen samples, often at half resolution, and relies on TAA to clean up. Jimenez's Advanced Warfare depth of field used 49 samples.

4 Bokeh: the shape of out-of-focus light

An out-of-focus point of light takes the shape of the aperture, the opening formed by the lens's blades. That shape and its quality is bokeh, from a Japanese word for blur. With six straight blades the discs are hexagons; with rounded blades they are circles. Small bright lights in the background, street lamps, candles, sparks, show it most clearly.

5 Motion blur along motion vectors

A real shutter stays open for a while, so anything that moves during the exposure is smeared along its path. Motion blur imitates that by averaging, for each pixel, samples taken along its motion vector, the same per-pixel screen movement TAA uses (9.2). Camera motion blur derives the vectors from depth and the camera's movement, so it blurs everything when the camera turns. Per-object motion blur uses the vectors each moving object writes, so a car blurs while the road under a tracking camera stays sharp.

How long the streak is depends on the virtual shutter. Film cameras traditionally expose for half of each frame's time, and engines express the equivalent as an intensity that scales the vectors, as URP and HDRP do, or as an amount and a target frame rate, as in Unreal. Blur length is also clamped, because long vectors need many samples and leave visible steps when they have too few. Blurring heavily on camera rotation is the most common cause of complaints, so engines clamp camera motion separately: HDRP has a Camera Clamp Mode, and URP's Clamp limits blur from camera rotation.

6 Grain, vignette, chromatic aberration and distortion

The remaining effects are cheap per-pixel adjustments with no gathering involved. Film grain adds fresh noise every frame, often with separate strengths for shadows, mid-tones and highlights, as in Unreal; besides the look, it breaks up colour banding the way dithering does (3.2). Vignette darkens the corners. Chromatic aberration shifts the red and blue channels apart, more towards the edges, as cheap lenses do. Lens distortion bends straight lines, outwards for barrel distortion or inwards for pincushion; URP's Lens Distortion has a Scale setting to zoom in and hide the edges where no image data exists.

7 The order, and where the time goes

The order matters because each effect changes the next one's input. Depth of field and bloom need HDR values, so they come before the tone curve. Anything that blurs should see anti-aliased, stable input, so most effects come after TAA, the firewall of 9.2; Karis's TAA talk lists bloom and depth of field after it. Unreal puts depth of field just before its upscaler instead, and Epic notes that motion blur and the tone mapper run after TSR at full resolution. Grain, vignette and fringes come last, then the interface, which no camera effect should touch.

8 Try it: the courtyard's effect stack

The Lantern Courtyard runs a small post chain of its own: height fog, bloom, then one pass for exposure, the tone curve, a light vignette and a touch of noise against banding, then FXAA. Switch between its views to take the stack apart.

9 Give players the switches

Several camera effects cause real problems for some players. Motion blur, camera shake and head bob contribute to motion sickness, which is why Xbox Accessibility Guideline 117 asks games to avoid them or let players turn them off, and to offer a field of view setting. Bright, flashing effects fall under guideline 118, which treats more than about three flashes a second over about a fifth of the screen as a failure. Grain, chromatic aberration, vignette and depth of field reduce contrast and detail, which hurts players with low vision.

4 What it costs

Camera effects cost GPU time in proportion to the pixels they touch and how far each pixel reaches. Bloom reads a lot but mostly at low resolution. Depth of field and motion blur gather many samples per pixel. The per-pixel effects are almost free once merged into the tone-map pass.

Memory is modest. A bloom chain like the courtyard's, four levels down from half resolution and three back up, in 16-bit floating point (8 bytes per pixel), adds about 11 MB at 1080p: 5.5 MB going down (a quarter, a sixteenth, a sixty-fourth and a 256th of 16.6 MB) and 5.4 MB coming up. Depth of field and motion blur add a few half- or quarter-resolution buffers for CoC and tiles.

  • Display resolutionMost effects run after the upscaler, so they cost the same at Performance as at native. Epic calls these the hidden costs of temporal upscaling, and Unreal halves motion blur's input resolution by default to contain them.
  • Blur sizeDepth of field and motion blur need more samples, or show steps, as their radius grows: shallow focus in close-ups and fast camera turns are their worst cases.
  • Quality settingsSample counts, bokeh shape (Godot's Circle costs most), high quality bloom filtering and full-resolution depth of field are the main knobs.
  • Art timeEvery volume, cutscene and camera needs its own values, and the look depends on the tone curve, so grading and camera effects are tuned together.

The usual trade

Keep bloom and the per-pixel effects on everywhere; they are cheap and part of the look. Use full depth of field mainly in cutscenes and photo mode, where focus is directed, and a cheaper Gaussian or none in gameplay. Offer motion blur with Off, Low and High, and test what fraction of players turn it off.

5 How it breaks

Most camera-effect bugs are an effect reading the wrong input, at the wrong point in the chain, or with too few samples.

A milky, washed-out picture

Cause
Bloom threshold too low or intensity too high, so ordinary surfaces bloom; or bloom applied after the tone curve, where everything bright looks equal.
Ask for
Bloom on HDR values before the tone curve, a threshold or energy-conserving setup chosen deliberately, and a test scene with a dark room and one bright light.

Flickering bloom on small highlights

Cause
One-pixel highlights (fireflies, glints on water or metal) fall on different downsampled texels each frame, so their bloom appears and disappears.
Ask for
A wider downsample filter and reduced weight for the brightest pixels in the first downsample (HDRP: High Quality Prefiltering), and stable input from TAA.

Halos around in-focus objects

Cause
The gather mixes sharp foreground pixels into the blurred background, or the reverse, because near and far fields are not separated or depth is ignored at edges.
Ask for
Separate near and far layers, depth-aware sample weighting, and a test shot with a sharp character against a distant blurred background and against a blurred foreground.

Motion blur on the weapon or the interface

Cause
First-person arms and weapons move with the camera but get camera-rotation vectors, or world-space interface is drawn before motion blur.
Ask for
Correct vectors (or a mask) for first-person geometry, camera motion clamped separately, and all interface drawn after post-processing.

Lens flares through walls

Cause
A sprite flare attached to the sun or a lamp with no occlusion test, or one that tests only the light's centre pixel.
Ask for
Occlusion testing over an area around the light, a fade instead of a pop, and a check at the screen edges, where Unity notes occlusion might not always work.

Questions to ask when reviewing camera-effect work

  1. In what order do the effects run, relative to TAA or the upscaler and to the tone curve?Bloom and depth of field need HDR values; grain and fringes belong after the upscaler.
  2. What does each effect cost at the display resolution, in the worst shot?Close-up depth of field and fast camera turns are the expensive cases.
  3. Do first-person geometry and the interface stay out of motion blur?Smeared weapons and HUDs are a common bug.
  4. Which effects can players turn off separately, and are the defaults sensible?Motion blur, shake and flashing are accessibility issues.
  5. Does bloom stay stable on tiny bright highlights?Firefly flicker in bloom is visible and distracting.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why is bloom computed before the tone curve and film grain after the upscaler?

    Show answer

    Bloom needs to know how bright a light really is, which only the HDR, scene-referred values say; after the tone curve a flame and a white wall look alike. Grain is fine noise that should stay crisp at display resolution; a temporal upscaler would treat it as detail to accumulate and smear it.

  2. With the focus at 3 m, why do objects 1 m from the camera look far blurrier than a mountain on the horizon?

    Show answer

    The circle of confusion grows with the difference between the object's distance and the focus distance divided by the object's distance. In front of the focus plane that ratio keeps growing as things get closer; behind it, it approaches 1 however far away they are. For a 50 mm lens at f/2 that is 45 pixels at 1 m against at most 22.6 pixels at infinity on a 1920-pixel image.

  3. Several players report nausea in your first-person game. Which camera settings should they be able to change?

    Show answer

    Motion blur (off, or reduced), camera shake and head bob, and field of view, as Xbox Accessibility Guideline 117 recommends. Each should be a separate control, not one switch for all cinematic effects.
