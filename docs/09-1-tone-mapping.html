9.1 HDR, exposure and tone mapping

A lit frame holds light from almost nothing to thousands of times brighter than a screen's white. Exposure decides which of that light becomes the middle of the picture; a tone curve squeezes the rest onto the screen without flat white highlights or black shadows; grading then sets the look. These three steps decide how every lighting decision in the game finally appears.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The renderer lights a frame in linear light with no upper limit, HDR rendering (3.2). Those values are scene-referred: they describe how much light is in the scene. A sunlit wall can be 2.0 and a lantern flame 30. A screen, though, takes display-referred values: what it should show, from black to its white. Something has to translate.

The translation has two parts, the same two a camera has. Exposure multiplies all the light so that the important part of the scene lands in the middle of the picture. Then tone mapping bends the values with a curve, so the bright end rolls off gently towards white instead of stopping dead, and the dark end fades into black instead of crushing. Grading comes after, to set mood and match shots.

Everyday comparison

A photographer shooting a room with a sunny window chooses an exposure: the room or the view. Film then did something kind: it did not burn out at a hard limit but faded into white gradually, so the window still showed a hint of the garden. Exposure is the dial; the tone curve is the film.

The problem it solves

Real light spans a range far larger than any screen. Without exposure, every light value set in physical units (7.1) would come out black or white. Without a tone curve, skies, lamps, fire and sunlit walls all clip to the same flat white, and colourful bright things change colour as one channel hits its limit before the others.

Why a lead cares

The tone mapper and exposure set are where art direction meets physics. Changing either late changes how every level, light and material looks, so artists will retune everything they have lit. Choose the tone curve and exposure approach in pre-production, lock them for lookdev, and treat a change as a project-wide decision.

3 How it works

Follow the light from the end of the lighting pass to the screen: how much range there is, how exposure chooses a slice of it, how the engine can choose automatically, how curves map it, and what grading does after.

1 How much range: the scene against the screen

Brightness of a surface or a light source is luminance, measured in candelas per square metre, the same unit as the nit used for screens. In the real world it runs from about a thousandth of a nit for a night sky to over a billion for the sun at noon. A typical LCD monitor shows white at about 250 nits and, at a typical contrast of about 1,000 to 1, black at about a quarter of a nit.

Real scenes are not only the sun and the night sky, but even one view holds far more than a screen's range: a sunny exterior with deep shade and a bright sky, or an interior with a window. That is why the renderer cannot simply divide by a constant. It needs to choose a slice, which is exposure, and then compress what is outside the slice, which is the tone curve.

2 Exposure and EV: sliding the window

Exposure is a single multiplier applied to all the light before the tone curve. Photographers and engines measure it as an exposure value, EV, on a log scale where each step, a stop, doubles or halves the light. EV100 is the value for ISO 100 film. A bright scene needs a high EV and a dark one a low EV: Unity's lighting guide gives about EV 14 for a sunlit scene, 10 for cloudy, 7 for low sun, 4 for an interior and 1 for moonlight. In Unreal's formula the multiplier is 1 / 2EV100, so each EV step halves the image brightness.

Engines set exposure in three ways. Fixed or manual exposure is a number the lighting artist chooses per area or per camera. Physical camera exposure derives it from shutter speed, aperture and ISO, which matters most when lights are set in physical light units (7.1). Automatic exposure measures the frame, step 3. Most projects use automatic exposure with limits, plus exposure compensation, an offset in stops that artists use to make a space look brighter or darker than the meter would.

Exposure aims at middle grey, a surface reflecting 18% of the light, which looks halfway between black and white. Reinhard and colleagues' 2002 tone reproduction paper builds on exactly this photographer's rule: map the scene's average to 0.18, then compress what lies above.

3 Auto-exposure: measure, then adapt slowly

Auto-exposure, also called eye adaptation, does what a camera's meter and our eyes do. Each frame the renderer measures the scene's brightness on a small, downsampled copy, picks a target exposure, and moves the current exposure towards it over time. Unreal's default meter builds a luminance histogram: it counts pixels in 64 brightness bands on a log scale, discards the darkest pixels and the brightest few by percentage, and averages the rest. Epic suggests a Low Percent of 70 to 80 and a High Percent of 80 to 95.

Auto-exposure misjudges scenes that are meant to be dark or bright: a night level metered to middle grey looks like grey day. Exposure compensation, minimum and maximum exposure limits, and metering masks that weight the centre of the screen are how artists keep it honest. Unreal adds local exposure, which compresses contrast region by region for scenes such as a bright doorway seen from a dark interior; Epic says it should always be set up when using Lumen GI.

4 The tone curve

After exposure, a tone curve maps each value to a screen value. Plotted against stops of scene light it is an S: a toe at the dark end that eases into black, a nearly straight middle where most of the picture lives, and a shoulder at the bright end that rolls off towards white. The shoulder is the point of the exercise. Without it, everything brighter than white is cut off at the same value.

Reinhard's simple operator, x / (1 + x) from the 2002 paper, maps every value below 1 and never reaches white, which guarantees nothing clips but leaves highlights grey. Filmic tone mapping shapes the curve like photographic film's response instead, with a toe and a shoulder, and is what Unreal calls its default tonemapper. Godot 4 offers Linear (the default, which clips), Reinhard, Filmic, ACES and AgX.

Code Two curves in a few lines

Reinhard per channel, and Krzysztof Narkowicz's widely copied one-line fit of the ACES curve, which already includes a pre-exposure so that an input of 1 gives about 0.8:

reinhard(x) = x / (1 + x)
aces_narkowicz(x) = clamp((x * (2.51 * x + 0.03)) / (x * (2.43 * x + 0.59) + 0.14), 0, 1)

Both run on linear values after exposure, and the result is then encoded for the screen with the sRGB curve (3.2). Narkowicz describes his fit as a simple luminance-only fit that over-saturates brights; the tone curve lab and the Lantern Courtyard use Stephen Hill's fuller fit, which adds a colour matrix before and after.

5 ACES, AgX and what happens to colour

ACES, the Academy Color Encoding System, is a colour management standard from film production. Its output transforms include a tone curve, and fits of the ACES 1 curve became the default in many engines: Unity's URP and HDRP have an ACES mode, and Unreal describes its filmic tonemapper as matching the ACES standard. ACES 2, documented in 2025, redesigned the output transforms with a gentler highlight roll-off and lower mid-tone contrast.

Most game tone curves are applied to each colour channel separately. For grey that is harmless. For a bright, saturated colour it is not: in a lantern flame the red channel is several times the green, so red reaches the shoulder first and stops growing while green keeps climbing. The colour slides from orange towards yellow, then white. Some shift towards white is right, since very bright light looks white to us; the problem is how fast, and that hue drifts on the way. AgX, created by Troy Sobotka and Blender's default view transform since version 4.0, mixes a little of each channel into the others before compressing in log space, so bright colours fade towards white with less change of hue. Blender's manual credits it with 16.5 stops of dynamic range.

6 Try it: the tone curve lab

The lab below holds one HDR picture, a sunny square with a lantern in a shaded arch, about 12 stops from the darkest shade to the flame, and two test ramps. Change exposure and the curve, and watch the plot, the clipped-pixel count and the flame's hue.

  • With No tone map, lower the exposure until the sky stops clipping. The arch goes nearly black: no single exposure fits this scene without a curve.
  • Switch to ACES fit and raise the exposure to +2. The flame turns yellow and the flame-coloured ramp passes through yellow before white.
  • Do the same with AgX-like: the ramp stays orange longer and fades towards white, and the shade keeps more detail, at the cost of a flatter look.

7 Grading, white balance and LUTs

Colour grading sets the look once the light is right: overall contrast and saturation, tints for shadows and highlights, curves. White balance removes a colour cast by making a chosen light read as neutral, through temperature and tint controls, as a camera does. Engines prefer to grade on scene-referred values before the tone curve, where the full range is still available: Unreal applies its colour grading controls in scene-referred linear space, and URP's High Dynamic Range grading mode grades before tonemapping.

A LUT, a lookup table, captures a whole grade as a small 3D texture. For every input colour it stores the output colour, so a grade built in an image editor or a grading suite costs one texture read per pixel at runtime. Unreal's LUTs are 16 × 16 × 16, stored unwrapped as a 256 × 16 texture, and are applied to the final low dynamic range image. URP uses 32 entries per side by default.

8 Where tone mapping sits in the frame

Everything up to tone mapping is scene-referred: lighting, fog, transparent surfaces and HDR effects such as bloom and depth of field (9.4). Tone mapping is the boundary. After it, values are display-referred, and only screen-level work follows: a LUT in some pipelines, encoding for the screen, and the interface. Anti-aliasing and upscaling usually run before this point (9.2, 9.3), and Unreal describes tone mapping as the last post-processing stage.

HDR output changes the last steps, not the first ones. With an HDR display the tone curve maps scene light to the screen's peak brightness, often 1,000 nits, rather than to standard white, and the encoding is the HDR10 PQ curve instead of sRGB (3.2). Not every tone mapper supports it: Godot's Filmic and ACES modes and its colour correction LUT do not, while Linear, Reinhard and AgX do.

4 What it costs

On the GPU, tone mapping is among the cheapest things in the frame: arithmetic per pixel and perhaps one LUT read, usually merged into a single full-screen pass with exposure, grading and encoding. Its real cost is bandwidth for that one pass and, above all, people-time.

The pass reads the HDR scene colour and writes the final image. At 1080p, reading a 16-bit floating-point colour target (8 bytes per pixel) is 2,073,600 × 8 = 16.6 MB, and writing an 8-bit target (4 bytes per pixel) is 8.3 MB: about 25 MB per frame, or 1.5 GB a second at 60 fps. At 4K it is four times as much. Auto-exposure adds a small reduction pass on a downsampled copy of the frame.

The usual trade

A neutral, low-contrast curve such as AgX or a neutral mode gives grading the most room and handles HDR screens well, but needs a deliberate grade to look finished. A punchy filmic curve looks good out of the box and is what many teams ship, at the cost of hue shifts in saturated highlights and less room to grade. Pick one in pre-production and grade against it from the first lit scene.

5 How it breaks

Exposure and tone mapping bugs rarely crash; they make whole levels look wrong, and artists then compensate light by light. Learn to spot these.

Flat white skies and windows

Cause
No tone curve, a curve that clips early, or exposure set for a dark interior while a bright exterior is in view.
Ask for
The tone mapper and exposure settings, a capture with clipped pixels highlighted, and local exposure or tighter metering where interiors and exteriors meet.

Fire, neon and skin change colour as they brighten

Cause
A tone curve applied per channel: the strongest channel reaches the shoulder first, so the hue drifts towards yellow or the secondary colours before white.
Ask for
A side-by-side with a hue-preserving option (AgX, a neutral mode, or the engine's hue-preservation settings) on test ramps of saturated colours.

Exposure pumping

Cause
Auto-exposure reacts too fast, or meters on a few bright pixels such as lamps or effects, so brightness swings as the camera moves.
Ask for
Slower adaptation, histogram percentages that ignore the brightest pixels, minimum and maximum exposure limits, and a metering mask.

A grey, washed-out image

Cause
Tone mapping applied twice, a LUT built for a different stage or colour space, a gamma step applied twice, or HDR output fed an SDR curve.
Ask for
The order of post-processing stages, which colour space each LUT was authored in, and a capture of the frame before and after the final pass.

Crushed, murky shadows

Cause
A high-contrast curve's toe plus a low exposure, or grading contrast pushed after the curve, so many dark values end at the same black.
Ask for
The curve's toe settings, exposure compensation for dark areas, and a check on several screens at the brightness players will use.

To diagnose, look at the frame before tone mapping. Unreal's HDR (Eye Adaptation) visualisation shows the histogram and metering, HDRP has exposure debug modes, and a frame capture (11.5) shows the scene colour target with its true values, so you can tell whether a problem is in the light or in the mapping.

7 Say it to your AI

Tone mapping briefs need the engine and pipeline, the current tone mapper and exposure mode, whether HDR output is supported, and reference images. Ask for test scenes and ramps, not just settings.

Questions to ask when reviewing exposure and tone mapping work

  1. Which tone mapper is used, and was the whole game lit against it?Changing it later changes every scene.
  2. Is exposure fixed, physical or automatic, and what are its limits and speeds?Unbounded auto-exposure turns night into grey day and pumps in mixed scenes.
  3. Does grading happen before or after the tone curve, and what colour space were LUTs authored in?A LUT at the wrong stage washes out or crushes the image.
  4. How do saturated bright colours look on a ramp?Per-channel curves shift hue; check fire, neon, skin and effects.
  5. What does the HDR output path use, and is it tested on real screens?Not every tone mapper supports HDR, and calibration is part of the feature.

8 Check yourself

  1. A sunlit wall renders at 2.0 and the sky at 4.0 in the lighting buffer. Without tone mapping they look identical on screen. Why, and what fixes it?

    Show answer

    Both are above 1.0, the screen's white, so both clip to the same white. Lowering exposure would separate them but darken everything else; a tone curve with a shoulder compresses values above the middle so both keep distinct screen values below white.

  2. The game's campfires look yellow-white, though the artist painted the flames orange. What is the likely cause?

    Show answer

    A tone curve applied to each channel separately. The flame's red channel reaches the curve's shoulder first and stops growing while green keeps rising, so the hue slides towards yellow and then white. Lower the fire's intensity or exposure, or use a curve that preserves hue better, such as AgX.

  3. Players complain that the screen keeps brightening and darkening in a street lit by bright neon signs. Which settings do you look at?

    Show answer

    Auto-exposure: its metering (which pixels are averaged; histogram percentages that ignore the brightest few percent, or a metering mask), its adaptation speeds, and its minimum and maximum exposure limits.
