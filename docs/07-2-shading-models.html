7.2 Shading models

A shading model is the formula that turns the light reaching a point, the surface there and the direction of the camera into a colour. Almost all of them are built from the same three terms, ambient, diffuse and specular, and the way those terms are written decides whether a game looks physically based, dated or hand-drawn.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Every pixel of a lit surface asks one question: of the light arriving at this point, how much leaves towards the camera, and in what colour? A shading model is the recipe the pixel shader follows to answer it, once for every light that reaches the pixel.

Nearly every recipe splits the answer into the two behaviours you met in 6.5 PBR materials. Diffuse reflection is light that goes into the surface and comes back out in all directions, carrying the surface's colour. Specular reflection is light that bounces off the surface like a mirror, in one preferred direction. A third, older term, ambient, adds a little light everywhere so that nothing is completely black.

Everyday comparison

A portrait painter works in layers. First a dark base colour over the whole face, then the light and shade that model its form, and last the small white catch-lights in the eyes and on the nose.

Those are ambient, diffuse and specular. A comic artist follows the same order with different rules: two flat tones instead of a smooth blend, and a black outline.

The problem it solves

Real light bounces around a room endlessly, and the exact answer for one pixel would take longer than a whole frame.

A shading model squeezes the answer into a few cheap terms with known maths, so a GPU can run it for millions of pixels and several lights, 60 times a second.

Why a lead cares

The shading model is the lowest layer of a game's look. It decides whether materials from different artists match, whether skin reads as skin or as plastic, and whether a stylised look holds up in motion. Changing it late means re-tuning every material and light in the game. Knowing the terms lets you read requests such as "softer skin", "three-tone characters" or "more sheen on the cloth" as changes to a particular term, each with a cost.

3 How it works

Start with the directions every model needs, then build the terms one at a time: diffuse, specular in its three common forms, ambient, and finally the stylised and special models.

1 The directions a model needs

At the point being shaded, the shader knows four things. The surface normal, N, says which way the surface faces, after any normal map has bent it. L points from the point towards the light, and V towards the camera. The material supplies the rest: base colour, roughness, metallic.

All three directions are unit vectors, length 1. That matters because the workhorse of every shading model is the dot product, and for unit vectors it gives the cosine of the angle between them: 1 when they point the same way, 0 at right angles, negative when they point apart (3.3 Vectors). A shading model is mostly a handful of dot products between N, L and V, plus some arithmetic on the results.

2 Diffuse: Lambert and N·L

The simplest diffuse model is Lambertian diffuse, after the 18th-century scientist Johann Heinrich Lambert. It assumes the surface sends the light it receives equally in all directions, so the camera's position does not matter. Only one thing changes the brightness: how squarely the light hits.

That is measured by N·L, the dot product of the normal and the direction to the light. A beam that hits head-on lands on the smallest possible area. Tilt the surface and the same beam spreads over more area, so each part gets less. At 60° it covers twice the area and each part gets half the light, and cos 60° is exactly 0.5. When the light is behind the surface the cosine turns negative, and shaders clamp it to 0.

Lambert diffuse is the diffuse colour times the light times max(N·L, 0), divided by π. The π keeps the surface from sending out more light than arrives, the rule of energy conservation. Epic's 2013 notes on Unreal Engine 4 report trying the more elaborate Burley diffuse model and finding only small differences, so they kept Lambert. Godot uses Burley by default and offers Lambert as an option.

Where the formula runs matters too. Shading every pixel is now normal. Older games, and some low-end settings today, use per-vertex lighting: the model runs once per vertex and the results are blended across each triangle, which is cheaper but smears highlights on large triangles. Godot's materials still offer it as a Per-Vertex shading mode, and URP's Forward path can light additional lights per vertex.

3 Specular: a reflection of the light

Specular light behaves like a mirror. Light arriving along L leaves along R, its mirror image about the normal, plus a spread that depends on how rough the surface is (roughness, microfacets). So unlike diffuse light, specular light depends on where the camera is.

Seen on a surface, that lobe is the specular highlight: a blurred image of the light source. Its size tells the eye how smooth the surface is, a pin-prick on polished plastic, a wide sheen on worn rubber. Walk around a statue and the highlights slide across it, while the diffuse shading stays put. That sliding is what makes a surface read as shiny.

4 Phong and Blinn-Phong

The Phong reflection model, published by Bui Tuong Phong in 1975, was the first widely used recipe. It reflects L about the normal to get R, then measures how close R is to the camera direction: max(R·V, 0) raised to a power, the shininess exponent. A higher exponent gives a smaller, tighter highlight.

In 1977 Jim Blinn changed one step. Instead of the reflection, he used the half vector H, the direction half-way between L and V, and measured it against the normal: max(N·H, 0) raised to a power. When H lines up with N, the surface is tilted exactly right to mirror the light into the eye. Blinn-Phong became the specular model of OpenGL's old fixed-function pipeline and of many games before physically based shading.

The two are not interchangeable. On a sphere they look much alike, but on a flat surface seen at a low angle they part ways. The Phong highlight stays round. The Blinn-Phong highlight stretches towards the viewer, the way the setting sun stretches into a long streak on a wet road. Real reflections on wet roads and polished floors stretch the same way, which is one reason Blinn-Phong is generally considered the more realistic of the two.

Simplified

In their classic form neither model conserves energy: a wide highlight is as bright as a narrow one, so rough surfaces reflect more light than arrives. Later versions multiply by a normalisation factor that dims the highlight as it widens. Unity documents its URP Simple Lit shader as based on Blinn-Phong and not energy conserving, and suggests it for stylised visuals and low-end platforms.

5 Cook-Torrance: the physically based specular

Physically based renderers replaced the shininess exponent with the microfacet model from 6.5 PBR materials. Its specular term is Cook-Torrance: three factors multiplied together and divided by 4 (N·L)(N·V). D is the microfacet distribution, almost always GGX, which counts the tiny facets turned towards H. F is Fresnel, how strongly each facet reflects at this angle. G removes facets hidden by their neighbours. Together they are the specular half of the BRDF most engines use.

D plays the part Blinn's exponent used to play, and it uses the same half vector. Matched by roughness, the two peaks are the same height. The difference is the tail: GGX falls off more slowly, so a GGX highlight has a soft glow around a sharp core. Epic's 2013 notes call the extra cost over Blinn-Phong "fairly small" and say the natural look of the longer tail "appealed to our artists".

Maths The terms as formulas

Vectors are unit length. Light is the light arriving, for example in lux; the result is the light leaving towards the camera.

Lambert diffuse = diffuse colour ÷ π × light × max(N·L, 0)

Phong specular = k × max(R·V, 0)ᵐ × light, with R = 2 (N·L) N − L

Blinn-Phong specular = k × max(N·H, 0)ⁿ × light, with H = normalize(L + V)

Cook-Torrance specular = D × F × G ÷ (4 (N·L)(N·V)) × light × max(N·L, 0)

In the classic models k is a specular strength the artist picks. Normalised versions use (m + 2) ÷ 2π for Phong and (n + 8) ÷ 8π for Blinn-Phong, so that a wider highlight is also a dimmer one. 6 The ambient term

Direct light alone leaves every surface that faces away from a light pitch black. In the real world those surfaces are lit by light bouncing off everything else. The oldest fix is the ambient term: a constant amount of light, times the surface's colour, added to every pixel.

A constant has no direction, so it lights the underside of a table as brightly as the top of it. The box and its shadow turn the same flat grey and lose their shape. Modern engines keep the idea of light that is not from any one lamp, but take it from somewhere better: the sky light from 7.1 Lights, reflection probes (7.6 Reflections and image-based lighting), and global illumination, darkened in creases by ambient occlusion.

8 Toon and other stylised models

Toon shading, or cel shading, imitates hand-drawn animation. It keeps N·L and the highlight but changes the curve that turns them into brightness. Instead of a smooth falloff, N·L is cut into a few flat bands, often two or three, each with a colour an artist picks. The highlight becomes a hard-edged shape, and an ink outline is drawn around the silhouette, usually as a second, slightly enlarged copy of the mesh drawn inside out, or as a post-process that finds edges in the depth and normal buffers.

The bands can come from fixed thresholds, as in Godot's Toon diffuse and specular modes, or from a ramp: a small gradient texture looked up with N·L, so artists paint the transition. Other stylised models bend the same terms in other ways. Wrap lighting lets N·L reach past 90° so light wraps softly around the form; Godot offers it as Lambert Wrap. Fully hand-painted looks skip lighting and use unlit materials with the shading painted into the textures.

Stylised is not cheaper

A toon character still needs N·L, shadows and usually a highlight, and the outline adds a second draw of every outlined mesh or a full-screen pass. Hard band edges also alias: a tiny wobble in a normal flips a pixel from one band to the next, so bands crawl in motion unless the step is softened or the normals are cleaned up.

9 Skin, cloth and other special models

The default model assumes light either reflects at the surface or comes straight back out of it. Some materials break that assumption badly enough that engines ship extra shading models for them.

Skin, wax, marble and leaves let light travel under the surface and come out somewhere else, called subsurface scattering; 9.6 Special surfaces and effects covers it with hair and foliage. Cloth shading adds a sheen term that brightens fabric where it turns away from the camera. Unreal's Cloth model adds a thin layer of fuzz; HDRP has a Fabric shader; Godot's Rim parameter emulates the micro-fur on fabrics and takes the light into account. Clear coat, a second thin specular layer on top of the base, covers car paint and lacquer.

Each extra model is another branch or variant in the shaders, and in a deferred renderer it needs room in the G-buffer to say which model a pixel uses. 7.3 Forward, deferred and clustered explains why that matters.

4 What it costs

The formulas themselves are cheap. The table counts the arithmetic for one light at one pixel, roughly as the shading builder above writes it: multiplies, adds and special functions such as a power or a square root each count as one.

TermWhat it computesOperations, about
AmbientColour times a constant, once per pixel, whatever the number of lights3
Lambert diffuseN·L, a clamp, colour times light10
Phong specularReflect L, R·V, a power15
Blinn-Phong specularNormalise L + V, N·H, a power15
Cook-Torrance with GGXH, then D, F and G and a divide40

Take a 1080p frame where each pixel is reached by three lights on average. That is 2,073,600 × 3 ≈ 6.2 million light evaluations. At about 50 operations each for Lambert plus GGX, the frame needs about 310 million operations, which a GPU that sustains 10 trillion operations a second finishes in about 0.03 ms. Real shaders reach only part of a GPU's peak, but even several times that is a small slice of a 16.7 ms frame.

So the choice between Blinn-Phong and GGX is rarely what makes lighting slow. The bill comes from what surrounds the formula.

What makes the bill grow

  • Lights per pixelEvery model runs once per light per pixel. Overlapping lights multiply it, which is the subject of 7.3.
  • ShadowsA shadowed light first renders the scene again from the light's point of view (7.4), which costs far more than its shading.
  • Special modelsClear coat evaluates a second specular layer. Unity notes that Complex Lit takes longer because it lights both the base and the coat. Subsurface scattering for skin usually adds a screen-space blur pass (9.6).
  • Shader variantsEach shading model, and each option inside it, is another compiled variant (5.6) to build, ship and switch between.
  • Stylised extrasOutlines draw outlined meshes a second time or add a full-screen pass. Ramps add a texture read per light.
  • PixelsEverything above scales with resolution: 4K has four times the pixels of 1080p.

In proportion

Pick the shading model for the look and for consistency between artists; speed rarely decides it. Performance problems in lighting almost always come from how many lights touch each pixel, how many cast shadows, and how many special models and variants the materials pull in.

5 How it breaks

Shading-model problems rarely crash anything. They make a scene look subtly wrong, and each has a typical picture.

Shadow sides are pure black

Cause
Only direct light reaches the surface. There is no ambient term, sky light, probe or global illumination for the parts that face away from every light.
Ask for
The scene's environment lighting setup, a view with direct light switched off to see what fills the shadows, and probes or GI for interiors.

Everything looks like plastic

Cause
Every material has the same highlight: one shininess or roughness value everywhere, often a default nobody changed, or a classic specular model with a fixed strength.
Ask for
Roughness maps per material, compared against photo references under the same light, and the engine's roughness buffer view to spot flat values.

Surfaces brighter than the light

Cause
A shading model that does not conserve energy, such as un-normalised Blinn-Phong, or diffuse and specular strengths that together reflect more than arrives.
Ask for
A test with a white surface under a light of known strength, checking it never returns more than it receives, and a physically based model for materials that fail.

Highlights jump between vertices

Cause
Per-vertex lighting on large triangles. The highlight only exists where a vertex happens to catch it, so it snaps, smears into triangle shapes or vanishes as things move.
Ask for
Per-pixel shading for those materials, or meshes dense enough for vertex lighting, checked with a moving light.

Toon bands crawl and break up

Cause
Hard thresholds on N·L flip with tiny changes in the normal, from animation, low-poly meshes or noisy normal maps, and nothing softens the step.
Ask for
A band edge softened over about a pixel, cleaned or hand-edited normals on characters, and a review of the look in motion as well as in stills.

Most engines can show the terms apart: Unreal's Detail Lighting and Lighting Only view modes, the lighting debug modes in Unity's Rendering Debugger, and Godot's Lighting and Unshaded debug draws. A frame capture tool shows which shader variant each draw used.

7 Say it to your AI

Good shading briefs name the engine and pipeline, say which materials the change applies to, describe the look with references, and ask how it will be checked in motion. Adapt these.

BriefUse when a game needs a stylised look
Engine: Unity 6.6, URP, Forward+. Target: PC and consoles at 60 fps.
Our characters should look like 2D animation: three flat shade colours chosen by the art director, a hard-edged highlight and ink outlines. Environments stay physically based with the Lit shader.

1. Compare three ways to build the character shading: the Unity Toon Shader package, a Shader Graph, and hand-written HLSL. For each, list what it supports, what it costs per character, and the maintenance risk.
2. Keep the main light's shadows and our additional lights working on characters.
3. Propose an outline method and its cost in extra draws or passes.
4. Describe how we will check that band edges do not crawl during animation, with a test scene and camera path.
  • Naming the pipeline and path fixes which lighting and shadow functions are available.
  • Splitting characters from environments stops a global change nobody asked for.
  • Asking for a motion test targets the most common way toon shading fails.
Bug reportUse when materials glow or look like plastic
Mobile build, Unity 6.6, URP. Props use the Simple Lit shader. Since the art team raised the sun's intensity, white-painted walls clip to flat white and metal props look like grey plastic. The same assets in the PC build, which uses Lit, look right.

Find the cause before changing any material. Compare what Simple Lit and Lit return for the same material under the same light: measure a white wall's output against the light arriving. Tell us which props need the Lit shader, what that costs on our lowest-spec phone, and which can stay on Simple Lit with adjusted values.
  • The same assets look right on PC, so the shader is the first suspect.
  • Asking for a measurement turns "looks wrong" into numbers.
  • The cost question keeps the fix inside the mobile budget.

Questions to ask when reviewing shading work

  1. Which shading model does each family of materials use, and why?Skin, cloth and foliage on the default model look wrong; special models everywhere cost variants.
  2. Does any material send out more light than it receives?Non-normalised specular and stacked strengths make surfaces glow under bright lights.
  3. Where does the light in the shadows come from?A flat ambient colour flattens scenes; sky light, probes and GI keep their shape.
  4. Is lighting computed per pixel everywhere it matters?Per-vertex lighting on large triangles loses or smears highlights.
  5. For stylised looks, what keeps bands and outlines stable in motion?Hard steps alias; they need softened edges, clean normals and a test in motion.
  6. How many new shader variants does this add?Every model and option multiplies compile time, memory and switching cost.

8 Check yourself

  1. The sun hits a sloping roof at 60° from the roof's normal. How bright is the roof's diffuse light compared with a roof facing the sun head-on?

    Show answer

    Half as bright. Lambert diffuse scales with N·L, the cosine of the angle, and cos 60° = 0.5. The same sunlight is spread over twice the area.

  2. You walk slowly around a polished bronze statue under a fixed lamp. What moves across its surface as you walk, and what stays put?

    Show answer

    The specular highlights move, because specular light depends on the view direction. The diffuse shading stays put, because it depends only on the normal and the light.

  3. Two materials have the same roughness. One uses Blinn-Phong with the matching exponent, the other GGX. Their highlights peak at the same brightness. What looks different?

    Show answer

    The falloff around the highlight. GGX has a longer tail, so its highlight has a soft glow around a sharp core, while the Blinn-Phong highlight fades to nothing sooner.
