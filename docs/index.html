Who it is for

You are sharp and comfortable working with AI tools, and you have never opened a renderer. You will lead or build a game where AI agents write much of the code and content. Your job is to know what to ask for, judge what comes back, and make the trade-off calls.

So the book teaches recognition and judgement first: what a technique is, why it exists, what it costs and how it fails. Writing the code yourself comes last and stays optional.

By the end you can

  • Follow one asset from an artist's tool to a lit pixel and name every stage it passes through.
  • Explain what a draw call, vertex buffer, shader, G-buffer and render pass are, and what each one costs.
  • Decode a feature list or a settings menu: SSAO against GTAO, SSR, SSGI, CSM, TAA, DLSS, Lumen, Nanite.
  • Look at a visual bug and name the technique most likely at fault.
  • Read a frame-time graph and a frame capture well enough to ask the right next question.
  • Write a brief an AI agent can act on, and review what it returns.
  • Choose lighting, shadow and anti-aliasing approaches that fit a platform and a frame budget.

The route the book follows

The book follows the path real data takes: from a team's idea, through art tools and the engine, across the CPU and into the GPU, until it leaves as a finished image. Each part owns a stretch of that route. Part 0 walks the whole route once, quickly, before any detail.

How every chapter works

Every chapter has the same eight parts in the same order, so you always know where to look. Parts four, five and seven are the ones a lead uses most: cost, failure and how to brief the work.

  1. The pictureOne diagram that holds the whole idea, before any prose.
  2. In plain wordsThe idea without jargon, an everyday comparison, and the problem it was invented to solve.
  3. How it worksThe mechanism step by step, with an interactive where motion explains it better.
  4. What it costsMilliseconds, memory, and what makes the cost grow.
  5. How it breaksThe visual faults it produces, what each looks like, and the usual fix.
  6. In the enginesWhat Unreal, Unity and Godot call it, and where the settings live.
  7. Say it to your AIBriefs and review questions that use the chapter's vocabulary, ready to copy.
  8. Check yourselfThree questions and the terms the chapter introduced.

Reading conventions

  • A dotted underline marks a glossary term. Hover or focus it for a definition; select it to open the glossary.
  • Colour means the same thing in every diagram: blue is the idea the chapter is about, teal is geometry and vertex data, amber is light, and rose is screen-space data such as depth. Colour never carries meaning alone; every coloured mark also has a label.
  • Maths and code sit in fold-outs marked +. You can skip every one of them.
  • Press / anywhere to search chapters and terms.
