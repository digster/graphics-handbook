9.6 Special surfaces and effects

A few things in every game refuse to behave like ordinary opaque surfaces: smoke and sparks, painted-on damage, water, leaves, skin, hair and the ground itself. Each breaks a rule the rest of the renderer relies on, so each needs its own trick, and each has its own way of eating the frame budget. This chapter is a field guide to them.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Most of a frame is made of ordinary surfaces: walls, floors, crates, armour. The renderer can assume a lot about them. They are opaque, so only the nearest one matters at each pixel. Light bounces off where it lands. Each pixel is shaded about once. Each surface has its own texture. They hold still, or follow a skeleton.

The surfaces in this chapter each break at least one of those assumptions, and every trick that follows is a way of getting back to something the GPU can do quickly.

An everyday comparison

A theatre stage. Most of the set is solid flats that the lighting crew handles routinely. Then there is the smoke machine, the rain curtain, the actor's wig and the painted backdrop of distant hills. Each needs a specialist and its own rig, and each can go wrong in front of the audience in its own way.

The problem it solves

These surfaces are what make a world feel alive: smoke drifting, water moving, grass bending, skin that looks like skin. Without special handling they look like cardboard cut-outs, or they cost so much that the frame rate collapses whenever they fill the screen.

Why a lead cares

This is where content and performance collide. An artist can add a smoke column that doubles GPU time without touching any code. The choice between hair cards and hair strands decides which platforms a character can ship on. Most of these effects need a budget per effect and per platform, set early, not a single quality toggle added at the end.

3 How it works

One step per surface type, starting with the effects that float in the air and ending with the ground.

1 Particles and particle systems

Smoke, sparks, dust, rain, embers and magic are mostly made of particles. A particle system is an emitter that spawns many small, short-lived elements, moves them every frame under simple rules such as gravity, drag and wind, and draws each one, usually as a camera-facing card, a billboard, with a soft texture.

Running the simulation on the CPU is flexible: gameplay code can read any particle, and a spark can trigger a sound when it hits the floor. GPU particles run the spawn and update steps in compute shaders instead, so hundreds of thousands of particles cost little CPU time. Unity's documentation puts its CPU Particle System at thousands of particles and its GPU VFX Graph at millions. The catch is that the CPU no longer knows where the particles are, so the engine cannot work out their bounds for culling: Niagara asks for Fixed Bounds on GPU emitters, and Godot's GPUParticles3D has a Visibility AABB. Particles that fly outside those bounds are culled while still visible on screen.

Simulation is rarely the real cost, though. Most particles are blended, see-through quads, so they cannot hide each other and every layer is shaded. That is overdraw, and section 4 puts numbers on it. Blended particles must also be drawn back to front, the transparency sorting of 5.5, or the layers mix in the wrong order.

2 Flipbooks

Few particle effects simulate the shape of a billowing puff of smoke, a flickering flame or an expanding explosion. They play a flipbook instead, an animation stored as a grid of frames in one texture, often rendered offline from a fluid simulation. Each particle shows one cell at a time and steps through them as it ages.

3 Soft particles

A sprite is flat, but the smoke it shows is not. Where the card passes through a wall or the floor, the depth test cuts it along a perfectly straight line, and the illusion breaks. Soft particles fix it by reading the depth buffer: wherever the sprite is close to what is already drawn behind it, it fades out. NVIDIA's Tristan Lorach described the technique in 2007, using Direct3D 10's then-new ability to read the depth buffer as a texture.

The same depth difference turns up again and again in this chapter: foam where water meets the shore, and fog on particles in 9.5. It costs one depth read per pixel, and it needs the depth buffer to be available as a texture while transparent objects are drawn, which is one reason engines copy it.

4 Decals

Bullet holes, scorch marks, puddles, posters and road markings are decals: a texture projected from a box onto whatever surfaces are inside it. In a deferred renderer the cheapest kind is the deferred decal of 7.3, which edits the G-buffer's colour, normal and roughness before lighting, so the decal is lit exactly like the wall under it.

Two things go wrong. A box projects straight along one axis, so on faces parallel to the projection the texture smears into long streaks; projectors fade by angle to hide it. And decals land on everything in the box, including a character walking past, so engines let you choose what receives them: Godot's Cull Mask, the decal response setting on Unreal materials, rendering layers in Unity. Decal cost grows with the screen area they cover, much like particles.

5 Water

A water surface has almost no colour of its own. What you see is the sky reflected on it and the floor seen through it, each bent by the waves. So a water shader is mostly a set of reads from images the frame has already made.

Moving water needs one more trick. A flow map is a texture whose colours store a direction per point; the shader pushes the water's normal and foam textures along it, so a river streams around rocks instead of sliding as one sheet. Pushing a texture forever stretches it, so shaders run two copies half a cycle apart and blend between them.

Because water reads the scene behind it, it must be drawn after the opaque scene. Unreal's Single Layer Water shading model runs as its own pass after the base pass and lighting and before ordinary translucency, for exactly that reason.

6 Foliage

Grass and leaves are drawn as cards whose texture alpha cuts out the shape: alpha-tested foliage, from 5.4. The pixel shader runs over the whole card and then discards most of it, so dense grass is costly in shading, not only in triangles. Three tricks sit on top.

Light passing right through a thin surface, a leaf, an ear, a curtain, is called transmission. Shaders estimate it from the surface's thickness and the light behind it; Unreal's Two Sided Foliage shading model, HDRP's Translucent material type and Godot's Backlight all provide it.

7 Subsurface scattering: skin, wax and leaves

Light that enters skin does not bounce straight back out. It wanders underneath, scattering off cells and blood, and leaves a few millimetres from where it came in. That is subsurface scattering. It softens the lighting, makes the edge of a shadow glow red, and is the difference between skin and painted plastic. Wax, marble, milk, jade and leaves do the same.

Simplified

Screen-space blurring only knows about what is on screen, so it cannot carry light through an ear from a lamp behind the head; that is transmission's job, computed separately from thickness. Offline renderers trace the wandering paths for real, and some engines offer ray-traced subsurface scattering on high-end hardware.

8 Hair and fur

A human head has on the order of a hundred thousand hairs, each far thinner than a pixel at any normal viewing distance, partly see-through, and lit by light that scatters along and through the fibres. Games draw them in one of two ways.

HDRP's documentation recommends the split most productions use: strands for main characters, cards for secondary characters and lower levels of detail. Unreal can generate the cards from the same groom, so the hair is authored once. Fur on animals is the same problem at a different density, and can be drawn with cards or with shells, stacked copies of the skin pushed outwards with a hair texture.

9 Terrain

Ground that stretches for kilometres cannot be one mesh with one texture. Terrain is built from a height map: a regular grid of vertices raised by the map's values, split into sections so that distant sections use fewer triangles. Its look comes from a handful of material layers, grass, rock, sand, snow, that artists paint where they want them.

The other enemy is repetition. A rock texture that looks good over two metres repeats hundreds of times across a valley, and the eye finds the grid. Terrain shaders mix in a large-scale variation texture, blend detail textures at two scales and offset or rotate tiles. Large terrains also lean on virtual texturing: Unreal's Runtime Virtual Texturing caches the result of the layered terrain material, so the blend is computed once and reused, and roads or decal-like materials written into it blend with the ground.

10 Cloth

Cloth adds two problems. Its motion is simulated: a set of points joined by springs, pushed by wind and collisions, solved every frame on the CPU or the GPU, with the mesh following the points. Its look needs the cloth shading model of 7.2, whose sheen at grazing angles the default model cannot produce. Small flags and banners often skip the simulation: the Lantern Courtyard's banner moves in its vertex shader, displaced by a wind function that is zero along the rod and grows towards the hem.

4 What it costs

Almost every effect in this chapter is priced in screen area. A small smoke puff, a decal on a wall or a patch of grass in the distance costs little; the same effects filling the screen can cost several milliseconds.

Worked numbers. A 1080p frame has 2,073,600 pixels. A smoke column built from 40 sprites, each covering a quarter of the screen, asks for 40 × 518,400 = 20.7 million pixel shader runs: ten full screens of shading, for one effect. If each run costs even a quarter of an opaque pixel, that is still two and a half screens of opaque shading added to the frame.

  • Screen area of see-through effectsSmoke, fog cards, fire, water and hair cards cost per pixel per layer. A close camera is the worst case, which is why explosions tank frame rate in cutscenes.
  • Particle count and simulationCPU particles cost game-thread time per particle; GPU particles move that to compute shaders, where it is usually small next to the shading.
  • Layers per terrain sectionEvery layer adds texture reads on every terrain pixel, and in URP every four layers add a pass.
  • Skin on screenScreen-space subsurface scattering costs per pixel of skin, so a close-up costs more than a crowd. HDRP's documentation puts its High Quality mode at about two and a half times the default.
  • Hair strands and their shadowsStrand count, shadow-casting lights and how many characters use strands at once; engines cap strands and fall back to cards.

The usual trade

Give each heavy effect a budget measured at its worst camera position, not its average, and decide early what each platform tier drops: resolution of transparents first, then particle counts, then hair strands for cards, then terrain layers. Effects authored without a budget are the most common late-project performance surprise.

5 How it breaks

Most of these bugs come from layers drawn in the wrong order, flat geometry that does not match what it shows, and effects that cost far more up close.

Hard lines where smoke meets walls and floors

Cause
Particles without depth fading, or a depth buffer the particle shader cannot read on this platform or path.
Ask for
Soft particles with a fade distance suited to the effect's size, and a check that the depth texture is available where they render.

Particles that pop in front of each other

Cause
Blended layers sorted by emitter or object centre, which swap order as the camera moves, or not sorted at all.
Ask for
Per-particle sorting by view depth where it matters, sort priorities between overlapping emitters, or additive and alpha-tested looks that do not care about order.

Decals that smear down the sides of objects

Cause
A projection box deeper than needed, and no fade by angle, so faces parallel to the projection receive stretched texels.
Ask for
Shallow projection boxes, angle fading, and receiving surfaces limited by layer or mask.

Frame time spikes when the camera enters an effect

Cause
Overdraw: large blended particles filling the screen, each layer shaded with a costly lit material.
Ask for
A shader complexity or overdraw view of the worst shot, fewer and smaller particles near the camera, cheaper materials, and low-resolution transparents.

Hair with gaps, hard edges or a dark halo

Cause
Hair cards sorted wrongly or alpha-tested too hard, missing scalp colour under the cards, or temporal anti-aliasing smearing dithered hair.
Ask for
A sorting or dithering approach chosen for the engine's anti-aliasing, a scalp texture under the hair, and close-up checks in motion.

Terrain that shows a repeating grid

Cause
One texture per layer tiled at one scale across the whole terrain.
Ask for
A large-scale variation texture, detail at two scales, randomised tiling, and a review from a high vantage point, not only at ground level.

7 Say it to your AI

Effect briefs go well when they give the worst camera position, the effect's size on screen, the platform and a budget for that one effect, and ask how the cost will be measured.

Unreal Engine 5.8, PlayStation 5, 60 fps target.
Our building-collapse effect is fine from a distance, but when the camera is inside the dust cloud GPU time goes from 13 ms to 24 ms. The effect has 5 Niagara emitters, about 300 large sprite particles with a lit translucent material, plus a flipbook smoke layer.

2. List fixes ranked by visual impact: particle size and count near the camera, material cost, flipbook use, lower-resolution translucency.
3. Estimate what each fix saves, and say how to measure it from the same camera position.
4. Propose a per-effect budget we can check in future reviews.

Questions to ask when reviewing effects and special surfaces

  1. What does this cost at its worst camera position?Average cost hides the close-up that drops frames.
  2. How are the see-through layers ordered, and what happens when two effects overlap?Sorting by object centre fails for large or intersecting effects.
  3. Does it read the depth buffer, and is depth available there on every platform and render path?Soft particles, water foam and refraction all need it.
  4. Where are the bounds of GPU particles, and what happens if particles leave them?Particles outside fixed bounds are culled while still on screen.
  5. What does each platform tier drop?Strands, terrain layers and full-resolution transparents should have planned fallbacks.

8 Check yourself

  1. A fire effect has only 60 particles, yet the frame rate halves when the player stands next to it. Why, and what would you ask for first?

    Show answer

    Overdraw. Close to the camera each blended sprite covers a large part of the screen, and every layer is shaded at every pixel it covers. Ask for an overdraw or shader complexity view of that shot, then fewer or smaller particles near the camera, a cheaper material, or lower-resolution transparents.

  2. Why does screen-space subsurface scattering blur red light further than blue?

    Show answer

    In real skin, red light travels further beneath the surface before it comes out, while blue is absorbed or scattered out sooner. The diffusion profile copies that with a wider blur for red, which is what makes shadow edges on skin glow red.

  3. A URP terrain uses six layers and renders more slowly than expected. What is happening?

    Show answer

    URP renders four terrain layers per pass, so six layers need a second pass over the terrain. Unity recommends four layers per terrain tile; painting different tiles with different sets of four keeps each tile to one pass.
