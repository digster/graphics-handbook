10.1 A deferred frame, pass by pass

Every chapter so far explained one piece of a frame. This one runs them in order on a real frame of the Lantern Courtyard: what each pass reads, what it writes, which chapter explains it and what it typically costs. Then it shows where a shipping game adds what this small renderer leaves out, how passes overlap on the GPU, and how to read a frame of your own.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A deferred frame is built in stages. First the renderer works out what is where: what the sun can see, and which surface covers each pixel, with its colour, direction and roughness. Then it works out how much light reaches each of those surfaces. Then it adds what sits in front of them: the sky, fog, glass, smoke. Finally it turns the result into a picture for a screen and puts the interface on top.

Pass ordering obeys a single rule. A pass runs after every pass whose output it reads. Lighting cannot start before the G-buffer exists, and bloom needs the lit image. Passes that do not read each other's output can run in either order, or at the same time.

An everyday comparison

A car assembly line. Each station takes parts made earlier and adds something: the body is welded before it is painted, and painted before the seats go in. No station can start before the parts it needs exist, but stations that do not depend on each other can work side by side.

The problem it solves

When a frame is too slow or looks wrong, the first question is always which pass. Knowing the order and what each pass reads tells you where to look, and what a change in one pass will do to every pass after it.

Why a lead cares

Performance reviews and most visual bug reports end at one pass. A frame capture lists the passes in this order with their times. A lead who can read it can ask where the extra 3 ms went, check that a fix landed in the right pass, and notice when a new feature quietly added a pass that nobody budgeted.

3 How it works

The steps follow the frame. Use the scrubber in step 1 to see each pass's output as you read; for each pass the chapter lists what it reads, what it writes, where the book explains it, and a typical cost. Costs marked illustrative are typical ranges for a 1080p frame on a current console, not measurements; ranges with a source are labelled with it.

2 Before the first pass: the CPU

The GPU's frame starts on the CPU. The engine decides what is visible (4.4 Culling), sorts and batches what is left into draw calls (4.3 Draw calls), and records them into command buffers that are handed to the GPU. While the GPU draws this frame, the CPU is already preparing the next one (2.2 The game loop and threads). Everything below happens on the GPU, in the order the command buffers say.

3 Shadow pass

The shadow pass draws every shadow caster from the light's point of view and keeps only depth. The courtyard's sun never moves, so it uses one orthographic camera fitted around the courtyard, with a small depth offset against acne.

  • Reads: the scene's meshes, seen from the sun.
  • Writes: a 2048 × 2048, 24-bit depth image, the shadow map.
  • Explained in: 7.4 Shadow maps, 7.5 Shadows at scale.
  • Typical cost: 1 to 3 ms for a sun with cascades, illustrative. It grows with the casters and cascades, not with screen resolution.

4 G-buffer pass

Every visible object is drawn once, and instead of colours the pixel shader writes what the surface is: its albedo, which way it faces, how rough and metallic it is. That is the G-buffer. Many engines draw a depth pre-pass first, so the G-buffer pass shades each pixel only once; the courtyard is simple enough to skip it.

  • Reads: meshes, and each material's textures (6.1 Textures and UVs, 6.5 PBR materials).
  • Writes: albedo in 8-bit sRGB, normals in 10:10:10:2, a layer of roughness, metallic, emissive and wetness in 8 bits each, and 24-bit depth.
  • Explained in: 7.3 Forward, deferred and clustered, 5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw, 5.5 Depth, stencil and blending.
  • Typical cost: 1.5 to 4 ms, illustrative. It grows with triangles, overdraw and material complexity, and every extra byte per pixel is written and later read at every pixel.

5 Ambient occlusion

With depth and normals on hand, the renderer estimates how enclosed each point is. The courtyard uses SSAO with 12 samples per pixel at half resolution, then two blur passes that stop at silhouettes.

  • Reads: depth and normals.
  • Writes: one 8-bit channel at half width and half height.
  • Explained in: 8.3 Ambient occlusion: SSAO to GTAO.
  • Typical cost: 0.5 to 2 ms, from 8.3: 0.5 ms for the original GTAO on PlayStation 4 at 1080p.

6 Lighting

One full-screen pass turns surfaces into light. For each pixel the courtyard rebuilds the world position from depth, adds the sun with a 12-tap filtered shadow lookup, the lantern with an inverse-square falloff that reaches zero at 10 m, sky light darkened by AO, and the glow of the lantern's glass. Games with many lights first sort them into tiles or clusters so each pixel loops over only the lights that reach it, and add global illumination here.

  • Reads: all three G-buffer layers, depth, AO and the shadow map.
  • Writes: HDR light in 16-bit floating point, values far above 1.
  • Explained in: 7.1 Lights, 7.2 Shading models, 7.3 Forward, deferred and clustered, 8.5 Real-time GI families.
  • Typical cost: 1 to 3 ms, illustrative. It grows with the number of lights touching each pixel and with shadow filtering; real-time GI adds its own budget, which Epic puts at 4 ms for Lumen in a 60 fps console game.

7 Reflections and screen-space GI

The courtyard marches reflected rays through the depth buffer, but only from puddle pixels; rays that leave the screen take the sky's colour instead. Games apply SSR to every glossy surface and blend it with reflection probes, and may add SSGI, the same marching used for bounced light.

  • Reads: the HDR light, depth, normals and the material layer.
  • Writes: a new HDR image with reflections added.
  • Explained in: 7.6 Reflections and image-based lighting, 8.4 Screen-space tricks and their blind spots.
  • Typical cost: 0.5 to 2 ms; 8.4 cites 2 ms for one published screen-space tracer at 1080p on a 2014 laptop GPU.

8 Fog and atmosphere

The air goes in after the surfaces are lit. The courtyard applies exponential height fog in closed form, coloured from the horizon sky, and adds the lantern's glow in the haze. A game may build a froxel volume for volumetric fog earlier in the frame and only apply it here, and draws the sky in this phase too.

  • Reads: the HDR image and depth.
  • Writes: a new HDR image with fog.
  • Explained in: 9.5 Atmosphere.
  • Typical cost: about 0.25 ms to apply fog at every pixel on Xbox One; about 1 ms for volumetric fog on PlayStation 4 at Unreal's High setting (both from 9.5).

9 Transparents and particles

The courtyard has no glass, smoke or water, so its frame has no step here. A game's frame does. The transparents pass draws see-through surfaces after the opaque scene is lit, sorted back to front and lit as they are drawn, because a G-buffer holds only one surface per pixel. Particles, water and glass all go here, with their soft-particle depth reads and their own fog.

  • Reads: the lit HDR image, depth for soft particles and foam, and a colour copy for refraction.
  • Writes: blends into the HDR image; usually no depth.
  • Explained in: 5.5 Depth, stencil and blending, 9.6 Special surfaces and effects.
  • Typical cost: from almost nothing to several milliseconds, illustrative; it is set by screen coverage, and a camera inside smoke is the worst case.

10 Anti-aliasing and upscaling

Games running TAA or a temporal upscaler put it here, after every surface is in the image and before most camera effects, so the effects see a stable, anti-aliased picture. Everything before it can run at a lower internal resolution; Unreal's documentation notes that post-processing after its temporal upsample runs at full resolution. The courtyard uses FXAA in its final pass instead, which needs no history.

  • Reads: the HDR image, motion vectors, depth and last frame's history.
  • Writes: an anti-aliased HDR image, at output resolution if it upscales, and a new history.
  • Explained in: 9.2 Anti-aliasing, 9.3 Upscaling and frame generation.
  • Typical cost: 0.5 to 1.5 ms, illustrative, more for machine-learned upscalers on weaker GPUs.

11 Bloom and the other camera effects

The courtyard keeps light brighter than 2.5 after an exposure of 2, shrinks it four times from half size to a sixteenth and blurs it back up. Games add depth of field and motion blur in this phase.

  • Reads: the HDR image after fog.
  • Writes: a chain of HDR images from half to a sixteenth of the width and height.
  • Explained in: 9.4 Camera effects.
  • Typical cost: 0.35 ms for bloom in a shipped Xbox One game, from 9.4; in the same game depth of field took 1.27 ms in a close-up and motion blur 0.52 ms.

12 Tone map

One pass turns scene light into display values: the courtyard multiplies by its exposure, adds 10% of the bloom, darkens the corners by up to 30%, applies a fit of the ACES filmic curve, encodes to sRGB and adds a trace of noise against banding.

  • Reads: the HDR image and the bloom chain.
  • Writes: an 8-bit image, with brightness stored in its alpha channel for FXAA.
  • Explained in: 9.1 HDR, exposure and tone mapping, 3.2 Colour done right.
  • Typical cost: well under half a millisecond, illustrative: one full-screen pass that reads and writes each pixel once, often merged with colour grading and the lens effects.

13 Final: anti-aliasing, the UI and present

The courtyard's last pass runs FXAA into the canvas. A game draws its interface here. UI compositing puts the HUD and menus over the finished picture after every camera effect, at the display's own resolution, so text stays sharp and is never blurred, fogged or smeared by TAA. Then the frame is handed to the display with present, into the swap chain.

  • Reads: the tone-mapped image, then the UI's own textures and text.
  • Writes: the back buffer that is presented.
  • Explained in: 9.2 Anti-aliasing, 3.1 Pixels and displays.
  • Typical cost: FXAA a fraction of a millisecond; UI from a tenth of a millisecond to about one for heavy menus, illustrative.

14 The frame on a timeline

A frame timeline draws the passes as bars along time, to scale, and is what most GPU profilers show first. Put the typical costs above on one line and the shape of a 60 fps frame appears: geometry and lighting take about half, and what is left has to cover the air, the glass, the camera effects and some headroom.

15 Async compute: two queues at once

The timeline above is a single line, but a GPU can work on more than one queue. Async compute submits some compute shader passes to a second queue, so they run at the same time as the graphics work. It pays off when the two use different parts of the GPU: a shadow pass is mostly rasterising and leaves the shader cores idle, which NVIDIA's guidance names as a good partner for compute work.

It is not free. Both queues share the same cores, caches and memory, so each overlapped pass runs slower than it would alone, and two bandwidth-heavy passes together can be slower than one after the other. NVIDIA advises measuring the whole frame, not the pass. Async work also needs explicit waits before anything reads its results; a missing one is a race that shows up as flickering garbage on some GPUs and not others, which is one reason engines let the frame graph insert them.

16 Reading a frame of your own

Every engine can show its own frame the way the scrubber shows the courtyard's. A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX records one frame and lists every pass in order, with the images it read and wrote and how long it took on the GPU. The engines have their own views as well: Unity's Frame Debugger steps through a frame's events, Unreal's stat GPU shows the frame's GPU statistics, and Godot's Visual Profiler splits CPU and GPU time by rendering category. 11.5 Profilers and the settings menu covers the tools; this chapter is the map for reading what they show.

Read a capture in three passes of your own: find the phases, find the longest bars, then check each long bar against what it should cost and what makes it grow. A shadow pass that takes 5 ms has too many casters or cascades; a transparents pass that triples in one shot has a camera inside an effect.

17 How the frame changes

The four phases survive in every renderer; the passes inside them change. Unreal Engine 5 at the high end replaces several of the courtyard's passes with heavier systems, and a phone keeps the frame short because every extra full-screen pass is expensive on a tile-based GPU.

Worked numbers for memory. At 1920 × 1080 each 4-byte-per-pixel image is 8.3 MB and each half-float HDR image 16.6 MB. The courtyard's frame would hold a 16.8 MB shadow map, 33.2 MB of G-buffer and depth, 1 MB of AO, three HDR images at 49.8 MB, an 11 MB bloom chain and an 8.3 MB output: about 120 MB. A frame graph would let the HDR images and the G-buffer share memory once nothing reads them, as 5.6 Shaders in practice shows, which is one reason engines adopted them.

  • ResolutionEvery full-screen pass scales with pixels: 4K is four times 1080p. Upscaling moves most passes to the lower internal resolution.
  • Geometry and castersThe shadow, depth and G-buffer passes grow with triangles and draws, and the shadow pass with every cascade and shadowed light.
  • Lights and GILighting grows with lights per pixel; real-time GI brings a budget of its own.
  • See-through coverageTransparents and particles grow with the screen area they cover, layer by layer.
  • Passes and bytesEach extra pass reads and writes whole images; on bandwidth-limited and tile-based GPUs that traffic, not the maths, is the cost.

The usual trade

Teams set a budget per phase early, for example shadows 2 ms and transparents 1.5 ms on the base console, and check every capture against it. New features then arrive with a pass, a cost and a place in the order already argued for, instead of being found in the profiler a month before ship.

5 How it breaks

Frame-level bugs come from a pass that costs too much, passes in the wrong order, surfaces that skip a pass, data from the wrong frame and queues that race each other.

One pass blows the budget

Cause
A pass far over its expected range: too many shadow casters, a transparent effect filling the screen, a GI or fog setting left at its highest quality.
Ask for
A frame capture of the worst shot, per-pass times against the budget table, and the setting or content change that brings that one pass back into range.

Dull glow or clipped highlights

Cause
An effect that needs HDR values running after the tone map, such as bloom or a custom glow, where everything bright has already been squeezed below 1.
Ask for
The pass moved before the tone map, and a list of where every custom pass is injected and why.

Glass and particles left out of effects

Cause
Transparents write no depth and miss the G-buffer, so depth of field blurs them by what lies behind, and AO, SSR and full-screen fog skip them.
Ask for
A decision per transparent material on where it is composited, fog applied in its own shader, and checks of glass in close-ups.

A flash or a pop on camera cuts

Cause
Passes that reuse last frame's results, such as exposure, TAA history or occlusion from the previous depth, still hold the old shot for a frame.
Ask for
History reset on cuts and teleports, and a list of every pass that reads data from an earlier frame.

Flickering garbage, on some GPUs only

Cause
A pass reading an image before another queue, or another pass, has finished writing it: a missing wait or barrier, often after work was moved to async compute.
Ask for
The read and write declarations checked in the frame graph, a run with async compute switched off to confirm, and validation layers on.

A soft, smeared or ghosting HUD

Cause
The interface drawn before the upscaler, TAA or motion blur, so it is rendered at internal resolution and blended with history.
Ask for
UI composited after every camera effect at output resolution, and world-space UI kept out of motion blur.

7 Say it to your AI

Frame-level briefs go well when they come with a capture or its numbers, name the pass and the phase, and ask for changes in terms of reads, writes and order.

  • Reads and writes are what a frame graph needs, and what a reviewer checks.
  • Translucents and UI are where full-screen effects usually go wrong.

Questions to ask when reviewing frame-level work

  1. Which pass does this change, and what reads its output afterwards?A change in one pass can break every pass downstream.
  2. Where in the order does a new pass run, and at which resolution?Before or after the tone map, transparents and the upscaler changes both the look and the cost.
  3. What does the capture say before and after?Per-pass times on the worst shot are the only proof of a performance change.
  4. Does anything now read data from an earlier frame?History needs resetting on cuts, or it shows as a flash or a smear.
  5. If work moved to async compute, was the whole frame measured on every target GPU?Overlap can be slower, and missing waits fail only on some hardware.

8 Check yourself

  1. Why must the lighting pass run after the AO pass, while the shadow pass and the G-buffer pass could run in either order?

    Show answer

    Lighting reads the AO image, so AO must be finished first. The shadow pass and the G-buffer pass read only meshes and textures, not each other's output, so neither depends on the other; engines are free to order or overlap them.

  2. Glass looks blurred along with the wall behind it whenever depth of field is on. What is happening?

    Show answer

    Transparent surfaces do not write depth, so depth of field sees only the wall's depth at those pixels and blurs the glass by it. The fix is to choose where the glass is composited relative to depth of field, as Unreal's per-material translucency pass setting allows.

  3. Moving AO to async compute made the frame 0.3 ms slower. How can that be?

    Show answer

    Both queues share the GPU's cores, caches and memory. If the work it overlapped with was also limited by memory or by shader cores, the two slow each other down, and the waits add their own cost. Overlap only pays when the queues stress different parts of the GPU.
