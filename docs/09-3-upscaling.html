9.3 Upscaling and frame generation

Most games on current hardware no longer render every pixel they show. They render a quarter to a half of them, let a temporal upscaler rebuild the rest from earlier frames, and on some GPUs invent whole frames in between. This chapter explains how that works, what each mode really renders, what it costs in milliseconds and latency, and where it breaks.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Shading cost follows pixel count, and pixel counts have run ahead of GPUs: a 4K frame has 8,294,400 pixels, four times 1080p. So games render at a lower internal resolution and enlarge the result to the screen's resolution. Enlarging a single frame cannot invent detail it never sampled. The modern upscalers get around that with the trick from 9.2: the camera is jittered every frame, so each frame samples different points, and the upscaler accumulates those samples into a full-resolution history. Several low-resolution frames together hold more real detail than any one of them.

Frame generation goes a step further. Instead of making each frame cheaper, it makes extra frames: from two rendered frames and their motion, it creates one to five frames to show in between. The screen updates more often. The game does not respond any faster, because the in-between frames are made after the fact, and each real frame has to wait for them.

An everyday comparison

A survey team cannot visit every house in a city each week, so each week it visits a different quarter of the houses and keeps last month's answers for the rest. Its map is nearly as complete as a full census for a quarter of the cost, as long as people do not move house too often. Frame generation is the cartoonist's in-betweener: given two key drawings, they draw the poses between. The animation is smoother; the story moves at the key drawings' pace.

The problem it solves

4K screens, 120 Hz and higher refresh rates, and ray-traced lighting all multiply GPU work. Upscaling cuts the per-pixel work by half or more for a small loss in sharpness, and frame generation lets a game that renders 60 frames a second fill a 120 or 240 Hz screen.

Why a lead cares

Upscaling is now a core part of the frame budget, and often the default: Unreal Engine 5 ships with TSR on, and many games' recommended settings assume DLSS or FSR. It decides how sharp the game looks, inherits every TAA artifact from 9.2, and adds vendor integrations that must be tested per GPU family. Frame generation adds latency, can mangle the interface, and needs a high base frame rate to look right. Both show up in store pages and reviews, so decide early which ones you support and on which platforms.

3 How it works

There are four separate ideas here, and games often use all four together: render fewer pixels and upscale, change that resolution every frame to hold the budget, shade some regions more coarsely than others, and add generated frames at the end.

1 Quality modes: how many pixels are rendered

Upscalers offer quality modes, each a fixed ratio between the display and internal resolution per axis. The names are shared and the ratios are not. Intel changed XeSS's ratios in version 1.3, so XeSS Quality now renders fewer pixels than DLSS or FSR Quality.

NVIDIA and Intel tell developers to query the ratio at runtime rather than hard-code it. Unreal's TSR uses a screen percentage directly: 50% is the same as Performance.

Lower modes also take longer to recover. With a quarter of the samples per frame, the upscaler needs about four frames to collect one real sample for every shown pixel, so anything new on screen, a disocclusion or a camera cut, looks low-resolution for longer. Epic's TSR documentation makes the same calculation: at 50%, a newly revealed area needs four frames, 66 ms at 60 fps, to reach one sample per pixel.

2 Spatial and temporal upscaling

Spatial upscaling enlarges one frame by interpolating between its pixels. Bilinear and Catmull-Rom filters do only that; AMD's FSR 1 (2021) also finds edges and sharpens them, which makes a 1440p image look closer to 4K than a plain stretch. It is cheap and works anywhere, and it cannot restore detail that fell between the samples. AMD's documentation says to run FSR 1 after anti-aliasing and tone mapping, on an already anti-aliased image.

Temporal upscaling is TAA (9.2) with the history kept at display resolution. Each low-resolution frame is rendered with a different jitter, its samples are placed where they belong in the high-resolution history, and the history is reprojected with motion vectors. One pass both anti-aliases and upscales. Every artifact from 9.2 comes along: ghosting, smearing of things without motion vectors, flicker on thin detail, and blur in motion.

3 The temporal upscalers

They share a design and differ in how they decide, pixel by pixel, how much history to trust. Hand-written ones use rules like neighbourhood clamping. Machine-learned ones replace those rules with a neural network trained on very high quality reference images, which tends to keep more detail and ghost less, and needs matrix-maths hardware to run fast.

  • TSR, Temporal Super Resolution, is Unreal Engine 5's own upscaler and its default anti-aliasing. It is hand-written, runs on any GPU with Shader Model 5 and on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series consoles, and has scalability settings that trade quality for cost.
  • DLSS is NVIDIA's family for GeForce RTX GPUs. Super Resolution is its upscaler; DLAA is the same network at native resolution, used as anti-aliasing. DLSS 4 (2025) moved to a transformer model, and DLSS 4.5 (2026) added a second-generation one; NVIDIA notes that RTX 20 and 30 GPUs lack native FP8 support, so the newest models cost more there.
  • FSR is AMD's family. FSR 2 and 3 are hand-written temporal upscalers that run on most GPUs from any vendor. FSR Upscaling 4 is machine-learned; AMD's page lists it for Radeon RX 9000, 7000 and 6000 GPUs, and on other hardware the same API falls back to FSR 3.1.
  • XeSS is Intel's machine-learned upscaler. It uses the matrix (XMX) units of Arc GPUs, and a cross-vendor version runs on any GPU with Shader Model 6.4.
  • STP, Spatial-Temporal Post-processing, is Unity 6's built-in temporal upscaler for URP and HDRP. It needs compute shaders, does not run on OpenGL ES, and turns TAA on.

On consoles, Sony's PS5 Pro has its own machine-learned upscaler, PSSR, whose 2026 update Sony says stems from its Project Amethyst work with AMD, and NVIDIA says Nintendo Switch 2's Tensor Cores run DLSS.

What the upscaler needs is close to what TAA needs: colour, depth, motion vectors, and the jitter offset used this frame. Exposure (9.1) helps it handle HDR values; masks mark particles and transparent effects whose history should count for less. One more setting matters: textures must be sampled as if the game ran at display resolution, or they come out blurry. NVIDIA's formula is a mip bias of log2(internal width ÷ display width) − 1, which gives −2 at Performance; AMD uses the same formula, and Intel recommends log2(internal ÷ display) without the −1. Godot applies log2 of the 3D scale automatically, as its documentation describes.

4 Dynamic resolution: holding the frame budget

Dynamic resolution changes the internal resolution every few frames, from measured GPU time. When an explosion fills the screen and the frame would overrun its frame budget, it renders fewer pixels; when the load passes, it renders more. A temporal upscaler makes the changes hard to see, because the history stays at display resolution. Engines build it in for consoles: Unreal documents TSR's dynamic resolution support on console platforms, and HDRP uses hardware dynamic resolution on all consoles it supports.

Unreal's controller is driven by r.DynamicRes.OperationMode (off by default), with a minimum screen percentage of 50, a maximum of 100 and a frame time budget of 33.3 ms by default; you set the budget to your target. A good controller leaves headroom below the budget, changes resolution in small steps, and waits a few frames between changes, because measurements are noisy and jitter interacts with the change, as Epic's documentation notes.

5 Variable rate shading: coarser shading where nobody looks

Variable rate shading, VRS, lowers shading cost inside a frame instead of across it. The GPU runs the pixel shader once for a block of pixels, 2 × 1, 2 × 2, or on some hardware up to 4 × 4, and copies the result. Microsoft's Direct3D 12 specification says depth, stencil and coverage are still computed for every pixel, so edges stay sharp. The rate can be set per draw call, per triangle, or with a small screen-space image where each texel covers a tile of 8 × 8 or 16 × 16 pixels.

VRS saves pixel shading only, so it helps most when the frame is limited by expensive pixel shaders. It needs hardware support: Direct3D 12 VRS Tier 2 or Vulkan's fragment shading rate for the image-based form, and Unity lists PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series consoles among its supported platforms.

6 Frame generation and latency

Frame generation creates frames between two rendered ones. It uses both frames, their motion vectors and depth to estimate where everything was in between. Each generated frame is shown at its moment in between. DLSS Frame Generation runs on GeForce RTX 40 and 50 GPUs. Multi Frame Generation, on RTX 50 only, makes up to three frames per rendered one in DLSS 4 and up to five in DLSS 4.5, whose Dynamic mode picks the multiplier to match the display's refresh rate. AMD's FSR Frame Generation has a machine-learned version for Radeon RX 9000 and an analytical one, previously FSR 3, for any GPU with Shader Model 6.2. Intel's XeSS Frame Generation can generate up to three frames on Intel hardware.

Input is still read once per rendered frame, so a game generating from 30 rendered frames still responds like a 30 fps game, and a little later. Generated frames also have fewer real pixels to work from when the rendered frames are far apart, so artifacts grow as the base frame rate falls. AMD's guidance is that FSR Frame Generation runs best from at least 60 fps before generation and that below 30 fps should be avoided entirely.

The vendors pair frame generation with a latency reducer that removes the other big source of delay, the queue of frames waiting for the GPU (frame latency, 2.2). NVIDIA Reflex "synchronizes CPU and GPU pipelines for just-in-time rendering", in NVIDIA's words, and DLSS Frame Generation requires it. AMD Anti-Lag 2 does the same for Radeon GPUs from RDNA 1 onwards, and AMD pairs it with FSR Frame Generation; Intel requires its Xe Low Latency mode with XeSS Frame Generation. NVIDIA describes the pairing as generating frames "while maintaining great responsiveness with NVIDIA Reflex". Measure latency in your own game rather than assume it.

Simplified

The timeline ignores the GPU time generation takes away from rendering, which lowers the rendered frame rate a little, and the display's own refresh timing. With variable refresh rate and the vendors' frame pacing, generated frames are spaced evenly; without it, uneven spacing can show as stutter.

4 What it costs

Performance mode renders a quarter of the pixels, so it is tempting to expect a frame four times faster. It never is. Shadow maps, geometry processing and CPU work do not depend on resolution, everything after the upscaler runs at full resolution, and the upscaler has its own cost. Epic's own example in the TSR documentation: a frame took 57.50 ms at native 4K and 33.37 ms when rendered at 1080p and upscaled with TSR. That is 42% faster, against the 75% the pixel count suggests.

  • Output resolutionAn upscaler's cost follows the pixels it outputs, not the ones it reads: AMD notes that FSR 4 is mostly bound by the presentation resolution.
  • Work after the upscalerMotion blur, bloom, tone mapping and the interface run at full resolution whatever the mode. Epic devotes a section of the TSR documentation to these hidden costs.
  • Older or smaller GPUsMachine-learned upscalers run slower without matrix hardware: DLSS preset L takes 10.60 ms at 4K Performance on an RTX 2080 Ti against 1.67 ms on an RTX 4090.
  • Generated framesEach multiplier step adds generation time, 1.72 ms for 2× and 3.32 ms for 4× on an RTX 5090 at 4K, and that GPU time comes out of rendering.
  • Integration and testingEach vendor SDK has its own inputs, masks, presets and certification checklist, and each needs testing on its GPU family and every quality mode.

The usual trade

Budget the game at its target internal resolution from the start, not at native: Quality or Balanced at 1440p and 4K output on PC, and dynamic resolution with a temporal upscaler on consoles. Treat frame generation as an optional extra for players whose base frame rate is already good, never as the way to reach 60 fps.

5 How it breaks

Upscalers fail the way TAA does, more visibly at lower modes. Frame generation adds failures of its own at the interface and in timing.

Shimmer and fizz around moving things

Cause
Newly revealed areas and thin detail have few real samples; at Performance or Ultra Performance it takes many frames to fill them in.
Ask for
A higher mode or minimum dynamic resolution, the vendor's recommended jitter phase count, and a disocclusion test scene for each mode.

Blurry textures with upscaling on

Cause
Textures still choose mip levels for the internal resolution, so they are a level or two softer than at native.
Ask for
The vendor's mip bias formula applied per mode, an exception for flickering high-frequency textures, and a side-by-side check of signs and text against native.

Smeared particles and transparent effects

Cause
Particles, glass and animated materials write no motion vectors and are not in the depth buffer, so the upscaler blends them with history from where they were.
Ask for
Reactive or transparency masks for those materials, as the vendor SDKs describe, and motion vectors for any opaque animated surface.

Interface warping with frame generation

Cause
The generator receives a frame with the interface already drawn in and moves it with the scene, so text and crosshairs wobble or double.
Ask for
The frame without the interface plus the interface separately (NVIDIA calls them the hudless and UI buffers), and a test pass over every menu and HUD element with frame generation on.

High frame counter, sluggish controls

Cause
Frame generation from a low base frame rate: input is read only per rendered frame and each one waits behind the generated frames.
Ask for
Reflex, Anti-Lag 2 or Xe Low Latency always on with generation, a minimum base frame rate (AMD suggests 60), and latency measured, not inferred from the frame counter.

7 Say it to your AI

Upscaling briefs need the engine and pipeline, the output resolution and target frame rate per platform, which vendor SDKs you are allowed to ship, and the artifacts you care about. Ask for the mip bias, jitter and mask requirements explicitly; they are where integrations go wrong.

Questions to ask when reviewing upscaling work

  1. What internal resolution does each mode render on each platform, and what is the frame time in each?The mode names hide different ratios; the budget needs real numbers.
  2. Is the mip bias set from the vendor's formula and updated when the mode or dynamic resolution changes?Without it every texture is softer than at native.
  3. Which materials provide motion vectors, and which need reactive or transparency masks?Missing inputs are the main cause of smearing.
  4. Which passes run after the upscaler, and what do they cost at display resolution?That is the part of the frame upscaling does not shrink.
  5. With frame generation on, what is the base frame rate, which latency reducer is active, and was latency measured?A high frame counter can hide a sluggish game.

8 Check yourself

  1. At 4K output, how many pixels does DLSS or FSR Performance mode render, and why is the frame not four times faster?

    Show answer

    1920 × 1080, a quarter of 3840 × 2160. Work that does not depend on resolution (shadow maps, geometry, CPU) stays the same, the passes after the upscaler run at full 4K, and the upscaler itself costs a millisecond or more. Epic's TSR example went from 57.5 ms to 33.4 ms, 42% faster.

  2. A game renders 30 frames a second and uses 4× frame generation to show 120. Why does it still feel sluggish?

    Show answer

    Input is read only for rendered frames, 30 times a second, and each rendered frame is held back so the generated frames can be shown first. Generation from a low base rate also has more artifacts. A latency reducer helps, but AMD's guidance is to start from at least 60 fps.

  3. After turning on an upscaler, signs and posters look blurrier than at native, though edges look fine. What is missing?

    Show answer

    A negative mip bias. Textures pick mip levels from the internal resolution, so they are sampled one or two levels too soft. NVIDIA's formula, log2(internal width ÷ display width) − 1, gives −2 at Performance.
