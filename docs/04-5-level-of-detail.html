4.5 Level of detail

A boulder that fills the screen needs thousands of triangles. The same boulder fifty metres away covers a few hundred pixels and needs almost none of them. Level of detail swaps in simpler versions as objects shrink on screen, so the GPU spends its triangles where the player can see them.

LOD over distance

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A mesh is built to look right close up. Seen from far away, the same mesh covers a handful of pixels, and most of its triangles land inside pixels that would look the same without them. The GPU still processes every vertex and sets up every triangle.

Level of detail, or LOD, is the fix. Each important mesh ships in several versions, from full detail down to a few hundred triangles or a flat picture, and the engine draws the cheapest one that still looks the same at the size the object appears. The versions form a chain: LOD0 is the most detailed, then LOD1, LOD2 and so on.

Everyday comparison

Zoomed in on a street, an online map shows every building outline and house number. Zoomed out to a whole country, it shows motorways and city names, and the house numbers are gone. Nobody misses them, because at that size they would be a grey smudge.

LOD makes the same choice for meshes: each object gets the detail its size on screen can show.

The problem it solves

Without LOD, a field of 2,000 boulders costs the same 27.6 million triangles whether the boulders are beside the player or on the horizon.

With LOD the distant ones cost almost nothing, and the triangle budget goes to what the player is looking at.

Why a lead cares

LOD is one of the biggest levers on a game's geometry cost, and when it goes wrong it is one of the most visible quality problems: popping is a classic review complaint. It also costs people-time, because every asset needs its levels made, checked and tuned per platform. Newer systems such as Unreal's Nanite remove much of that authoring work but have their own limits, which you need to know before planning content around them.

3 How it works

One boulder runs through this chapter: 2 m tall, on a 1920 × 1080 screen, seen through a camera with a 60° vertical field of view. Eight steps take it from one mesh to a full LOD system.

1 Screen size decides, not distance

An engine could switch levels at fixed distances, but distance alone says little. A house 10 m away fills most of the screen, while a boulder at the same distance is a small lump. What matters is how big the object looks. Engines measure its screen size: the height of the object, or of a sphere around it, as a fraction of the screen's height. Each level has a threshold, and the engine draws the most detailed level whose threshold the object still reaches.

Screen size depends on the field of view as well as on distance. Zooming a sniper scope from 60° to 15° makes everything more than four times taller on screen, so distant objects should move up to more detailed levels while the scope is up.

Maths Height on screen from distance and field of view

height in pixels = screen height in pixels × object height ÷ (2 × distance × tan(vertical field of view ÷ 2))

For the boulder at 10 m: 1080 × 2 ÷ (2 × 10 × tan 30°) = 1080 × 2 ÷ 11.55 ≈ 187 pixels, which is 17% of the screen height.

Unity's LOD Group sets its thresholds as exactly this percentage of the screen height. Unreal's Static Mesh Editor shows the same kind of number as Current Screen Size: the mesh's height as a proportion of the viewport's height. Thresholds written as a fraction of the screen do not change with resolution. Thresholds written in pixels do: Godot's automatic mesh LOD and Nanite both work in pixels, so a 4K screen asks them for more detail at the same distance.

2 The LOD chain, and where it comes from

A typical chain has three to five mesh levels. A common rule removes a fixed share of the triangles at each step: the example LOD Group settings in Unreal's documentation keep 50% of the triangles per level, and Unity's Mesh LOD generator makes each level with about half the indices of the one before. The boulder here uses a steeper chain, a quarter of the triangles per level, with each level taking over when the height on screen halves. Halving the height quarters the area, so every level draws triangles of about the same size on screen. Below the last level the object is not drawn at all, a form of small-object culling.

Lower levels can be modelled by hand, but most start as mesh simplification, also called decimation. The standard method collapses one edge at a time: it merges the edge's two vertices into one and deletes the two triangles that shared the edge. Before each step it estimates how far the surface would move, and it always collapses the cheapest edge next. Garland and Heckbert's quadric error metric, published in 1997, is the classic way to make that estimate. Unreal, Unity and Godot all describe their automatic LOD generators as collapsing edges; Godot's uses the open-source meshoptimizer library.

Simplifiers do well on organic shapes and badly on thin parts, hard edges and texture seams. Unity's documentation warns that its generator can give poor results on strongly curved meshes and on meshes made of many separate pieces. Hero assets still get hand-made or hand-fixed levels, and every level needs checking in game.

Simplified

The boulder's levels in this chapter are the same shape sampled with coarser grids of triangles, not the output of a simplifier. A real simplifier keeps triangles where the shape needs them, so real levels are uneven inside but keep their outlines better.

3 Why tiny triangles waste work

One number tells you whether an object is drawn at a sensible level: its pixels per triangle, the pixels it covers divided by the triangles drawn. As triangles shrink toward a pixel, they stop adding visible detail and start to slow the GPU, for two reasons.

First, GPUs shade pixels in 2 × 2 blocks, pixel quads, so each pixel can be compared with its neighbours when filtering textures. A triangle that covers one pixel still runs the pixel shader for all four pixels of its quad and throws three results away. That waste is quad overdraw, covered in 5.4 Pixel shaders and overdraw.

Second, the hardware that turns triangles into pixels, described in 5.3 Rasterization, is built to be fast per pixel, not per triangle. Epic's Nanite presentation notes that GPUs set up at most about four triangles per clock, which is one reason Nanite draws its smallest triangles with its own rasterizer written as a compute shader.

4 Switching levels: popping, bias and fades

Changing from one mesh to another in a single frame causes LOD popping: a visible jump in outline or shading. It is easiest to see on large objects approached slowly, and on anything with a strong silhouette or sharp highlights.

Two controls fight it. A LOD bias moves every threshold at once. In Unity and Godot a value above 1 keeps detailed levels farther away, at higher cost, which makes it a typical per-platform quality setting. Textures have a LOD bias of their own, for mipmaps, covered in 6.2. A LOD cross-fade draws both levels for a short range or time and hands pixels from one to the other, usually through a dither pattern, because true transparency would need sorting and blending. TAA then smooths the dither over a few frames.

Try it. Drag the boulder from 24 m out past 28 m in Pop mode and watch LOD3 turn into a picture. Switch to the dithered fade and do it again. Then tick No LOD to see what full detail costs at a distance.

Thresholds also need a little slack. An object resting right at a switch point flickers between levels as the camera bobs. Godot's visibility ranges, for example, switch with hysteresis: the switch back happens at a slightly different distance from the switch out.

5 Impostors and billboards

Below some size even a few hundred triangles are too many. An impostor replaces the mesh with a picture of it on a flat card of two triangles. The picture is rendered ahead of time, often from many directions into one texture atlas, so the engine can show the view closest to the camera's direction. A billboard is a card that turns to face the camera every frame. Impostors usually are billboards, and so are many particles.

Impostors are cheap to draw, but they break when seen from an angle they were not captured from, or under lighting that differs from the capture. They suit things that are only ever seen from far away: trees on a hillside, distant rocks, crowds.

6 HLOD: one stand-in for a whole group

Per-object LOD still leaves one object per draw. A distant city block of twelve buildings, each at its lowest level, is still twelve meshes with their own materials and draw calls. HLOD, hierarchical level of detail, works one level up: an offline build merges the whole group into one proxy, a simplified merged mesh with one baked material, and the engine draws the proxy while the group is far away.

Open worlds depend on it. Unreal's World Partition streams the world in grid cells and uses HLOD proxies to show cells that are not loaded at all. Godot builds the same idea from visibility ranges: a merged mesh appears beyond a set distance and its parts hide. HLODs are built data, so when the source objects change the proxies must be rebuilt, and that belongs in the automated build.

7 Cluster LOD and Nanite

Per-object LOD has a blind spot: one object gets one level. A long cliff, a castle wall or a terrain tile can be close at one end and far at the other. Cluster LOD chooses detail per small piece instead. The mesh is cut into meshlets, also called clusters: chunks of up to about 128 triangles that the GPU culls and draws as units. NVIDIA's guidance for its mesh shaders recommends up to 64 vertices and 126 triangles per meshlet.

Nanite, Unreal Engine 5's virtualised geometry system, is the best-known example. Epic's 2021 SIGGRAPH presentation describes how it works:

  • At import, the mesh is split into clusters of 128 triangles. Neighbouring clusters are grouped, each group is simplified to half its triangles with its outer edges locked so neighbours still meet, and the result is split into clusters again. Repeating this builds a hierarchy from full detail down to a single cluster.
  • Every frame, on the GPU, Nanite culls clusters that cannot be seen, using the GPU culling covered in 4.4, and picks for each part of the mesh the coarsest clusters whose simplification error would be smaller than one pixel on screen. A change smaller than a pixel is not visible, and temporal anti-aliasing smooths what is left, so there is no cross-fade.
  • Only what a view needs is in memory. The GPU requests missing detail and the CPU streams it from disk in fixed-size pages. Epic recommends SSD storage for Nanite content.
  • Tiny triangles get their own rasterizer. Clusters whose triangles are smaller than about 32 pixels are drawn by a compute-shader rasterizer, which Epic measured as about three times faster on average than its fastest hardware path for this work.

Simplified

Nanite's hierarchy is a graph, not a tree: clusters are regrouped differently at each level so that locked edges do not stay locked, and a cluster can have several parents. The idea of picking a cut through it is the same.

Nobody authors LOD levels for a Nanite mesh, which removes most of the work in steps 2 and 4. The limits matter, though. In Unreal Engine 5.8, Nanite supports opaque and masked materials but not translucent ones, does not support morph targets, and does not run with forward rendering, stereo rendering for VR or MSAA. Ray tracing uses a simplified fallback mesh by default. Check the list for your version before planning content around it.

8 Tessellation, briefly

Tessellation works in the other direction: it adds triangles on the GPU at draw time. Direct3D 11 introduced three pipeline stages for it. A hull shader decides how finely to split each patch, a fixed tessellator splits it, up to a factor of 64 per edge, and a domain shader places every new vertex, often pushing it out along a height map. In principle that gives detail on demand: split more up close, less far away.

In practice hardware tessellation fell out of favour for most real-time work. It produces exactly the small triangles that rasterizers and quad shading handle worst, so its cost climbs quickly with the split factor. Unreal Engine 5 removed it, and its migration guide says Nanite covers most of the old uses; Unreal now offers displacement through Nanite Tessellation instead. On newer GPUs, mesh shaders can generate, cull and choose detail for geometry in groups, with a task stage that can make LOD decisions up front.

Every mesh band costs about the same 6,200 triangles, which is what a well-tuned chain looks like. The other line to watch is the impostor band: 1,462 objects are still 1,462 objects, and unless the engine draws them with instancing, as 4.3 covers, they cost a draw call each.

The people-time is in review. Automatic generation makes the first pass cheap, but every asset still needs its thresholds checked in game on each platform, and hero assets often need hand-made levels. Budget it as part of asset sign-off.

What makes the costs grow

  • Close-up detailLOD0 counts on anything the camera can reach, since those are drawn at full detail every frame.
  • LOD biasA bias of 2 keeps every level twice as far away. In a scattered field like the one above that is about four times the triangles.
  • Resolution, for pixel-based systemsSelection by pixel error, as in Godot's automatic LOD and Nanite, asks for more detail at 4K than at 1080p.
  • FadesBoth levels are drawn for as long as each fade lasts.
  • Extra passesEvery pass that draws the object, including each shadow map, pays for the level it draws.
  • Impostor and HLOD texturesBaked atlases and proxy textures add memory and build time.

5 How it breaks

LOD problems show up at distances and in motion, so judge them in game, moving, at the target resolution. Still screenshots in an editor viewport hide most of them.

Popping

Cause
A threshold sits where players look closely, or the next level differs too much in outline, normals or UVs from the one before.
Ask for
Thresholds tuned in game at the target resolution and field of view, a cross-fade for large objects, and levels reviewed in motion.

Flicker at a switch point

Cause
An object rests almost exactly at a threshold, and camera bob or animation crosses it back and forth. There is no hysteresis.
Ask for
A margin between the switch out and the switch back, or a fade, and a check of objects placed near common camera positions.

Thin parts vanish, holes open

Cause
Automatic simplification collapsed thin geometry such as posts, railings and leaves, or split the mesh open along UV seams.
Ask for
Hand-fixed levels or a higher triangle share for those meshes, and a review list of every auto-generated level.

Impostors look flat or wrong

Cause
The card is seen from an angle that was not captured, often from above, or the lighting baked into the capture does not match the scene.
Ask for
Captures that cover the angles players see, a normal atlas so cards can be relit, or a switch distance far enough that nobody looks closely.

Slow at a distance

Cause
Meshes with no lower levels, thresholds never set, a raised LOD bias or a forced LOD0, leaving far fewer than one pixel per triangle.
Ask for
A report of meshes without levels, triangle counts per mesh from a capture, and a target range for pixels per triangle at each switch.

Stale or missing HLOD

Cause
Proxies were not rebuilt after the level changed, or they leave out objects and lights that matter at a distance, such as lit windows at night.
Ask for
HLOD builds in the automated build, a check that flags proxies older than their sources, and a review of what each proxy includes.

To see which level each object uses, Unreal's viewport has Mesh LOD Coloration and Hierarchical LOD Coloration view modes. A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX shows the triangle count of every draw.

7 Say it to your AI

Good LOD briefs give the target resolution and field of view, the triangle counts you have, and a measurable goal such as pixels per triangle, and they ask how to verify the result in game.

BriefUse when setting up LODs for a batch of assets
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.8, without Nanite, because we also ship a mobile build. Targets: PC at 2560 × 1440 and mobile at 1280 × 720, both with a 70° vertical field of view.
We have 140 rock and cliff meshes from 4,000 to 60,000 triangles, with no LODs yet.

1. Propose a LOD chain per size class (small, medium, large): number of levels, triangle share per level, and Screen Size per level, with mobile overrides.
2. Aim for 10 to 50 pixels per triangle at every switch point. Show the calculation for one mesh of each class.
3. List the meshes that automatic reduction is likely to break (thin parts, hard edges, many separate pieces) and how we should review them.
4. Tell us how to check the result in game: which view modes, which statistics, and what we should see.
  • Resolution and field of view decide how big things look, so they decide the thresholds.
  • A pixels-per-triangle target turns "looks fine" into a number the AI can design for and you can check.
  • Asking which meshes will break gets you a review list instead of a blanket setting.
Bug reportUse when objects pop or flicker
Large cliff meshes visibly pop between LOD1 and LOD2 about 40 m from the player, and one cliff near the start area flickers between the two while the camera idles. Unity 6.6, URP, a LOD Group on each cliff, Fade Mode None, LOD Bias 1.

Find the cause and propose fixes in order of cost: threshold changes, Cross Fade with a Fade Transition Width, and changes to the LOD meshes themselves. For the flicker, explain why an idle camera crosses the threshold and how to stop it. Show before and after with the LOD Group's screen-size readout and the triangle counts from a capture.
  • The distance and the two levels involved locate the problem in the chain.
  • Fixes ordered by cost stop the AI from regenerating every mesh first.
  • The idle-camera flicker points at a missing margin, so asking why tests its understanding.

Questions to ask when reviewing LOD work

  1. What triggers each switch, a fraction of the screen or a pixel error, and at what resolution and field of view was it tuned?The same threshold behaves differently on another screen or with a zoomed camera.
  2. What are pixels per triangle at each switch point?One number shows whether levels are too dense or too coarse.
  3. Which levels were generated automatically, and who looked at them in game?Simplifiers break thin parts and seams without warning.
  4. How are switches hidden, and what does that cost?Fades draw two levels; transparency fades also need sorting.
  5. Are HLODs and impostors rebuilt automatically when their sources change?Stale proxies are easy to miss and look like bugs in the level.
  6. If we use Nanite, which of our materials and features does it not support?Translucency, morph targets, forward rendering and VR all need a plan.

8 Check yourself

  1. The 2 m boulder is 20 m from a camera with a 60° vertical field of view, on a 1080p screen. How tall is it on screen, and which level of this chapter's chain does it use?

    Show answer

    1080 × 2 ÷ (2 × 20 × tan 30°) ≈ 94 pixels, 8.7% of the screen height. That is between 6.25% and 12.5%, so LOD3.

  2. At that size the boulder covers about 7,400 pixels. What are its pixels per triangle at LOD3, and at LOD0?

    Show answer

    About 7,400 ÷ 216 ≈ 34 at LOD3. At LOD0 it is about 7,400 ÷ 13,824 ≈ 0.54: more triangles than pixels, so most triangles add nothing visible, and those that cover a pixel each pay for a whole 2 × 2 quad.

  3. A distant city costs 4,000 draw calls even though every building is at its lowest LOD. Which technique fixes that, and what does it need from the pipeline?

    Show answer

    HLOD: merge each block into one proxy mesh with a baked material, drawn while the block is far away. The proxies must be rebuilt whenever their sources change, ideally in the automated build.
