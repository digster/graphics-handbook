8.1 Direct and indirect light

Light does not stop at the first surface it hits. It bounces, again and again, losing some energy and picking up colour each time, and those bounces light most of what you see indoors. This chapter shows what bounced light is, why it is hard to compute, and how each technique in the rest of Part 8 approximates it.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

Direct light travels straight from a light to a surface: the sun on the floor, a lamp on a desk. Everything Part 7 computed, from shading to shadow maps, was direct light. Indirect light reaches a surface after bouncing off at least one other surface first. The underside of a table, the ceiling of a sunlit room and the side of a building facing away from the sun are lit almost entirely by it.

Lighting that includes those bounces is called global illumination, GI: global because the light at any point depends on every other surface in the scene, not just on the lights. That dependence is what makes it expensive. To light one point you need the light leaving every surface it can see, and that light in turn depends on every surface those can see.

Everyday comparison

Close the curtains on a sunny day, leaving one gap. A bright stripe lands on the floor, and yet you can see the whole room: the stripe acts like a dim lamp, lighting the ceiling and walls, which light everything else a little more. Paint the walls black and the room goes dark, though the stripe is just as bright.

The problem it solves

With direct light alone, anything the lights cannot see is pitch black. Games used to hide that with a flat ambient term, the same extra light everywhere, which makes interiors flat and shapes hard to read.

Global illumination puts the missing light back where it really goes, with its direction and its colour.

Why a lead cares

Bounced light is the biggest single step from a game look to a believable one, and choosing how to get it is one of the largest technical decisions on a project. Baking it costs build time and forbids moving lights; computing it every frame costs milliseconds and hardware; every method has its own artefacts, from leaking light to lag. Unreal Engine 5 projects start with Lumen switched on, Unity starts from baking, and Godot offers several options. The acronyms in Part 8 all name different trade-offs on the one problem this chapter describes.

3 How it works

Follow light from the sun through a room, bounce by bounce, then look at the vocabulary and the one equation every GI technique tries to solve.

1 Paths of light

Think of light as travelling along paths from a light to your eye. A direct path has one corner: the light hits a surface and goes to the eye. Indirect paths have more corners, one per bounce. A pixel's colour is the sum of the light arriving along every path that ends at it, and there are infinitely many.

2 A bounce: every lit surface becomes a light

A bounce is one reflection along a light path. When light hits a matte surface, the surface sends part of it back out in all directions (diffuse reflection, 7.2). How much it sends back is its albedo: a white wall about 80%, grey stone about half, black paint a few percent. So after one bounce, every lit surface is a weak, wide light for everything that can see it.

The room below is solved bounce by bounce. Start at 0, where only the sunbeam lights anything, then add bounces and watch the room fill.

  • Step to 2 and 4 and watch the dark corner fill in, and the red wall's colour spread onto the floor and ceiling beside it.

3 Why each bounce adds less

Every bounce loses light twice: the surface absorbs what its albedo does not reflect, and some of what it reflects escapes, here through the window. In this room each bounce after the first carries about half to two thirds of the one before: 64%, then 29%, 18%, 9% of the direct light. The sum of that shrinking series is finite, which is why many bounces settle on a fixed brightness: about 2.3 times the direct light here, after 27 bounces were added up.

The method the demo uses is radiosity: cut every surface into patches, work out once how much of each patch's light reaches each other patch, then pass light around until it settles. Cindy Goral and colleagues at Cornell brought it to computer graphics in 1984, adapting methods from heat transfer, and showed the effect a coloured wall has on its neighbours. Radiosity only handles matte surfaces, but its ideas live on in baking tools and in Unity's Enlighten, which precomputes which patches can see each other and then updates their light at run time over several frames.

Simplified

The demo is a 2D slice, so light only travels in the plane of the picture, and every surface is perfectly matte. A real room leaks light in and out in three dimensions and has shiny surfaces too. The shape of the result, a big first bounce and quickly shrinking later ones, is the same.

4 Colour bleeding

A surface reflects each colour channel by its own albedo. A red wall reflects most red light and little green or blue, so the light leaving it is red, and anything it lights picks up a red tint. That is colour bleeding: one surface's colour spreading onto its neighbours through bounced light. It is why a white shirt looks green on a lawn and why a room's walls set the mood of everything in it.

Colour bleeding is a test of a GI system and a check on the art. Too little and a red-walled room looks pasted together; too much, from albedos brighter than real materials (6.5's plausible albedo range) or from multipliers turned above 1, and whole rooms turn the colour of one wall.

5 Radiance and irradiance, in plain words

Two words appear in every GI paper, engine document and AI answer. Radiance is the light travelling along one direction: what a single camera pixel or your eye measures when it looks at a point. Its everyday counterpart is luminance, measured in nits (3.2). Irradiance is all the light arriving at a point on a surface from every direction above it, each direction weighted by how squarely it strikes: the counterpart of illuminance, measured in lux (7.1). Irradiance decides how bright a matte surface is; radiance is what reaches the camera.

The difference matters when you read about GI techniques. Lightmaps and most light probes store irradiance, or something close to it, which is enough for matte surfaces; reflections (7.6) need radiance from particular directions, which costs far more to store.

6 The rendering equation, in pictures

In 1986 James Kajiya presented an equation that, as his abstract puts it, generalises a variety of known rendering algorithms. The rendering equation says that the light leaving a point towards your eye is the light the point emits, plus the sum over every direction above it of the light arriving from that direction, times how much the material sends it towards your eye (its BRDF, 6.5), times how squarely it arrives.

Maths The rendering equation as most texts write it

Lo(x, ωo) = Le(x, ωo) + ∫Ω fr(x, ωi, ωo) · Li(x, ωi) · (n · ωi) dωi

Lo is the radiance leaving point x in direction ωo, towards the eye. Le is what x emits. The integral adds up, over the half-sphere Ω of incoming directions ωi, the incoming radiance Li times the BRDF fr times the cosine n · ωi. The catch is that Li(x, ωi) is the Lo of whatever surface lies in direction ωi, so the unknown appears on both sides. Kajiya's 1986 paper wrote it in a different but equivalent form, as transport between pairs of surface points; this direction form is the one most textbooks and engine documents use today. The ambient occlusion integral in 8.3 is this equation with the incoming light reduced to a yes-or-no test for open sky in each direction.

7 Diffuse GI and specular GI

Bounced light reaches the eye in two ways, matching the two halves of a material (7.2). Diffuse GI is bounced light that a matte surface scatters evenly: it does not change as the camera moves, and it varies smoothly across a room, which is why it can be baked into textures or stored in a grid of probes (8.2). Specular GI is bounced light seen in reflections: a shiny floor showing the red wall. It changes with every move of the camera and has sharp detail, which is why it needs the reflection techniques of 7.6.

8 What a flat ambient term misses

Before GI was affordable, games added an ambient term: one constant amount of light everywhere, standing in for all bounces. A sky light (7.1) improves on it by giving the light a direction, more from above than below, but neither knows anything about the room. 4 What it costs

The rendering equation asks, for every pixel, for the light from every direction, each of which depends on every other surface. Brute force is hopeless in real time: a modest 64 rays per pixel for one bounce at 1080p is 2,073,600 × 64 ≈ 133 million rays a frame, before shading anything they hit or tracing a second bounce. Every practical technique pays somewhere else instead.

Bounces themselves have a price. In a bake, Unreal's Lightmass documentation says the first bounce takes the most time and contributes the most to quality, followed by the second, and later bounces add little time but little visible effect unless materials are very bright. Unity's lightmapper uses 2 bounces by default and says values up to 10 suit most scenes, with higher values for interiors. The bounce room shows why: the first two bounces add 93% of the direct light between them; all the rest together add another 39%.

What makes the bill grow

  • BouncesEach one is another pass over the scene's light; later ones matter more in bright, closed interiors.
  • Things that changeMoving lights, opening doors and time of day force real-time GI or several bakes.
  • Scene sizeBakes and GI data grow with the area covered; real-time methods need representations of everything in range.
  • Directions per pointRays or probe resolution per pixel decide noise and detail.
  • ResolutionPer-pixel methods scale with screen pixels, which is why many run at reduced internal resolution.
  • Specular GISharp reflections of bounced light need far more data than soft diffuse light.

In proportion

Epic's Lumen budget of 4 ms at 60 fps is a quarter of a 16.6 ms frame for global illumination, reflections and fog together. Baked GI costs almost nothing per frame but moves the bill into build times, memory and the rule that lights and walls stay where they were baked. Most projects decide this per level, not per game.

5 How it breaks

GI bugs are about light being in the wrong amount, in the wrong place or at the wrong time.

Flat, lifeless interiors

Cause
Indirect light comes only from a flat ambient term or the sky, so every surface away from the lights gets the same amount, with no direction or colour.
Ask for
A GI method for interiors, baked or real time, plus ambient occlusion, and before-and-after shots of the darkest rooms.

Shadows and interiors that are pitch black

Cause
No indirect light at all: GI disabled for the level or platform, a bake with zero bounces, or a light's indirect contribution set to 0.
Ask for
The GI method per platform, the bounce count and each light's indirect intensity, and the fallback when GI is turned off on low settings.

Light leaking through walls

Cause
The GI system's simplified scene, probes or voxels, is coarser than the wall, so light from outside is counted inside. Epic advises walls no thinner than 10 cm for Lumen. Chapter 8.5 covers light leaking in detail.
Ask for
Wall thickness checks, probe placement that keeps samples on the right side of walls, and the method's leak-reduction settings.

A whole room tinted by one wall

Cause
Colour bleeding made too strong: albedo brighter or more saturated than real materials, or bounce multipliers above 1 (Unity's Albedo Boost is physically accurate at 1, and Indirect Intensity defaults to 1).
Ask for
An albedo check against the plausible range, multipliers back at 1, and a comparison bake with neutral walls.

Bounced light lags behind changes

Cause
Real-time GI spreads its updates over several frames, or accumulates over time; Unity's documentation says Enlighten takes several frames to propagate changes.
Ask for
How many frames a full update takes, how fast changes such as doors and switched lights are handled, and a recording at the target frame rate.

To diagnose, separate direct from indirect light. The courtyard's Direct light view shows the frame with no sky light and no bounces; most engines have a similar lighting-only or GI-only view, and switching the GI method off for a moment shows exactly what it contributes.

Multipliers are not fixes

Every engine lets you scale bounced light up. Turning those values above 1 brightens a dark room, but it breaks the energy balance materials were authored for and over-saturates colour bleeding. Check albedo and light intensities first, and use a GI method that suits the space.

7 Say it to your AI

GI briefs need what changes in the game (lights, time of day, geometry), the platforms and budget, the kinds of spaces, and whether bake times matter to the team. Adapt these.

Questions to ask when reviewing GI work

  1. What in this level changes at run time, and which GI method handles each change?Baked light cannot react to moving lights, opening doors or time of day.
  2. Have any GI multipliers been set above 1?They hide problems and break colour and energy balance.
  3. How many bounces does the bake or method use, and where does it matter?Closed bright interiors need more than open exteriors.
  4. Where could light leak, and how thick are the walls?Thin walls and coarse GI representations leak light.
  5. How long does a change take to show in the indirect light?Multi-frame updates show as lag after sudden changes.
  6. What does the game look like with GI off on the lowest settings?The fallback decides how low-end players see every interior.

8 Check yourself

  1. Sunlight through a window lights only a patch of the floor, yet the ceiling above is clearly lit. Where does the ceiling's light come from, and how many bounces is that?

    Show answer

    From the sunlit floor: light hits the floor, which reflects part of it upwards. That is indirect light after one bounce. Light that then bounces off the ceiling onto the walls has bounced twice.

  2. In the bounce room the first bounce adds 64% of the direct light and the second 29%. About how much will the third add, and why does the total stop growing?

    Show answer

    Roughly half to two thirds of the second: the demo computes 18%. Each bounce loses what surfaces absorb and what escapes through the window, so every bounce is a fraction of the one before, and the sum of such a shrinking series is finite, about 2.3 times the direct light here.

  3. A white statue beside a red curtain has a pink tinge on one side after a bake. Is this a bug?

    Show answer

    Not by itself: it is colour bleeding, light reflected off the curtain carrying its colour. It becomes a bug when it is far stronger than in real life, usually from albedos that are too bright or saturated, or from bounce multipliers set above 1.
