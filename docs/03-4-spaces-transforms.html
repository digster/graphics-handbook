3.4 Spaces and transforms

Every corner of every model starts as numbers measured from the model's own origin and ends as a pixel position on screen. In between it passes through a short, fixed chain of coordinate spaces, each reached by one matrix multiply. Knowing that chain lets you read camera settings, untangle import problems and spot a whole family of shader bugs.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A position is only a list of numbers, and numbers mean nothing until you know what they are measured from. A coordinate space is that reference: an origin, a set of axes and a unit. "Three metres to the left" depends entirely on who is standing where and facing which way.

A renderer uses a handful of spaces because each one makes a different job easy. Artists build a model around its own origin. Level designers place it in a shared world. The camera needs everything measured from itself. The GPU needs a fixed box it can clip against, and the screen needs pixels. A transform, a move, turn and resize, converts positions from one space to the next.

Everyday comparison

"The stapler is 20 cm left of my keyboard" is object space. "My desk is in room 4.12, 3 m from the window" is world space. "From where you are standing, it is ahead and slightly to your left" is view space. A photo of it is screen space.

All four describe the same stapler. Each is easiest for a different person.

The problem it solves

One crate model may appear 500 times in a level, the camera moves every frame, and the screen might be 1280 or 3840 pixels wide.

Keeping each concern in its own space means the crate's vertices never change, moving the camera touches one matrix, and changing resolution touches one more.

Why a lead cares

A large share of the graphics bugs you will triage are space bugs: models imported lying on their backs or 100 times too large, lighting that turns with an object, effects that drift when the camera moves, distant surfaces that flicker. Each has a short, specific cause in this chapter. Camera feel, too, from field of view to how close the player can get to a wall, is set by numbers explained here.

3 How it works

Ten steps: what a space is, how matrices carry transforms, the four links in the chain, then two practical matters, depth precision and the axis conventions that differ between tools.

1 One point, many sets of numbers

Take one corner of a crate. Measured from the crate's own centre, along the crate's own axes, it is at (1, 1). That is object space, also called model or local space, and it is what a vertex buffer stores. Measured from the level's origin, along the level's axes, the same corner is at (5.37, 3.37). That is world space: the one space every object, light and camera in the level shares.

2 Transforms live in matrices

Moving from one space to another is a transform: some mix of moving (translation), turning (rotation) and resizing (scale). Graphics code stores each transform as a matrix, a 4 × 4 grid of 16 numbers. Multiplying a position by the matrix applies the transform. Multiplying two matrices together gives one matrix that does both, which is how a long chain of transforms collapses into a single multiply per vertex.

Order matters. Turning a crate and then moving it is not the same as moving it and then turning it, because a turn always happens around the origin. Engines fix one order for you, scale then rotate then translate, so the object turns around its own origin before it is placed.

Maths What the 16 numbers mean, and why there is a fourth coordinate

A 3D position is written with a fourth number, w, set to 1: (x, y, z, 1). A direction gets w = 0. In the matrix, the first three columns say where the x, y and z axes end up, which handles rotation and scale; the fourth column holds the translation.

[ Xx Yx Zx Tx ] [ x ]
[ Xy Yy Zy Ty ] × [ y ]
[ Xz Yz Zz Tz ] [ z ]
[ 0 0 0 1 ] [ w ]

Because the translation is multiplied by w, a point (w = 1) moves and a direction (w = 0) only turns. That is the matrix form of the difference between points and vectors from 3.3. Some engines and APIs write vectors as rows instead of columns, which flips the multiplication order on paper; the result is the same.

3 Object to world: the model matrix

Every object in a scene has a transform: a position, a rotation and a scale, the three fields you see in any engine's inspector. Together they make its model matrix, which takes the mesh's vertices from object space into world space. Five hundred crates share one mesh; only their model matrices differ. When an object is the child of another, its matrix is multiplied by its parent's, so moving a ship moves its crew.

4 World to view: the view matrix

The camera is an object in the world too, with its own position and rotation. Rendering is simpler if everything is measured from the camera, so the next step moves the entire world until the camera sits at the origin, looking straight along one axis. That is view space, and the matrix that gets there is the view matrix. It is the exact opposite, the inverse, of the camera's own transform.

Nothing moves relative to anything else in this step. Distances and angles between objects stay the same; only the point of view changes. Which axis the camera looks along is a convention: Godot and OpenGL look along −Z, and Unity's documentation notes that its camera space follows the same rule, even though "forward" in a Unity scene is +Z.

5 What the camera can see: the view frustum

A camera sees a pyramid-shaped region with its tip cut off: the view frustum. Four numbers define it. The field of view is how wide it opens, as an angle. The aspect ratio, such as 16:9, sets how much wider it is than tall. The near plane is where drawing starts; anything closer to the camera is cut away. The far plane is where drawing stops.

Field of view needs care in briefs: some engines measure it across the height of the screen and others across the width. A vertical 60° on a 16:9 screen is about 91.5° horizontally. Mixing the two up is a common reason a camera "feels wrong" after a port.

Maths Converting between vertical and horizontal field of view

tan(horizontal FOV ÷ 2) = tan(vertical FOV ÷ 2) × aspect ratio

For a vertical 60° at 16:9: tan 30° × 16 ÷ 9 = 0.577 × 1.778 = 1.026, and 2 × arctan 1.026 ≈ 91.5°. Godot's documentation gives the same kind of table for its default vertical 75°, which is about 107.5° horizontally at 16:9.

6 View to clip: the projection matrix

The projection matrix holds the frustum's numbers and takes positions from view space into clip space. Its output has four numbers per vertex, x, y, z and w, and it is what a vertex shader must hand to the GPU. The GPU uses them to cut triangles at the frustum's edges, which is where the name comes from.

The projection decides what kind of picture you get. A perspective projection makes lines of sight meet at the camera, so distant things look smaller, the way eyes and lenses work. An orthographic projection keeps lines of sight parallel, so size never changes with distance. Its frustum is a plain box. Orthographic views suit 2D games, UI, isometric strategy games, level-editor views and the shadow maps of a sun (7.4 Shadow maps).

7 Clip to screen: the divide and the viewport

After clipping, the GPU performs the perspective divide: it divides x, y and z by w. A perspective projection sets w to the vertex's distance in front of the camera, so the divide is literally "divide by distance", and that is what makes far things small. The result is normalised device coordinates (NDC): everything visible now lies between −1 and 1 across and up. Depth lies between 0 and 1 in Direct3D, Vulkan and Metal, and between −1 and 1 in classic OpenGL.

Last, the viewport transform stretches that square over the actual screen, giving screen space: pixel positions, usually counted from the top-left corner with y going down. Techniques described as screen-space work from this rendered image and its depth, which is fast but blind to anything the camera does not currently see.

8 The whole chain in one multiply

A vertex shader does not apply the model, view and projection matrices one at a time. The engine multiplies them together once per object, projection × view × model, into a single MVP matrix. The shader then takes each vertex from object space to clip space with one 4 × 4 multiply, and the fixed hardware does the divide and the viewport. Shaders often keep the world-space and view-space positions along the way too, because lighting, fog and other effects need them.

Walk the chain below. Each click applies one transform. The table follows one corner of the house through every space, and the slider and projection switch change what the last two steps do.

Code The chain in a vertex shader

HLSL-style pseudocode. Engines generate this for you; you meet it in custom shaders and in AI-written ones.

float4 worldPos = mul(Model, float4(positionOS, 1));   // object -> world; w = 1: a point
float4 viewPos  = mul(View, worldPos);                  // world -> view
float4 clipPos  = mul(Projection, viewPos);             // view -> clip; the GPU divides by w later
float3 normalWS = normalize(mul((float3x3)NormalMatrix, normalOS)); // directions: no translation
return clipPos;                                          // equivalently mul(MVP, float4(positionOS, 1))

The suffixes OS and WS (object space, world space) are a common naming habit worth asking for. The normal uses a separate matrix, the inverse transpose of the model matrix, so that uneven scaling does not bend it.

9 Why the near plane matters more than the far plane

The z value after the divide is what goes into the depth buffer, and it is not spread evenly. Because the divide is by distance, depth changes quickly close to the camera and slowly far away. The near plane controls how lopsided this is. Pull it in from 1 m to 0.1 m and the first metre in front of the camera uses up about 90% of all depth values, leaving very little to tell apart two surfaces 50 m away. Pushing the far plane out barely matters by comparison.

Maths The depth curve

For a standard perspective projection with depth from 0 to 1, near plane n and far plane f, a surface at distance d gets

depth = f ÷ (f − n) × (1 − n ÷ d)

With n = 0.1 and f = 100, a surface 1 m away already has depth 0.90 and one 10 m away has 0.991. With n = 1, they get 0 and 0.909. Changing f from 100 to 1000 moves the first factor from 1.001 to 1.0001, which is why the far plane hardly matters.

10 Handedness, up axis and units

Every tool picks its own axes. The up axis is the one it treats as vertical: Y in Unity, Godot and glTF files; Z in Unreal Engine, Blender and 3ds Max. Handedness says which way the third axis points once two are fixed. Point the thumb of one hand along x and the index finger along y: the middle finger is z. Whether your left or your right hand matches the tool's axes is its handedness. Units differ too: Unreal measures in centimetres, Unity and Godot in metres.

Moving a model between two conventions needs some combination of three fixes: a turn to change the up axis, a mirror to change handedness, and a scale to change units. Importers apply these automatically when their settings are right. A mirror also reverses the order of every triangle's corners, its winding order, so a correct importer reverses the winding too; one that forgets shows you the inside of every model.

Simplified

Real importers also decide which way a model's front faces: Unreal treats +X as forward, while glTF and Godot expect a model's front to face +Z. That can add a further turn about the up axis. FBX files can also declare their own axes and units, which importers read and convert.

4 What it costs

The arithmetic is cheap and the GPU does most of it. Transforming one vertex by a 4 × 4 matrix is 16 multiply-adds. A scene with a million visible vertices, drawn in a depth pass, three shadow cascades and a main pass, transforms 5 million vertices per frame: 80 million multiply-adds, or 4.8 billion per second at 60 fps. That is under 0.1% of the 10.3 trillion operations per second the PlayStation 5's GPU is rated for.

The real costs sit elsewhere: on the CPU, which builds a matrix for every moving object every frame and walks the parent-child hierarchy to do it; in the data sent to the GPU; and in people-time when conventions go wrong.

What makes the cost grow

  • Moving objectsStatic objects keep their matrices; each moving one needs a new matrix and an upload every frame.
  • Deep hierarchiesEach parent-child link is another 4 × 4 multiply on the CPU, every frame the parent moves.
  • More passesEvery shadow cascade or extra view runs every vertex through a different view and projection.
  • Large worldsPrecision falls with distance from the origin. Fixes such as 64-bit positions cost memory and engineering.

The expensive part is people

A wrong up axis, unit or handedness on import is cheap to fix on day one and expensive after 2,000 assets have been made, placed and hand-corrected. Decide the pipeline's conventions, and the export settings that produce them, before production starts, and test them with one asset end to end.

5 How it breaks

Space and transform bugs have distinctive looks. These six account for most of what you will see.

Lying on its back

Cause
The up axis was not converted between a Z-up source and a Y-up engine, or it was converted twice: once at export and again at import.
Ask for
One agreed place for the conversion, the exporter or the importer, written down, and a test asset with a clear top and front.

A hundred times too small or too big

Cause
A unit mismatch: centimetres read as metres or the reverse. Physics then misbehaves too, because gravity and mass assume a scale.
Ask for
The importer's unit setting (Unity's Convert Units, Unreal's Convert Scene Unit) and a reference object of known size in every test level.

Mirrored, or inside out

Cause
A handedness change mirrored the model without reversing its winding, or a negative scale on an object flipped it. Text reads backwards; faces vanish.
Ask for
A check of the importer's axis settings, a search for negative scales in the scene, and a test asset with lettering on it.

Lighting turns with the object

Cause
A shader compared a normal in object space with a light direction in world space. It looks right until the object rotates.
Ask for
The space of every vector in the lighting code, named in the variable (normalWS, lightDirWS), and a test that spins the object.

Distant surfaces flicker

Cause
Z-fighting: two surfaces closer together than the depth buffer can tell apart at that distance, usually because the near plane is very small.
Ask for
The near and far planes in use and the largest near plane the camera can live with; then whether the engine uses reversed depth (5.5).

Shaking far from the origin

Cause
32-bit floats lose precision as numbers grow. About 10 km from the origin, positions can only land on a grid about 1 mm apart, and animation, physics and cameras start to jitter.
Ask for
The world's size against the engine's limits, and a plan: keeping play near the origin, moving the origin with the player, or 64-bit positions.

A camera that clips into walls is the near plane's other failure: set it too large and the camera cuts away geometry the player should see up close. Near-plane values are a negotiation between that and flickering in the distance.

All three engines run the same chain. What differs is the axes, the units, how field of view is measured and what the importer converts for you.

Porting caveat

A field of view of 90 in Unreal and 90 in Unity are different cameras: one is measured across the width, the other by default across the height. When moving a camera between engines, convert the angle, and check which axis each engine's "forward" is before copying any rotation values.

7 Say it to your AI

Good briefs about spaces name the engine, its conventions and the source tool, state the numbers in use, and ask the AI to say which space every value is in. Adapt these.

Bug reportUse when imported models arrive rotated or at the wrong size
We model in Blender 4.5 and import FBX into Unreal Engine 5. Props arrive lying on their backs and about 100 times too small. Characters look correct.

1. List the axis, handedness and unit conventions at each end, and every place in our pipeline that converts them: Blender export settings, FBX import options, any scripts.
2. Find where props and characters take different paths, and why.
3. Propose one place to do each conversion, the exact settings, and a test asset with a known size, top and front that we can import after every pipeline change.
  • The two symptoms together point at an up-axis and a unit conversion, not a single bug.
  • "Characters look correct" tells the AI to look for two export paths.
  • A fixed test asset turns the fix into a check that stays.
BriefUse when distant surfaces flicker or the camera clips into walls
Unity 6, URP, PC and consoles. Our third-person camera uses a near plane of 0.01 m and a far plane of 20,000 m. Distant terrain and buildings flicker where surfaces overlap, and we have had complaints about the camera seeing through walls when backed into corners.

1. Explain how near and far affect depth precision for our setup, with numbers.
2. Recommend near and far values and anything else in the camera rig, such as collision or a minimum distance, so that we never need a near plane below that value.
3. Tell us whether our pipeline uses reversed depth and what that changes.
4. Give us a test scene with surfaces 10 cm apart at 50 m, 500 m and 5 km to verify the result.
  • Stating both numbers lets the AI reason from the actual ratio.
  • Asking for the camera rig keeps it from fixing one symptom by causing the other.
  • The test scene makes the improvement measurable.

Questions to ask when reviewing transform code

  1. Which space is each position and direction in?Mixing spaces is the most common transform bug. Names such as positionWS make it visible.
  2. Is this a point, with w = 1, or a direction, with w = 0?A direction that gets translated is wrong as soon as the object moves.
  3. In which order are the matrices multiplied, and does the engine use row or column vectors?The wrong order swings objects around the origin.
  4. What are the near and far planes, and why those values?A tiny near plane is the usual cause of distant flicker.
  5. Where are axes and units converted on import, and is it done exactly once?Converting twice is as broken as never converting.
  6. Does anything rely on negative scale?Negative scale mirrors geometry and flips its winding.

8 Check yourself

  1. A crate sits at world position (10, 0, 5) with no rotation and a scale of 1. One of its corners is at (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) in object space. Where is that corner in world space?

    Show answer

    (10.5, 0.5, 5.5). With no rotation or scale, the model matrix only adds the crate's position.

  2. Distant buildings flicker. The camera uses near 0.01 m and far 5,000 m. Which single change will help most: far to 1,000 m, or near to 0.1 m?

    Show answer

    Near to 0.1 m. Depth precision is set mostly by the near plane; a ten times larger near plane leaves roughly ten times more depth values for distant surfaces, while shortening the far plane barely changes them.

  3. Props made in Blender appear lying on their backs in a Y-up engine, but at the right size. What is the likely cause, and what is not the cause?

    Show answer

    The up axis was not converted from Blender's Z-up, or was converted twice. Units are not the cause, since the size is right. Check the export and import axis settings and make sure exactly one of them converts.
