9.5 Atmosphere

Air is never empty. Fog, haze, beams of sunlight, the blue of the sky and the clouds are all light bouncing off particles between the camera and the scene. This chapter shows how renderers fake that cheaply with height fog, simulate it in a grid of froxels, draw the sky from a model of the atmosphere and march rays through clouds, and what each of those costs.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

On a clear day you can see for kilometres, yet distant hills still look paler and bluer than near ones. In a misty forest, beams of sunlight stand out between the trees. Headlights in fog light up the fog itself, not only the road. All three happen because air is full of tiny particles that light hits on its way through.

Anything that light travels through and that scatters or absorbs some of it on the way is called participating media: clean air, haze, fog, smoke, cloud. Light crossing it changes in two ways. Some is lost, absorbed or bounced off in another direction; that loss is extinction. And some light from elsewhere, from the sun, the sky or a lamp, is bounced into the path towards your eye; that gain is in-scattering. Fog is both at once, along every view ray: the scene behind dims and the air itself glows.

An everyday comparison

A sunbeam through a dusty window. The dust shows you where the light goes. Sweep it away and the beam disappears, though the same sunlight still crosses the room.

Renderers add the dust back on purpose, because the beam is what tells you the light is there.

The problem it solves

Without atmosphere a game world looks like a model on a table: everything equally sharp and saturated however far away it is. Atmosphere gives distance, sets the mood and the time of day, and hides the place where the world, or the draw distance, ends.

Why a lead cares

Atmosphere is one of the cheapest ways to make a game look expensive, and one of the easiest places to lose a millisecond without noticing. Height fog costs almost nothing. Volumetric fog costs about a millisecond on a console, volumetric clouds about two. Fog also hides draw distance and level-of-detail switches, so changing it can expose problems in streaming and LOD that nobody saw before.

3 How it works

The techniques run from a single formula per pixel to full simulations in a 3D grid. Each step up handles one more thing the cheaper method gets wrong.

1 Distance fog: one formula per pixel

The oldest fog in games is distance fog. For each pixel the renderer knows how far the surface is, from its own shader or from the depth buffer. It turns that distance into a visibility between 0 and 1 and blends the pixel towards a fog colour by the rest. Older games used thick fog to hide how short their draw distance was.

For fog of even thickness the physical curve is exponential: every metre removes the same share of the light that is left. That is the Beer–Lambert law, and it is why the second mode below is called exponential. The other two curves are artistic choices.

2 Height fog: thick at the ground, thin above

Real mist pools in valleys and thins with height. Height fog models that with a density that falls exponentially as you go up: a density at the ground, and a falloff that sets how quickly it thins. Integrating that density along a straight view ray has a closed-form answer, so the renderer never steps along the ray. One short formula per pixel gives the share of light that survives, whatever direction the pixel looks.

The fog colour matters as much as its density. A flat grey makes distant hills look painted over. Better height fog takes its colour from the sky in the direction of each pixel, so the ground melts into the horizon, and adds a warm glow towards the sun. Unreal's Exponential Height Fog calls that glow Directional Inscattering, and HDRP's Fog override has a Color Mode of Sky Color. The courtyard below uses the horizon sky as its fog colour and adds the lantern's glow in the haze, also with a closed-form formula.

3 Which way light scatters: the phase function

A particle does not scatter light equally in every direction. The phase function describes how much goes each way, relative to where the light came from. Air molecules send light forwards and backwards about equally. Dust, pollution and water droplets send most of it on forwards, which is why haze glows brightest around the sun and looks dull with the sun behind you.

4 Volumetric fog: the air in a grid

Height fog cannot vary from place to place, and it does not know about shadows: the air under a bridge glows as brightly as the air in the sun. Volumetric fog fixes both by storing the air in a 3D grid that fills the view. Each cell holds a density, which can come from height fog, noise, fog volumes placed by artists or particles, and the light that reaches it from every light, with that light's shadows.

The grid is shaped like the view frustum. Its width and height follow tiles of the screen and its depth is cut into slices. Each cell is a froxel, a frustum voxel. Bart Wronski's 2014 system for Assassin's Creed IV tried a world-aligned box first; aligning the grid to the frustum made the march along each view ray a simple walk down a column of cells. The slices are spaced exponentially, thin near the camera where mistakes show and deep far away.

Such a coarse grid would show its cells, so every engine hides them the same way: each frame the sample positions are moved slightly inside every cell, and the result is blended with the previous frames, reprojected to where they now are. Wronski notes that reprojection is easier in 3D than on screen, because the cells behind a moving object are still valid. The price is lag. A light that moves or flickers fast leaves a smear in the fog, which Epic's documentation warns about.

Simplified

Production systems add more on top: separate scattering and extinction values per cell, sky and ambient light injected from spherical harmonics or probes, local fog volumes, and culling of cells hidden behind geometry. The three passes in the picture, inject, accumulate and apply, are the core of all of them.

5 Light shafts

Light shafts, also called god rays or crepuscular rays, are beams of light through hazy air with darker air between them. In volumetric fog they come for free once lights cast shadows into the grid: where a row of sunlit cells lines up between dark ones, you see a beam. Unreal turns this on per light with Cast Volumetric Shadow, and its documentation puts shadowed point and spot lights at about three times the cost of unshadowed ones in the fog.

Before volumetric fog, many games drew shafts as a post effect: take the bright pixels near the sun on screen and smear them outwards from the sun's position. It is cheap and can look striking, and Wronski's talk lists its weakness. The shafts exist only while the light source is on screen.

6 The sky: Rayleigh and Mie scattering

The sky is the same physics at the scale of a planet. Air molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of light. Scattering by such small particles, Rayleigh scattering, grows steeply as the wavelength shrinks, so blue light is scattered far more than red. Larger particles, dust, pollution and droplets, cause Mie scattering, which treats all colours about alike and points strongly forwards.

7 A sky model and aerial perspective

A sky atmosphere model simulates that scattering for a whole planet: ground radius, atmosphere height, Rayleigh, Mie and ozone amounts and how they thin with altitude. From those it computes the sky's colour for any sun angle, including sunsets and twilight, so a day–night cycle needs no hand-painted skies.

The same model also gives aerial perspective: the haze that makes distant objects paler and bluer. Applying it to the scene, not only the sky, is what makes terrain melt into the horizon instead of ending in a line.

Not every engine uses this model. HDRP's Physically Based Sky is a full atmosphere with its own precomputed tables. Godot's PhysicalSkyMaterial uses the Preetham analytic daylight model, which is cheaper and simpler and supports one sun; its height fog can blend towards the sky colour with Aerial Perspective.

8 Volumetric clouds

Clouds used to be painted onto the sky dome. Volumetric clouds are instead a 3D density field between two altitudes, rendered by ray marching: stepping along each view ray through the layer, reading the density at each step and adding up light and opacity. Guerrilla's clouds for Horizon Zero Dawn, presented by Andrew Schneider in 2015, set the pattern most engines follow.

  • Shape comes from small tiling noise textures: a 128³ texture of Perlin–Worley and Worley noise for the base shapes, a 32³ one for detail at the edges, and a 128² curl-noise texture to swirl them. A weather map painted or simulated over the world says where clouds are and what type.
  • Light at each step comes from a few samples of density towards the sun: Beer's law for how much sunlight gets through, a phase function for the bright silver lining, and a darkening on edges facing the light that Schneider called the powdered-sugar look.
  • Speed comes from doing as little as possible: long, cheap steps through empty air, short full-detail steps only inside cloud, and stopping as soon as the cloud is opaque.

9 Where atmosphere sits in the frame

Atmosphere runs after the opaque scene is lit and before transparent objects. HDRP's documentation spells the order out: opaque surfaces, then the sky, then fog, then transparents. The froxel volume itself can be built much earlier, as soon as the shadow maps exist, because it does not read the scene's colour; Wronski suggests overlapping it with the G-buffer pass on the GPU's compute queue.

Transparent objects, glass, water and particles, are the awkward part. A fog pass that works from the depth buffer only fogs what is in the depth buffer, and transparent surfaces usually are not. They need fog applied in their own shaders: by reading the froxel volume at their depth, which is one reason engines build it, or per vertex, as Hillaire's paper does for aerial perspective. 10.1 A deferred frame, pass by pass shows the courtyard's fog pass in place between reflections and bloom.

4 What it costs

Height fog and a sky model cost a few tenths of a millisecond. Volumetric fog and volumetric clouds are each in the range of one to three milliseconds, which at 60 fps is a large slice of a frame budget of 16.7 ms.

Worked memory numbers. A froxel grid of 160 × 90 × 64 cells is 921,600 cells. If each cell holds four 16-bit values, 8 bytes, one volume takes 7.4 MB; at 128 slices, 14.7 MB. Systems keep two or three such volumes, for the injected light, the accumulated result and last frame's history, so the fog's memory is tens of megabytes at most and does not grow with screen resolution.

  • Cells in the gridTile size and slice count set the cell count, and every cell is lit every frame. Unreal's r.VolumetricFog.GridSizeZ sets the slices; Epic's own figures show a GTX 970 at 3 ms with eight times the cells of the 1 ms PlayStation 4 setting.
  • Shadowed lights in the fogEach light that reaches the fog adds work to every cell it touches, and a shadow lookup costs more again: about three times in Unreal for point and spot lights.
  • Fog rangeThe same slices stretched over a longer distance get coarser. Covering a long view well means more slices, or a cheaper fog beyond the volume.
  • Steps per rayFor clouds, every step and every light sample is paid per traced pixel. HDRP's documentation notes that primary steps add cost linearly and light steps more steeply.
  • Screen resolutionOnly the passes that touch every pixel, applying fog and compositing clouds, grow with resolution. The grid and the sky tables do not.

The usual trade

Pair a cheap height fog that covers the whole view with volumetric fog over a limited range near the camera, where shafts and lit haze are noticed; Godot's documentation recommends using the two together. Quality tiers then change the grid size and whether shadowed local lights reach the fog. Volumetric clouds are the most expensive layer, and Unreal's documentation lists them as unsupported on mobile.

5 How it breaks

Atmosphere bugs come from three places: the coarseness of the grid, the history that hides it, and the parts of the frame that never pass through the fog.

Fog in visible bands or blocks

Cause
Too few slices or cells for the fog's range, a fog render target with too little precision, or temporal jitter switched off so the grid shows. Dark scenes show it most, as colour banding.
Ask for
More slices near the camera or a shorter volumetric range, jitter and temporal filtering on, and dithering when the result is written out.

Glow that trails behind moving lights

Cause
The fog's temporal history keeps last frames' light, so a torch or a muzzle flash leaves a smear, a fog version of ghosting.
Ask for
A lower history weight, keeping fast flickering lights out of the volumetric fog, or a separate cheap glow for them.

Glass and particles that ignore the fog

Cause
Fog applied as a full-screen pass from the depth buffer, which transparent surfaces do not write, so they stay crisp and bright inside thick fog.
Ask for
Fog applied inside the transparent shaders, from the froxel volume or per vertex, and a check of every transparent material in the densest fog.

A grey band where land meets sky

Cause
A constant fog colour that does not match the sky, a fog cut-off distance, or the sky excluded from fog, so distant terrain ends in a line.
Ask for
Fog coloured from the sky (sky-colour mode or directional inscattering), aerial perspective from the sky model, and fog that reaches the sky by the right amount.

Light shafts that vanish when the camera turns

Cause
Screen-space shafts made by blurring bright pixels from the sun's position. When the sun or the window leaves the screen there is nothing left to blur.
Ask for
Shafts from shadowed volumetric fog for any shot that matters, keeping screen-space shafts only as an extra on sun-facing views.

Speckled or smeared clouds

Cause
Too few march steps or light samples, or temporal reprojection failing when the camera turns quickly or at the screen edges, where there is no history.
Ask for
More primary steps where the cloud layer is seen, a tuned accumulation factor, and a test of fast camera turns and cuts, not only slow pans.

7 Say it to your AI

Atmosphere briefs go well when they name which layer is involved (height fog, volumetric fog, sky or clouds), the lights that feed it, the camera moves that show the problem, and a budget in milliseconds.

Questions to ask when reviewing atmosphere work

  1. Which layers are on, and over what distance does each one apply?Volumetric fog usually covers tens of metres; something else must cover the rest of the view.
  2. Where does the fog colour come from?A constant colour that does not follow the sky causes the grey horizon band and wrong-looking sunsets.
  3. Do transparent materials and particles receive fog?Full-screen fog passes skip them; check glass, water and smoke in the thickest fog.
  4. Which lights feed the volumetric fog, and which of them cast volumetric shadows?Each shadowed local light is a large share of the fog's cost.
  5. How does it behave on fast camera turns and cuts?Temporal history hides the coarse grid and the cloud noise, and fails exactly then.

8 Check yourself

  1. A level designer asks why the fog looks the same on a mountain top as in the valley below it. What kind of fog is the game using, and what would fix it?

    Show answer

    Distance fog, which depends only on how far a pixel is from the camera. Height fog makes density fall with altitude, so the valley fills with mist while rays from the summit pass through thin air.

  2. Why does volumetric fog cost about the same at 1080p and 4K, when most effects cost four times as much?

    Show answer

    Its expensive work happens in the froxel grid, which has a fixed number of cells, such as 160 × 90 × 64, whatever the screen resolution. Only the final pass that applies the fog to each pixel grows with resolution.

  3. The god rays in a cathedral scene disappear when the player looks slightly away from the windows. What is the likely cause?

    Show answer

    Screen-space light shafts, made by blurring bright pixels outwards from the light's position on screen. With the windows off screen there is nothing to blur. Shadowed volumetric fog keeps the shafts in the volume in front of the camera, so they stay.
