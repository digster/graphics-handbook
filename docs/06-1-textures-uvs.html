6.1 Textures and UVs

A mesh only knows the shape of an object. Its surface detail comes from flat images the GPU reads while it shades, pinned onto the mesh by two numbers stored at every corner. How those images are sized, cut up and laid out decides how sharp a game looks and how much memory it needs.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A texture is an image the GPU reads while it decides the colour of each pixel. Most textures hold colour. Many hold other data in the same grid of numbers: which way the surface bumps, how rough or metallic it is, how much light reaches into its cracks.

A mesh on its own is a shell of flat triangles. To put a picture on it, every corner gets UV coordinates: a position on the image, from 0 to 1 across and from 0 to 1 up. The GPU stretches each triangle of the image onto the matching triangle of the mesh. Making those UVs means cutting the surface open and flattening it, which is skilled work done by an artist in a modelling tool.

Everyday comparison

A world map is the surface of a globe unwrapped onto a flat sheet. To flatten it, the mapmaker has to cut the globe somewhere, which is why the Pacific is split across the two edges of many maps. And something always stretches. On a common map Greenland looks as large as Africa, though Africa is about fourteen times bigger.

UV unwrapping makes the same two choices for every model: where to cut, and how much stretching to accept.

The problem it solves

Detail could be stored as colour at each corner, but a brick wall would then need a corner at every brick edge, which means millions of vertices for one wall. A texture stores millions of colour samples in one compact grid, and the mesh needs only enough corners to give the shape.

The detail moves out of the geometry and into an image that can be painted, reused, swapped and shrunk on its own.

Why a lead cares

Textures are one of the largest uses of memory and download size in most games, and their size and count are set by decisions made long before anything is optimised: texture resolution per asset type, how consistently detail is spread across assets, and how much is shared. Bad UVs cost artist days to redo once an asset is textured. Agreeing a texel density, a set of shared textures and naming and import rules on day one is cheap, and it makes AI-written tools for checking assets straightforward to brief.

3 How it works

Follow one texture from the grid of numbers it is made of to the many ways engines arrange and share them. There are eight steps.

1 A grid of texels

A texture is a rectangle of values. Each value is a texel, short for texture element: the texture's equivalent of a pixel. The name keeps the two apart, because a texel almost never lands on exactly one pixel of the screen. Up close one texel can cover dozens of pixels; far away one pixel can cover dozens of texels. 6.2 Filtering and mipmaps is about what the GPU does in each case.

A colour texel usually holds four colour channels, red, green, blue and alpha, at 8 bits each: 4 bytes before any compression. The texture resolution is its width and height in texels. A 2048 × 2048 texture holds 4,194,304 texels, which is 16 MiB at 4 bytes each (1 MiB is 1,048,576 bytes).

Game textures are almost always a power of two on each side: 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096. Older GPUs required it. Modern ones accept any size, but powers of two still halve cleanly all the way down to 1 × 1 when the smaller copies of 6.2 are made, and they fit the 4 × 4 blocks that compressed formats work in. Sides do not have to match: 2048 × 1024 is common.

2 UVs: from corners to pixels

UV coordinates are stored once per vertex. When the GPU fills in a triangle, it blends the three corners' UVs for each pixel inside it, so every pixel gets its own position on the image. The pixel shader then reads the texture at that position. That read is called sampling, and the result is one colour, or one set of numbers, for that pixel.

U runs across the image and V runs up it, both from 0 to 1, whatever the resolution. A UV of (0.5, 0.5) is the centre of a 256-texel texture and of a 4096-texel one. That is what lets an engine swap a texture for a smaller version on a low-end platform without touching the mesh.

Simplified

On a triangle seen at an angle, the blend is corrected for perspective, so the texture does not swim across it. 5.3 Rasterization covers how. Tools also disagree on which way V runs: some put V = 0 at the top of the image, some at the bottom. Exporters and importers convert between the two, and a missed conversion flips the texture upside down.

3 Unwrapping: seams and islands

UV unwrapping is the job of giving every vertex its UVs. A closed surface cannot lie flat without cuts, so the artist marks UV seams along some edges, and the tool flattens the pieces between them. Each connected flat piece is a UV island. The tool then packs the islands into the 0 to 1 square, leaving gaps between them.

Every cut is a trade. More seams let each island lie flatter, with less stretching, and pack more tightly. Fewer seams mean fewer places where the texture can visibly break and fewer split vertices. Artists hide seams where players rarely look: under arms, along hard edges, on the back of props.

Try it below. Step the cube through its unwrap, then switch layouts. One island keeps 5 of the cube's 12 edges joined but leaves most of the texture empty. Six packed islands use far more of it at the cost of cutting every edge. The uneven layout shows what happens when islands are sized carelessly; turn on the texel density view to see it in numbers.

4 Texel density

Texel density is how many texels cover each metre of a surface. It is the texels across an island divided by the metres across the face it covers. A 1 m crate with a 512-texel texture across each face has 512 texels per metre. A 4 m wall with a 1024-texel texture across it has 256.

Density matters more than resolution, because players compare things side by side. Put that crate against that wall and the wall looks soft, whatever its texture size says. Studios set a target density per kind of asset and camera distance, often a few hundred to around a thousand texels per metre for objects the player walks up to, and check every asset against it. Godot's documentation gives the same advice: size each texture so its texel density matches the objects around it.

Stretching is the same problem inside one island. If an island is squashed in one direction compared with its face, density differs along U and V and the texture smears. Artists check both by applying a checker texture: squares that stay square and the same size everywhere mean uniform density.

5 Beyond 0 to 1: wrap modes

UVs do not have to stay between 0 and 1. A floor 10 m long can have U run from 0 to 20, so a texture made to tile seamlessly repeats twenty times along it. What the GPU does with a UV outside the 0 to 1 range is the wrap mode, set per texture or per sampler.

  • Repeat (also called wrap) drops the whole number and keeps the fraction, so 2.3 reads the same texel as 0.3. Tiling floors, walls and terrain use it.
  • Clamp holds UVs at 0 or 1, so the edge texels stretch outwards. Decals, UI images, skies and anything that must not repeat use it.
  • Mirror flips the texture on every repeat, so edges always meet their own mirror image and never show a seam, at the cost of visible symmetry.

Graphics APIs offer a couple more. Direct3D 12 also has border, which returns a fixed colour outside the square, and mirror once, which mirrors a single time and then clamps. Wrap modes can differ along U and V: a road texture often repeats along its length and clamps across it.

6 Sharing textures: atlases and trim sheets

Every texture an object uses must be bound before it is drawn, and objects that use different textures generally cannot share a draw call. Two layouts let many objects share one texture.

A texture atlas packs the textures of several objects into one image, each in its own region, and each object's UVs point into its region. Sprites, UI icons, fonts and small props are classic atlas users. The price is that an atlas region cannot repeat with the wrap mode, since repeating would run into its neighbours, and neighbouring regions need a gap so that blurred, shrunk copies of the texture do not bleed into each other.

A trim sheet is a texture made of horizontal strips, each a reusable detail that tiles along U: a bolted edge, a panel seam, a pipe, a grille. Instead of an image painted for one asset, artists lay the UVs of many walls, doors and frames over the strips they want. One trim sheet can texture a whole set of buildings at a constant texel density, and many environment teams rely on them for exactly that reason.

7 Cubemaps and texture arrays

Not every texture is one flat picture. A cubemap is six square images arranged as the faces of a cube, and it is read with a direction instead of a UV: the GPU finds which face the direction points through and where on that face it lands. That makes it the natural format for anything seen in every direction from one point, such as the sky and the reflections in 7.6 Reflections and image-based lighting.

A texture array is a stack of textures of the same size and format, read with a UV plus a layer number. The shader can choose the layer per pixel, so one binding can serve a terrain's grass, rock, sand and snow, a set of decals or the frames of an animation. Unity's manual describes arrays as an optimisation for techniques that use many textures of the same size and format; Godot's built-in materials cannot show them, so they need a custom shader.

8 Channel packing

Many material inputs need one number per texel, not three. Channel packing stores up to four of them in the red, green, blue and alpha channels of one texture. The most common is an ORM map: ambient occlusion in red, roughness in green and metallic in blue. Godot's ORM material reads exactly that order, and many asset stores and texturing tools export it.

Packing turns three texture reads into one and three files into one. It also changes how the texture must be imported. The channels hold data, not colour, so the texture must be read as linear values with the sRGB flag off (3.2 Colour done right); and the shader must agree with the exporter about which channel holds what. There is no standard beyond convention, so write the team's order down.

5 How it breaks

Most texture mapping faults are visible in a still screenshot. These six cover most of what reaches review.

Stretched or smeared texture

Cause
The UV island does not match the shape of its face: it was squashed in one direction, or a planar projection was used on a side that faces away from it.
Ask for
A checker texture on the asset and a re-unwrap of the stretched islands, with the stretch check of the modelling tool turned on.

A visible line where the pattern breaks

Cause
A UV seam sits somewhere players look, and the texture does not continue across it. At a distance, islands packed too close bleed into each other along their edges.
Ask for
Seams moved to hidden edges, texturing painted across seams in 3D, and a wider gap between islands, filled with the colour of the island next to it.

One asset blurry beside another

Cause
Texel density differs between assets that sit together: the blurry one spreads too few texels over too many metres.
Ask for
A project texel density target per kind of asset, and an automated check that reports texels per metre for every mesh.

Obvious tiling pattern

Cause
A tiling texture repeats at a short interval and has one distinctive feature, which forms a grid at a distance.
Ask for
Fewer distinctive features in the tile, a second layer at a different scale, decals, or blending between materials by vertex colour or a mask.

Thin lines along an image's edge

Cause
A texture that must not tile is set to repeat, so filtering blends in texels from the opposite edge. In an atlas, the same happens when a region touches its neighbour.
Ask for
Clamp for decals, UI and other non-tiling textures, set by an import rule per texture type, and gaps between atlas regions.

Wrong shine or metalness

Cause
A packed ORM map was imported as sRGB, which bends its values, or the shader reads roughness from a different channel than the exporter wrote it to.
Ask for
The packing order written down, sRGB off for every data texture, and a material test scene with known rough, smooth and metal swatches.

A frame capture tool such as RenderDoc or PIX shows every texture bound to a draw and the sampler settings it was read with, which settles wrap mode and sRGB questions in a minute.

Godot default

Godot imports images with 2D-friendly defaults: lossless, with no smaller copies for distance. Its Detect 3D option switches a texture to GPU compression and turns those copies on once it sees the texture used in 3D, then reimports it. Set the import options of 3D textures deliberately rather than relying on detection.

7 Say it to your AI

Good texture briefs give the engine and version, the target texel density in texels per metre, the texture budget, and say which textures hold colour and which hold data. Adapt these.

BriefUse when assets look inconsistently sharp
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.7. Our environment art target is 512 texels per metre for anything the player can walk up to, and 256 for background set dressing.

Write an editor utility that, for every static mesh under /Game/Environment:
1. Computes texel density from UV channel 0 and the size of the texture bound to the material's base colour, as the median over all triangles, in texels per metre. Also report the 10th and 90th percentiles, so stretched islands show up.
2. Reports the share of the 0 to 1 UV square covered by islands.
3. Flags meshes more than 25% off their target, and meshes with overlapping islands in the lightmap UV channel.
Write the results to a CSV. Explain how it handles UVs outside 0 to 1 and meshes with several materials.
  • A numeric target turns a vague complaint about blurriness into something a tool can check.
  • Median and percentiles catch stretching, not only the average.
  • Asking about tiling UVs and multiple materials exposes the cases a naive script gets wrong.
Bug reportUse when edges of images show stray lines
Unity 6, URP. Our UI icons come from one 2048 × 2048 atlas and our wall decals are separate textures. Many icons show a thin line of a neighbouring icon's colour along one edge, and the decals show a faint line of colour from their opposite edge along the top.

Find the cause of each. Check the wrap mode on the atlas and the decal textures, the gap between atlas regions, whether the UVs reach the exact edge of each region, and the filter and mipmap settings. Tell me which settings to change on which textures, what gap the atlas needs, and how to add an import rule so new textures get the right wrap mode by type.
  • Two related symptoms with different causes stop the AI from fixing only one.
  • Naming the suspects (wrap mode, gaps, UV edges, filtering) keeps the search narrow.
  • An import rule prevents the same bug on the next hundred textures.

Questions to ask when reviewing texture and UV work

  1. What texel density does this asset have, and what is the target for its kind of asset?Density mismatches are the most visible texture fault, and only a number settles them.
  2. Which textures hold colour and which hold data, and is sRGB set to match?A packed or normal map read as sRGB gives wrong shading with no error message.
  3. What wrap mode does each texture use, and why?Repeat on a decal or clamp on a tiling texture shows up as lines and smears.
  4. How wide are the gaps between islands and atlas regions, at full size?Gaps halve at every smaller copy; too narrow and islands bleed into each other at distance.
  5. Which channel holds what in each packed texture, and where is that written down?Exporters, importers and shaders must all agree, and nothing checks it for you.
  6. How much of the UV square is unused?Empty space is memory spent on nothing, and tighter packing raises density for free.

8 Check yourself

  1. How many texels does a 2048 × 2048 texture hold, and how much memory does it take at 4 bytes per texel?

    Show answer

    4,194,304 texels, which is 16 MiB. With the smaller copies for distance it is about 21.3 MiB, and about a quarter of that once compressed at 1 byte per texel.

  2. A 1 m cube is unwrapped as one cross-shaped island in a 1024 texture, giving 256 texels per metre. Cutting it into six packed islands raises that to 341. What did that cost?

    Show answer

    Seams. The cross keeps 5 of the 12 edges joined and cuts 7; six separate islands cut all 12. Each extra seam is a place the texture can visibly break and adds split vertices.

  3. A decal on a wall shows a faint line of colour along its top edge that is not in the image. Which setting do you check first?

    Show answer

    Its wrap mode. Set to repeat, the GPU blends texels from the opposite edge into the border when it filters. Decals and other images that must not tile should clamp.
