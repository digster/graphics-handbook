7.5 Shadows at scale

One shadow map can cover a room. Stretched over a valley, each of its texels grows wider than a hand, and the shadows right in front of the player turn to blocks. This chapter shows how engines spend shadow texels where the camera needs them, with cascades, atlases and virtual shadow maps, and how they fill the gaps at the smallest and largest scales.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The sun lights everything the player can see, from the boots on their feet to the mountains on the horizon. A shadow map has a fixed number of texels, so the more ground it covers, the coarser each texel gets. The camera has the opposite need: something 2 m away fills a big part of the screen and needs fine detail, while something 200 m away is a few pixels and needs very little.

Every technique in this chapter answers the same question: how do you put shadow texels where the screen needs them? Split the view into a few maps by distance. Pack many small maps into one big texture. Keep maps that did not change. Or cut a huge virtual map into pages and only fill the ones the camera can see. At the extremes, two cheaper tricks take over: a short trace through the depth buffer for the last few centimetres, and a trace through a simplified copy of the world for the far distance.

Everyday comparison

Planning a road trip, you use a street map for your own neighbourhood, a regional map for the next hundred kilometres and a country map for the rest. Each map is the same size of paper, but each covers a much larger area than the one before.

Nobody prints the whole country at street-map detail. It would fill a warehouse, and you would only ever look at the page you are on.

The problem it solves

To give a 400 m wide view the 2 mm shadow texels the screen needs right in front of the camera, one map would need 200,000 × 200,000 texels: 80 GB at 16 bits each.

Four 2048 × 2048 cascades take 32 MB and put almost the same detail where the player is looking.

Why a lead cares

The sun's shadow is often the most expensive single shadow in a frame, because it covers everything in view and redraws the scene once per cascade. Its settings, the cascade count, the shadow distance and the splits, sit in every quality preset and decide familiar complaints: blurry shadows at the player's feet, a visible line on the ground that moves with the camera, shadows that pop in a few metres ahead. Unreal Engine 5 projects use virtual shadow maps by default, which changes the budget again and works best with Nanite geometry. Knowing which approach your game uses tells you which numbers to ask for.

3 How it works

Start with why one map fails, then follow the fixes in roughly the order engines adopted them: cascades, atlases, caching and virtual shadow maps, and the specialist techniques that cover the very small, the very far and the moving.

1 Why one map cannot cover a big view

A directional light's shadow map is an orthographic picture: every texel covers the same patch of ground. The camera's picture is a perspective one: each screen pixel covers a patch that grows with distance. A pixel 2 m away is about 2 mm wide on a 1080p screen with a 90° field of view; a pixel 200 m away is about 21 cm wide.

So a single map is always wrong somewhere. Fit it to the whole view and near the camera dozens of pixels share one texel, which shows as blocky, stair-stepped edges. This mismatch is called perspective aliasing. Unity's manual describes it as shadow map pixels close to the camera that look enlarged and chunky compared to those farther away.

Raising the resolution helps slowly and costs fast: memory grows with the square of the size, so halving the texel size quadruples it. What the screen needs grows with distance, so the fix has to as well.

2 Cascades: one map per distance range

Cascaded shadow maps, CSM, cut the camera's view frustum into a few slices by distance and give each slice its own shadow map. Microsoft's article on the technique lists the steps every frame: partition the frustum, fit an orthographic projection around each slice, render a shadow map for each, then, while lighting each pixel, pick the map that matches its distance. The distance where one slice ends and the next begins is a cascade split.

Because every cascade has the same number of texels but the near ones cover far less ground, detail ends up roughly where the screen needs it. The price is one shadow pass per cascade: four cascades draw the shadow casters up to four times.

3 Where the splits go

Zhang, Sun and Nyman compared three ways to place the splits in GPU Gems 3. Even splits divide the distance into equal lengths, which leaves the first cascade far too long. Logarithmic splits make each cascade reach the same multiple further than the last, which they describe as the optimal spread of perspective aliasing, but the first cascades get so short that few objects fit in them. Their practical split scheme blends the two with a weight, usually written λ, and they use 0.5 as the default.

Maths The practical split scheme

For m cascades between a near distance n and the shadow distance f, split i (from 1 to m) is:

logi = n × (f / n)i / m    eveni = n + (f − n) × i / m

spliti = λ × logi + (1 − λ) × eveni

With a 90° field of view, the smallest circle around a slice is centred on its far edge with the far distance as its radius, so a stable map for the slice is a square 2 × spliti across, and its texel size is 2 × spliti ÷ resolution. A screen pixel at distance z is 2z ÷ 1920 wide.

Engines expose the same choice differently. Unreal's Distribution Exponent pulls cascades towards the camera as it grows. Unity sets Split 1 to 3 directly, in percent of the shadow distance or in metres. Godot's DirectionalLight3D uses fractions of its Max Distance, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 by default, so 10 m, 20 m and 50 m of the default 100 m.

4 Shadow distance: where shadows stop

The shadow distance is how far from the camera real-time shadows reach. It is the far end of the last cascade, and it is the single most powerful setting here, because every split is a share of it. Halving it halves every texel. Unity's URP documentation uses the example of reducing Max Distance from 40 to 10 so a 1024 map looks better near the camera than a 2048 map did.

Past the shadow distance there are no dynamic shadows, so engines fade them out over a border rather than cutting them: URP's Last Border, HDRP's Border 4, Godot's Fade Start (80% of Max Distance by default) and Unreal's Distance Fadeout Fraction. What fills the distance beyond depends on the game: nothing, baked shadows, a few extra far cascades for large objects only (Unreal's Far Shadow Cascade Count), or distance-field shadows (step 10).

5 Seams between cascades

Where one cascade hands over to the next, the texel size jumps. Shadow edges change from fine steps to coarse ones at a fixed distance from the camera, and filtering (PCF, 7.4) softens each side by a different amount, so the boundary can show as a line that slides across the ground as the player walks.

6 Keeping cascades still

A cascade's map is re-fitted to its slice every frame. If its size changes when the camera turns, every texel changes size and shadow edges crawl, the shimmering from 7.4's gallery. Two habits stop it. Fit the map around the circle (in 3D, the sphere) that encloses the slice, whose size does not depend on where the camera points. Then move the map only in whole-texel steps as the camera moves, so texel boundaries stay put in the world.

7 Shadow atlases for many lights

Point and spot lights cast shadows too, and a scene may have dozens. Giving each its own texture wastes memory and means switching textures while lighting. A shadow atlas packs many lights' shadow maps into one large texture, like a texture atlas packs many images, and the shader reads each light's region by its offset and scale.

8 Caching: do not redraw what did not move

Most shadow casters never move, and many lights never move either. Shadow caching keeps a shadow map from earlier frames and re-renders it only when something that affects it changes. Godot does this for every omni and spot light: each frame it checks whether the light needs a different atlas slot or whether any object affecting the map changed, and if neither happened, the map is left untouched. HDRP lets you choose per light: Every Frame, On Enable or On Demand. It can also cache only the static casters and render the dynamic ones on top every frame, at the cost of a copy from the cached map into the dynamic one and memory in both atlases.

Caching saves the whole shadow pass when it works. When it does not, the cost lands all at once: moving the light, or a caster that changes shape every frame, forces a full redraw.

9 Virtual shadow maps: pages where pixels need them

Virtual shadow maps apply virtual texturing (6.3) to shadows. In Unreal Engine 5, where they are the default for new projects, every shadow map has a virtual resolution of 16,384 × 16,384 texels, cut into pages of 128 × 128. After the depth pass, the engine works out which pages the on-screen pixels will actually sample, and only those pages are given memory and rendered, into a fixed pool of physical pages.

For the sun, Unreal uses a clipmap: a stack of levels centred on the camera, each its own 16K virtual map covering twice the radius of the one inside it. By default, levels 6 to 22 are used, from about 64 cm around the camera to about 40 km. Each pixel uses the level whose texel size suits its distance, so the cascades are effectively 17 deep with no splits to tune.

Three things decide what virtual shadow maps cost. The number of pages drawn each frame: moving the light invalidates every cached page, and anything that deforms in the shader, such as World Position Offset on foliage, skinned characters or Pixel Depth Offset, invalidates its pages every frame. The geometry drawn into those pages: Epic's documentation says the system is designed for Nanite and that non-Nanite geometry is much more expensive to render into it. And the size of the physical page pool, set by r.Shadow.Virtual.MaxPhysicalPages, which can overflow. Soft edges come from Shadow Map Ray Tracing, a few rays per pixel through the map itself, 8 at the Epic scalability level, softened by the light's Source Angle or Source Radius.

10 Contact shadows: the last few centimetres

Even a fine cascade has texels of a centimetre or more, and bias pushes the shadow a little away from where objects touch the ground. Contact shadows fill that gap in screen space. For each pixel, the shader steps a short way towards the light through the depth buffer, the same kind of ray marching chapter 8.4 explores. If a step lands behind a stored surface, something on screen blocks the light, and the pixel is shadowed.

Because contact shadows only see the depth buffer, they inherit every screen-space blind spot: nothing off screen casts, nothing hidden behind another surface casts, and a thin pole looks as solid as a wall. They are a finishing layer on top of shadow maps, short and cheap, never a replacement. Epic's documentation adds that with virtual shadow maps they are usually not needed for sharp contact detail.

11 Distance-field shadows: soft shadows far away

Beyond the last cascade, a different representation of the world can take over. A signed distance field stores, for points in a volume around a mesh, the distance to its nearest surface: positive outside, negative inside. A ray can then jump forward by that distance at every step without ever passing through anything, which makes tracing towards the sun cheap. Distance-field shadows trace that ray for every shaded pixel against the scene's mesh distance fields.

In Unreal, Distance Field Shadows is on by default for new directional lights and covers the range from the end of the cascades out to the Distance Field Shadow Distance; Epic's example shows trees shadowing up to 1.2 km away. It needs Generate Mesh Distance Fields in the project settings. The fields are built offline, so only rigid meshes cast: no skeletal meshes, and World Position Offset is not reflected. The shadows are computed at half resolution, which can make their edges jagged.

12 Capsule shadows and ray-traced shadows

Characters move and deform, so they cannot have a baked distance field. Capsule shadows, an Unreal feature, stand a character in for a set of capsules attached to its bones, taken from a physics asset, and cast soft shadows from those. Their main use is in areas lit by baked or bounced light, where a shadow map has no single direction to work from: Capsule Indirect Shadow casts a soft, directional shadow there, from Lightmass's volume lighting samples. Capsule Direct Shadow, the version for direct light, is not rendered for lights that use virtual shadow maps.

At the top end, ray tracing (8.6) answers the shadow question directly: for each pixel, trace a ray to the light against the real triangles. There is no texel size, no cascade and no bias to tune, and an area light's soft shadow falls out of tracing several rays towards different points of it. The costs are dedicated hardware, an acceleration structure for the whole scene, and noise that needs denoising when only a few rays per pixel are affordable. In HDRP, ray-traced shadows replace the cascade shadow map for a directional light, with a Sample Count per light whose cost grows linearly.

What makes the bill grow

  • Shadow distanceThe far cascade's area grows with the square of it, and so do the casters it draws.
  • Cascade countEach cascade is another shadow pass over its casters.
  • ResolutionMemory grows four times for each doubling of the map size.
  • Moving thingsCaches and virtual pages only save work for casters that stay still and do not deform.
  • Shadowed lightsEvery point and spot light with shadows needs atlas space and its own passes, six for a point light.
  • Rays per pixelSoft virtual shadows, contact shadows and ray-traced shadows all scale with screen resolution and samples.

In proportion

The cheapest saving is usually the shadow distance. Players rarely notice a dynamic shadow ending at 80 m instead of 200 m if something else, baked shadows, distance-field shadows or fog, covers the far range, and the far cascade's draws shrink by more than six times.

5 How it breaks

Large-scale shadow bugs tend to show at fixed distances from the camera, which is the clue that separates them from the bias and acne problems of 7.4.

A line on the ground that follows the camera

Cause
A cascade boundary with no blend band, or one too narrow, so shadow detail and softness jump at a fixed distance.
Ask for
The blend or border setting per cascade, split distances moved away from where players look most, and a capture with cascades colour-coded.

Shadows pop in a few steps ahead

Cause
The shadow distance ends where the player can clearly see, with a short or missing fade, and nothing covers the range beyond it.
Ask for
The shadow distance and fade per quality level, and a plan for the far range: distance-field shadows, baked shadows, far cascades for large objects, or fog.

Blocky shadows at the player's feet

Cause
The first cascade is too long, from even splits or a long shadow distance, so its texels are many screen pixels wide.
Ask for
Texels per metre where the character stands, a first split that ends just past the character, and a shorter shadow distance before a higher resolution.

Shadows of tall objects cut off or missing

Cause
Casters outside the cascade's box, towards the light or behind the camera, are culled from the shadow pass, so their shadows end at a straight line or vanish.
Ask for
How casters outside each cascade are kept (Godot's Pancake Size fixes missing shadows from large objects; URP's Conservative Enclosing Sphere stops culling at cascade corners), and a test with tall objects just out of view.

Square holes or flicker in virtual shadows

Cause
The physical page pool overflowed, or too many pages are invalidated every frame by moving lights, foliage with World Position Offset or deforming meshes.
Ask for
Virtual shadow map page statistics, the pool size, per-object Shadow Cache Invalidation Behavior for swaying foliage, and fewer shadow-casting local lights.

Contact shadows from things that should not cast

Cause
Contact shadows read the depth buffer, so anything that writes depth casts them, even with shadow casting switched off. HDRP documents this.
Ask for
Which materials write depth, a material that does not for glass, effects and helpers, and a shorter contact length.

To diagnose, colour the cascades. URP's Rendering Debugger has a Shadow Cascades view, and a frame capture in RenderDoc or PIX shows one shadow pass per cascade with its draw count. For virtual shadow maps, Unreal's visualisation modes and page statistics show cached and invalidated pages; turn them off before profiling, as they add a small cost.

Virtual shadow maps change the rules

In Unreal, per-light Shadow Bias and Shadow Slope Bias do not affect virtual shadow maps, cascade settings only apply with classic shadow maps, and Capsule Direct Shadow is not rendered with them. Epic's documentation calls virtual shadow maps a likely poor fit for projects that do not use Nanite. Check which method a project uses before tuning anything in this chapter.

Questions to ask when reviewing large-scale shadow work

  1. Which technique does the sun use: cascades, virtual shadow maps or ray tracing?Every other setting depends on it, and some do nothing under the others.
  2. What is the texel size where the player usually looks, and where does the first cascade end?Blurry shadows at the feet usually mean a long first cascade.
  3. What happens past the shadow distance?Without fade or a far-range technique, shadows pop in visibly.
  4. How many casters does each cascade draw, and do small objects need to cast in the far cascades?The far cascade covers the largest area and often draws the most.
  5. Which lights and casters re-render every frame, and why?Caching and virtual pages only save work for things that stay still.
  6. Are contact shadows on for every light, and what writes depth that should not cast?They cost per light per pixel and catch unintended casters.

8 Check yourself

  1. A single 2048 × 2048 sun shadow map is fitted around a 90° view out to 200 m. How wide is a texel, and why does that look bad at the player's feet but fine on distant hills?

    Show answer

    The map must cover a 400 m square, so a texel is 400 m ÷ 2048 ≈ 19.5 cm. At 2 m a screen pixel is about 2 mm wide, so one texel spans dozens of pixels and edges turn to blocks. At 200 m a pixel is about 21 cm, close to one texel. That mismatch is perspective aliasing.

  2. Your four cascades use even splits over 200 m. What happens to shadows near the camera if you switch to logarithmic splits, and what new problem appears?

    Show answer

    The first cascade shrinks from about 51 m to under 4 m, so its texels drop from about 5 cm to under half a centimetre and near shadows sharpen. But the first cascade now ends before a third-person character a few metres away, so the character falls into a coarser cascade. A blend of the two schemes, the practical split scheme, is the usual compromise.

  3. A forest with swaying trees makes Unreal's virtual shadow maps expensive, though the same forest standing still is cheap. Why?

    Show answer

    Virtual shadow maps keep pages from earlier frames and redraw only invalidated ones. World Position Offset moves the trees' vertices in the shader, which invalidates their pages every frame, so most of the forest's pages are redrawn every frame instead of being reused.
