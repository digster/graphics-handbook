11.1 Frame budgets

A frame rate target turns into a fixed number of milliseconds, and every system in the game has to fit inside it. This chapter shows how teams split that time, how to tell whether the CPU or the GPU is the one running out, and why an average frame rate says little about how a game feels: 1% lows, frame pacing, latency and headroom say the rest.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A frame budget is the time one frame may take: 1000 divided by the target frame rate, so 33.3 ms at 30 fps, 16.7 ms at 60 and 8.3 ms at 120. 0.2 What a frame is introduced it. A budget only works when it is split up and owned: so many milliseconds for shadows, so many for animation, so many kept back for surprises, each checked against captures from the slowest target hardware.

Budgets are written in milliseconds because milliseconds add up and frame rates do not. Going from 30 to 40 fps means finding 8.3 ms; going from 60 to 70 means finding 2.4 ms; going from 120 to 144 means finding 1.4 ms. The same effect is a bigger share of a faster frame.

An everyday comparison

A television news bulletin. The programme is exactly 30 minutes, every night. The editor gives each story a slot in minutes, keeps a minute in hand for breaking news, and the studio, the graphics team and the newsreader all work in parallel. If any one of them overruns, the whole programme overruns, however early the others finished.

The problem it solves

Without a budget, every team adds its feature and the frame rate falls a little at a time until nobody can say which change broke it. With one, each team owns a number, every capture is checked against it, and a new feature has to find its milliseconds before it goes in.

Why a lead cares

The frame rate target is a product decision; the budget is how it is kept. A lead sets the target per platform and mode, makes sure each system has an owner and a number, asks which side limits before anyone optimises, and reads 1% lows and frame-time graphs instead of averages. Most performance arguments end when someone puts a capture next to the budget.

3 How it works

The steps go from one frame to many: how a budget is split, which lane sets the frame time and how to find out, a budget builder to try it, then the measures that describe a whole run, how it feels in the hand, and the margin that keeps it safe.

1 Splitting the budget

Budget allocation divides the frame between the systems that run in it. On the CPU, the game thread gets slots for gameplay, physics, animation and AI, and the render thread for culling and recording draw calls. On the GPU, each phase of 10.1 A deferred frame gets its own. Each lane has the whole frame, because the lanes run side by side, and each keeps something back.

2 Which lane sets the frame time

The lanes form a pipeline, as 2.2 The game loop and threads showed: the game thread updates frame N while the render thread records frame N − 1 and the GPU draws frame N − 2. Once every lane has a frame to work on, a finished frame comes out as often as the slowest lane can produce one. That lane is the bottleneck, and the others wait for it.

A game whose GPU is the bottleneck is GPU-bound: lowering the resolution or the effects speeds it up, and making the game code faster changes nothing. A game whose game thread or render thread is the bottleneck is CPU-bound: lowering the resolution does nothing, and fewer draw calls or cheaper systems help. Godot's optimisation guide says it in one line, that the total frame time is determined by the slower of the two.

3 Telling CPU-bound from GPU-bound

Every engine and every frame-timing tool shows the lanes side by side. Unreal's stat unit lists Frame, Game, Draw (the render thread), GPU and RHIT; its documentation says that when Frame is close to Game, the game thread is the bottleneck, and when it is close to Draw, the render thread. Unity's Highlights profiler module compares CPU Active Time with GPU Time against a target frame time, marks frames where either is over, and counts them by resource. Intel's PresentMon measures, for each frame of any game, how long the CPU was busy and how long the GPU was busy.

A quick experiment confirms the reading: drop the internal resolution sharply. If the frame time falls a lot, the GPU was the bottleneck. If it barely moves, the bottleneck is on the CPU, and the per-thread numbers say which thread.

4 Build a budget

The budget builder below is a small model of one frame. Pick a target, then add passes and systems, raise the resolution or the draw calls, and watch which lane runs out first. When a lane bursts the budget, the demo ranks the cuts that save the most frame time on that lane; try one and see whether another lane takes over as the bottleneck.

Simplified

Real frames vary from one to the next, engines overlap lanes in more complicated ways, dynamic resolution changes the GPU's cost every few frames, and the render thread is split across worker threads in modern engines. The model keeps the part that matters for a budget: each lane has the whole frame, and the longest one sets the pace.

5 Averages hide the worst frames

An average frame rate is the wrong number to judge a run by, because a few very slow frames barely move it. A frame-time graph shows every frame as a bar against the budget line, and the 1% low turns its worst part into one number. Tools define it differently, so check which one a report uses. NVIDIA's FrameView averages the slowest 1% of frames and reports the result as a frame rate, and the same for the slowest 0.1%; it also offers a percentile figure, the frame rate that separates the slowest 1% of frames from the rest. FrameView's guide adds that the closer the 1% low is to the average, the more consistent the experience.

6 Frame pacing

Frame pacing is about rhythm. Two runs can share an average and even a 1% low and still feel different, if one shows its frames at regular intervals and the other alternates short and long. Objects then move unequal distances between images, which reads as judder. Android's Frame Pacing library documentation describes a 30 fps game on a 60 Hz phone showing frames for 49, 16 and 33 ms in turn when frames are released at the wrong moments, and says short frames followed by long ones are perceived as stutter.

The usual fixes are to cap the frame rate at a level the game can always hold, to present frames at even intervals, and on displays with variable refresh, to keep frame times steady rather than merely fast. Android's library can also choose a refresh rate that divides evenly: on a phone with 60 and 90 Hz modes, a game that cannot hold 60 can run at an even 45 instead of dropping to 30.

7 Input latency and the budget

The budget also sets how quickly the game answers the player. Frame latency, from 2.2, is the time from pressing a button to seeing its result, and in a pipelined frame it is several frame times long. Halving the frame time halves it; every frame that waits in the GPU queue adds one more. Tools measure it directly: PresentMon reports click-to-photon latency per frame, and FrameView reports PC Latency in games that support it.

8 Headroom

Headroom is the part of the budget left unspent on purpose. Typical scenes are planned below the deadline, often by about 10%, as 0.2 suggested, so that an explosion filling the screen, a streaming burst or a crowd does not push frames past it. Headroom also pays for the features that arrive after the budget was written, for a phone that is getting warm (10.2), and for the spread between the best and the worst console a game ships on.

Dynamic resolution spends headroom automatically: it watches GPU time and lowers the internal resolution when a scene gets heavy. It helps only when the game is GPU-bound, and it cannot help with a single long frame, because it reacts after the fact.

4 What it costs

A budget costs nothing to write and a lot to keep. The table turns targets into time, with 10% held back; the people-time is in keeping captures, owners and alarms running for the whole of production.

Worked example. A 60 fps game is GPU-bound at 15.0 ms. A new volumetric fog costs 2 ms on the GPU. The frame becomes 17.0 ms: over budget, so on a fixed 60 Hz display with v-sync every frame waits for a second refresh and the game shows 30 fps. On a variable-refresh display it shows about 59 fps instead, and every frame of latency is 2 ms longer. To keep 60 fps with 10% headroom, the team must find 2 ms elsewhere on the GPU: a lower internal resolution, a cheaper shadow level, or the fog at half resolution. Cutting 2 ms from animation would change nothing, because the game is GPU-bound.

  • Frame rate targetEach step up shrinks every lane's budget: 120 fps leaves half of what 60 does.
  • Platform spreadThe weakest console or the minimum PC sets the real budget; the development PC hides problems.
  • ModesA Performance mode and a Quality mode are two budgets, each needing its own captures (10.3).
  • Worst casesThe busiest moment, not the average scene, has to fit, so headroom grows with how spiky the game is.
  • Session lengthOn phones and handhelds the budget shrinks as the device heats up.

The usual trade

Teams set lane budgets in pre-production, give each system an owner, and run automated captures of the same shots every night on target hardware. A change that pushes a system past its number is flagged the next morning, when it is still cheap to fix. Without that, budgets drift until a performance push near alpha.

5 How it breaks

Budgets fail by measuring the wrong thing, optimising the wrong lane, or forgetting the worst moment and the weakest machine.

Good average, players report stutter

Cause
The run was judged on its average frame rate. A few long frames, from streaming, shader compiles or a heavy event, barely move the average.
Ask for
A frame-time graph of the same run, the 1% and 0.1% lows with the tool's definition, and captures of the slowest frames.

An optimisation that changed nothing

Cause
Work was cut on a lane that was not the bottleneck: game code made faster in a GPU-bound scene, or resolution lowered in a CPU-bound one.
Ask for
The per-lane times before and after, the resolution test, and the bottleneck named before any optimisation work starts.

Judder at a decent frame rate

Cause
Uneven frame pacing: frames released at the wrong moments, an uncapped frame rate wandering under a variable-refresh display, or a software cap without v-sync.
Ask for
A frame-time graph, the cap and v-sync settings, and a cap at a rate the game always holds, with even presentation.

Over budget only on the weakest target

Cause
Budgets were checked on development PCs or the strongest console. The base console, minimum-spec PC or handheld has less of everything.
Ask for
Captures on the weakest target in every milestone build, with budgets set per platform and graphics mode.

Every new feature breaks the frame

Cause
Budget creep: the plan used up the headroom, so every addition, or every busy scene, misses the deadline.
Ask for
A budget with headroom written down, a cost estimate for each new feature before it lands, and what it replaces or which owner gives up time.

Feels sluggish at a good frame rate

Cause
Frames queue up waiting for the GPU or the display, so each one is older by the time it is seen; v-sync with extra buffers makes it worse.
Ask for
Measured latency with PresentMon or FrameView, the number of queued frames, and the engine's low-latency mode or Reflex or Anti-Lag 2 where supported.

7 Say it to your AI

Budget briefs go well when they give the target per platform and mode, the measured lane times and the definitions of every statistic.

BriefUse when setting budgets in pre-production
Unity 6.6, URP Forward+. Targets: PS5 and Xbox Series S at 60 fps, Switch 2 at 30 fps. Third-person action game, up to 40 animated enemies on screen.

1. Propose millisecond budgets per platform for the main thread, the render thread and the GPU, with 10% headroom on each.
2. Split each lane by system (gameplay, physics, animation, AI, UI) and by GPU phase (shadows, opaque, lighting, transparents, post, UI).
3. Mark every number as an estimate and say what to measure in the first vertical slice to replace it.
4. Name the Profiler modules and markers we use to check each budget, and the worst-case shot for each lane.
  • Per-lane budgets match how the hardware actually works.
  • Marking estimates keeps invented numbers from hardening into targets.
  • Naming the worst-case shot ties each budget to something QA can capture every night.
DiagnosisUse when a build feels worse than its numbers
Unreal Engine 5.8, PC, 60 fps target, VRR monitor. PresentMon log of a 3-minute run attached: average 61 fps, 1% low (slowest 1% averaged) 29 fps. stat unit in the same area: Frame 16.1, Game 15.8, Draw 9.4, GPU 11.2.

1. Say which lane limits and why, from these numbers.
2. From the log, find the slowest frames and whether they cluster in time, alternate with fast frames, or appear alone.
3. For each pattern found, give the likely cause (hitch, pacing, CPU spike) and the capture we should take next.
4. Say which fixes would not help and why.
  • Lane times let the AI name the bottleneck instead of guessing at the GPU.
  • Defining the 1% low avoids two people meaning two different numbers.
  • Asking what would not help guards against optimising the wrong lane.

Questions to ask when reviewing performance work

  1. Which lane limits, and how do we know?A fix on any other lane changes nothing.
  2. What are the 1% and 0.1% lows, and by which definition?Averages hide hitches, and tools disagree on the lows.
  3. Was it measured on the weakest target, in every graphics mode?The development PC sets nobody's frame rate.
  4. How much headroom is left in the worst shot?The busiest moment, not the average scene, has to fit.
  5. What does the frame-time graph look like?Pacing problems and hitches show there and nowhere else.

8 Check yourself

  1. stat unit shows Frame 22.0 ms, Game 21.6, Draw 12.0, GPU 15.0. What limits the frame, and what will lowering the resolution do?

    Show answer

    Frame is close to Game, so the game thread is the bottleneck: the game is CPU-bound. Lowering the resolution shortens the GPU's 15 ms, which was already waiting, so the frame time stays near 22 ms. Work has to come off the game thread.

  2. Two builds both average 60 fps. One has a 1% low of 58 fps, the other 22 fps. Which will players prefer, and what do you ask for next?

    Show answer

    The first: its slowest frames are close to the average, so it feels smooth. The second has frames far slower than the rest, which players see as stutter. Ask for its frame-time graph and captures of the slowest frames to find what causes them.

  3. A GPU-bound 60 fps game runs at 15.5 ms on a fixed 60 Hz display with v-sync. A 2 ms effect is added. What frame rate do players see, and why is the answer not 53 fps?

    Show answer

    30 fps. The frame becomes 17.5 ms, which misses the 16.7 ms refresh, so each image waits for the next one and stays up for 33.3 ms. 1000 ÷ 17.5 ≈ 57 fps would be the uncapped rate; v-sync on a fixed display rounds it down to the next whole number of refreshes.
