8.7 Path tracing and denoising

Path tracing follows light the way the rendering equation describes it: one random path per sample, bounce after bounce, until it reaches a light. Averaged over enough samples it is the reference every other technique is judged against. Real time can afford a sample or two per pixel, so the raw image is mostly noise, and smarter sampling and denoising do the rest.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

The rendering equation (8.1) says the light leaving a point is what it emits plus all the light arriving from every direction, weighted by its material. The light arriving from each direction is itself light leaving some other point, so the question never ends. Path tracing answers it by asking just one direction at a time: trace a ray, pick one random direction at the hit, trace again, and keep going until the path reaches a light. One such path is a very rough answer. The average of many is the right one.

Using random samples to estimate an average is Monte Carlo integration. Its catch is the leftover randomness: each pixel's few samples differ from the true average by a random amount, so the image is speckled with noise. Denoising removes it by borrowing information from neighbouring pixels and from previous frames.

Everyday comparison

An opinion poll. Ask one person per street and each street's result is a coin toss; ask a thousand per street and it is accurate, at a thousand times the effort. Path tracing is the poll; samples per pixel are people per street. Denoising is noticing that neighbouring streets of similar houses vote alike and pooling their answers.

The problem it solves

Every technique in Part 8 handles one kind of light with its own approximation: probes for diffuse bounce, screen-space or ray-traced reflections, AO for contact. Path tracing computes all of them, every bounce, in one method, with no leaks and no blind spots. That is why film uses it and why engines ship it as a reference.

Why a lead cares

Real-time path tracing exists in shipping games, but on top-end GPUs and only with heavy sampling tricks, an AI denoiser and upscaling. For most teams the engine's path tracer is a reference tool: it shows lighting artists what the real-time GI should look like and settles arguments about whether a bug is in the lighting or in the approximation. Know which of the two you are being sold.

3 How it works

A path tracer is a loop around the ray tracing of 8.6. The interesting parts are the choices it makes at every bounce, how its error shrinks with samples, and the tricks that make few samples go further.

1 One path, several bounces

A sample starts as a camera ray through a random point inside the pixel, which also smooths jagged edges. At each surface it hits, the path tracer does two things. It sends a shadow ray to a random point on a light, as a ray-traced shadow does, and adds that light if nothing blocks it. Then it picks a random new direction, weighted by the surface's BRDF, and continues. Each bounce multiplies what the path can still carry by the surface's colour, so a path loses energy as it goes. It ends when it reaches a light, leaves the scene, or runs out of allowed bounces.

2 Samples, and why noise falls so slowly

The number of paths averaged per pixel is the samples per pixel, or spp. Monte Carlo error falls with the square root of the number of samples: in the words of Physically Based Rendering, the error goes down at a rate of O(n−1/2). Four times the samples halve the noise. Going from 1 to 1,024 samples, a thousand times the work, cuts the noise by a factor of 32.

Maths The Monte Carlo estimator

To estimate the light arriving at a point, an integral over directions, take N random directions ωi, each drawn with probability density p(ωi), and average:

estimate = (1 / N) Σ f(ωi) / p(ωi)

Dividing by p corrects for directions that were picked more often than others, so the estimate is right on average whatever p is. Its variance falls as 1/N, and noise, the square root of variance, as 1/√N. A good p, close in shape to f, makes the variance small from the start: that is importance sampling, step 5.

Simplified

The demo's denoiser is a small spatial filter in the spirit of SVGF: it divides out the surface colour, then blends neighbours whose depth and normal match and whose brightness is within the local noise. Real-time denoisers also reuse previous frames, which this still image cannot show. The noise readout is the standard error of each pixel's average, combined over the image.

4 Fireflies

Some light is found only by rare paths. A floor next to a mirror sphere receives light that bounced off the mirror from a small, bright lamp. A random path finds that route very rarely, but when it does, it carries the lamp's full brightness. One sample hundreds of times brighter than its neighbours lands in one pixel, and that pixel turns white. These isolated bright pixels are fireflies. They average out only at enormous sample counts, and in animation they flicker from frame to frame.

Renderers fight fireflies three ways. Better sampling finds the rare light paths more often (step 5). Clamping caps how bright any one sample may be: Unreal's path tracer calls it Max Path Intensity and HDRP's Maximum Intensity. It works, but it throws away energy, so caustics and bright reflections come out darker. And denoisers try to spot and suppress outliers, which a plain blur cannot do: a blur spreads a firefly into a blob.

5 Importance sampling: ask the useful directions

Monte Carlo is right on average whichever directions it samples, as long as it corrects for how often it picks each one. So pick the directions that matter. Importance sampling draws directions in proportion to how much light they are likely to bring: more of them near the surface normal, where light counts most; along a shiny surface's reflection lobe; and straight at the lights, which is what the shadow ray in step 1 does. Aiming at lights is called next event estimation. Combining several strategies and weighting each where it does best is multiple importance sampling, from Eric Veach and Leonidas Guibas in 1995, and every production path tracer uses it.

6 ReSTIR: reuse good samples across pixels and frames

Aiming at "the light" stops working when a scene has thousands of lights, or millions of glowing triangles: which one should a pixel aim at? ReSTIR, from Benedikt Bitterli, Chris Wyman, Matt Pharr, Peter Shirley, Aaron Lefohn and Wojciech Jarosz (SIGGRAPH 2020), answers it by resampling. Each pixel looks at many cheap candidate lights, keeps one in proportion to how much it would contribute ignoring shadows, and remembers it in a tiny record called a reservoir. Then pixels borrow: each combines its reservoir with those of a few random neighbours, and with its own reservoir from the last frame. Only the final winner gets a shadow ray.

The paper reports scenes with up to 3.4 million dynamic emissive triangles rendered in under 50 ms per frame at 1920 × 1080, and the unbiased version reaching equal error 6 to 60 times faster than previous methods, on a GeForce RTX 2080 Ti or a Titan RTX. NVIDIA's RTXDI library is built on ReSTIR and has since added versions for bounced light (ReSTIR GI) and full paths (ReSTIR PT). The family is the main reason real-time path tracing with many lights became possible.

7 Denoisers: neighbours, history and guides

A real-time denoiser works from more than the noisy image. The rasterized G-buffer gives clean depth, normals and surface colour for every pixel, which say where one surface ends and the next begins. Motion vectors say where each pixel was last frame, so previous results can be reused. These guides let the denoiser average a lot of noisy samples without blurring across edges.

SVGF, spatiotemporal variance-guided filtering, by Christoph Schied and colleagues at NVIDIA (High Performance Graphics 2017), is the clearest example. It takes one path per pixel, accumulates each pixel's history with temporal accumulation, estimates how much each pixel's brightness varies, and runs a five-level à-trous filter, a blur whose taps spread further apart at each level, with edge-stopping on depth, normals and brightness. The paper reports about 10 ms at 1920 × 1080 on a Titan X (Pascal), with the filter alone averaging 4.4 ms at 1280 × 720. NVIDIA's open-source NRD library offers successors: ReLAX, an à-trous denoiser designed for RTXDI, ReBLUR, and SIGMA for shadows.

8 AI denoisers and ray reconstruction

An AI denoiser replaces hand-written filter rules with a neural network trained on pairs of noisy and clean images. Offline, Intel's Open Image Denoise is widely used: Unreal's default path tracer denoiser runs Open Image Denoise networks on the GPU, and HDRP recommends it for its path tracer. In real time, NVIDIA's ray reconstruction, introduced with DLSS 3.5 in 2023, replaces a game's several hand-tuned denoisers, one per ray-traced effect, with a single network trained on offline-rendered images; NVIDIA describes it as available on all GeForce RTX GPUs. AMD's FSR Ray Regeneration is a machine-learning denoiser for ray-traced lighting in its FSR SDK, which runs separately from upscaling or alongside it.

NVIDIA's own announcement names the weaknesses these networks target: hand-tuned temporal denoisers can introduce ghosting and remove dynamic effects, and spatial blending can blur detail. A network does not abolish those trade-offs; it moves them. When an AI denoiser fails, it fails in ways that are harder to tune by hand, so review it in motion, not in screenshots.

9 Reference rendering and real time

Path tracing appears in games in two very different roles. As reference rendering, the engine's path tracer accumulates thousands of samples while the camera stands still and restarts when anything moves; Epic describes Unreal's as being for final shots and ground truth comparisons, not gameplay. As real-time rendering, a game traces about one or two paths per pixel, samples lights with ReSTIR, denoises with a network, and upscales. NVIDIA's Cyberpunk 2077 Overdrive mode, which it calls full ray tracing, otherwise known as path tracing, recommended a GeForce RTX 40 series GPU with DLSS 3 at launch in 2023.

4 What it costs

Path tracing cost is a product: pixels × samples per pixel × bounces × rays per bounce. At 1080p, one sample per pixel with three bounces and a shadow ray at each is 2,073,600 × 3 × 2 = 12,441,600 rays a frame, six times a single ray-traced effect. A reference frame at 1,024 samples is 1,024 times that, about 12.7 billion rays, which is why it takes seconds to minutes rather than milliseconds.

  • BouncesEach bounce traces again, less coherently. Bright interiors keep paths alive longer; Epic recommends keeping diffuse base colours below 0.8 for the path tracer.

  • Materials at every hitA path shades a material at every bounce, so expensive materials cost several times over.
  • MotionReuse across frames is what makes one sample enough; fast motion and disocclusion take it away exactly when the image changes most.

The usual trade

Real-time path tracing replaces a stack of separate techniques, each with its own leaks and tuning, with one that is correct but noisy. You trade art-team time spent fixing light leaks and probe placement for GPU time and denoiser artifacts, and you narrow the hardware that can run it. Most games that offer it today offer it as an optional top setting.

5 How it breaks

Path tracing's own failures are noise and fireflies. Most of what players report, though, comes from the denoiser and the reuse across frames that hide the noise.

Fireflies: bright specks that flicker

Cause
Rare paths that reach a small, bright light, often through a mirror, glass or glossy metal. Bloom (9.4) can spread them into blobs.
Ask for
Better light sampling for that light, a firefly clamp (Max Path Intensity, Maximum Intensity) at the lowest value that does not visibly darken the scene, and denoiser outlier rejection.

Blotchy, boiling light in motion

Cause
The denoiser has turned fine grain into large patches that crawl as the noise changes each frame: too few samples for the light, or history lost to camera motion.
Ask for
Captures in motion, the samples per pixel for the worst lights, and whether temporal history is being reset too often.

Light smears behind moving objects

Cause
Temporal reuse keeps light, or a ReSTIR light choice, from where an object was. It is ghosting, and it shows most at disocclusion and on objects without correct motion vectors.
Ask for
History rejection settings, motion vectors on every moving object, including particles and animated materials, and slow-motion captures of the trail.

Lighting lags behind changes

Cause
Accumulation over many frames: a switched-off light or an opened door takes several frames to show, because old samples still dominate the average.
Ask for
History reset or faster blending on large lighting changes, and a recording of the change at the target frame rate.

A soft, waxy look: lost contact shadows

Cause
A spatial filter strong enough for the noise also smooths small, real lighting detail: contact shadows, creases, light in small gaps.
Ask for
Side-by-side comparison with the engine's reference path tracer, more samples rather than more blur where detail matters, and the denoiser's edge-stopping settings.

The reference path tracer is the diagnostic tool here too: if the reference shows the same thing, it is the content or the lighting; if only the real-time image does, it is sampling or denoising.

7 Say it to your AI

Path tracing briefs need the purpose (reference stills, cinematics, or real-time), the sample and bounce budget, the denoiser, and what "clean enough" means. Ask for comparisons with a reference image, not adjectives.

BriefUse when setting up a lighting reference
Unreal Engine 5.8, Lumen GI and reflections in hardware mode. We want a path-traced reference for our five lighting test rooms, to compare against Lumen.

1. Give the project settings and Post Process Volume Path Tracing settings for reference stills: samples per pixel, max bounces, Max Path Intensity, denoiser.
2. Explain which settings would make the reference itself wrong (clamping too hard, too few bounces, emissive double-counting) and how to check.
3. Describe a repeatable capture: camera bookmarks, exposure locked, the same frame with Lumen and with the path tracer, exported for side-by-side review.
4. List what differences to expect between the two that are not bugs.
  • Saying it is a reference, not gameplay, gets settings tuned for correctness rather than speed.
  • Asking how the reference can be wrong guards against a clamped or under-bounced "ground truth".
  • Locking exposure makes the comparison about lighting, not brightness.
Bug reportUse when ray-traced lighting looks noisy or smeared
Unity 6.6, HDRP 17.6, ray-traced GI and reflections on, Performance mode, denoising on. 1440p, RTX 4070.
1. In the neon alley, the walls shimmer with bright specks that come and go.
2. When the player runs past a neon sign, a glow trails behind them for about half a second.

For each problem, say whether it is a sampling problem, a denoising problem or a reuse-over-time problem, and why. Propose fixes in order of visual risk with their GPU cost. Tell us what to capture to confirm the cause, including a comparison with HDRP's path tracer for the same view.
  • Separating sampling, denoising and reuse makes the AI reason about which part is failing.
  • Small bright neon is a classic firefly source; the trail is classic temporal reuse.
  • The path tracer comparison tells you whether the content itself is the problem.

Questions to ask when reviewing path tracing and denoising work

  1. How many samples per pixel, how many bounces, and is anything clamped?Clamps and bounce limits change the answer, not just the noise.
  2. How are lights chosen when there are many of them?Without importance sampling or resampling, many lights mean many rays or a lot of noise.
  3. What does the denoiser read, and does every moving thing write motion vectors?Missing guides cause blur across edges; missing motion vectors cause ghosting.
  4. How long does lighting take to respond to a sudden change?Reuse across frames trades noise for lag.
  5. Has the result been compared with a reference render, in motion?Denoised stills hide what players see when the camera moves.
  6. What runs on GPUs without the required features?AI denoisers and path tracing modes are tied to hardware generations.

8 Check yourself

  1. A still render at 64 samples per pixel is still too noisy. Roughly how many samples would halve the noise, and what does that say about brute force?

    Show answer

    About 256: noise falls with the square root of the samples, so halving it takes four times as many. Each further halving costs four times the last, which is why both offline and real-time renderers rely on better sampling and denoising rather than more samples alone.

  2. What problem does ReSTIR solve, and why does it need only one shadow ray per pixel?

    Show answer

    Choosing which of many lights to sample. Each pixel resamples many cheap candidates without tracing shadow rays, keeps a winner in a reservoir, then combines reservoirs from random neighbours and from the previous frame. Only the final winner is tested for visibility, so the shadow ray goes where it is most likely to matter.

  3. Why does a real-time denoiser divide out the albedo before filtering, and what does it need motion vectors for?

    Show answer

    So that it filters only the light, not the texture detail, which the G-buffer already knows exactly; the albedo is multiplied back afterwards. Motion vectors tell it where each pixel was last frame, so it can reuse its history; without them, moving objects smear.
