7.1 Lights

Every pixel starts with light. Engines offer a small kit of light types, from the sun to a glowing sign, and measure them in real units, so a lamp can be as bright as the real one. Each type looks different, and each costs something different, every frame.

1 The picture

2 In plain words

A light in a game engine is an instruction to the renderer: light arrives at surfaces from here, this bright, this colour. The lamp model you see in a scene is a separate object. The light itself is invisible, and the material of each surface decides what happens when its light lands (6.5 PBR materials).

Real light comes from everywhere and bounces endlessly. Renderers cannot afford that for every pixel, so they offer a few simple kinds of light whose effect on a surface has a quick formula: the sun, a bulb, a spotlight, a glowing panel, the sky. Each kind has a position or a direction, a brightness, a colour and a reach.

Everyday comparison

A photographer lighting a room works with the sun through the window, a bare bulb, a spotlight, a big softbox and the glow of the sky outside. Each gives a different kind of light: hard or soft, even or fading, aimed or everywhere.

Game engines hand lighting artists the same kit, with sliders where the photographer has stands and dimmers.

The problem it solves

A few light types with known maths make lighting fast enough to compute for millions of pixels, 60 times a second.

Real-world units on top make it consistent: a lamp set to the lumens on its box looks right next to a sun set to the lux of a real sunny day, in every level, whoever placed them.

Why a lead cares

Lights are among the most common things a lighting artist adds, and each dynamic light costs GPU time in every frame for every pixel it reaches. How many lights may overlap, which may cast shadows and which are baked are budget decisions with a visible price. Agreeing on physical units early keeps lighting consistent between artists and levels, and lets anyone check a value against a real lamp.

3 How it works

Start with the kinds of light, then how each fades, how bright it is, what colour it is and when its work is done.

1 The light types

  • A directional light is so far away that its rays arrive parallel and equally strong everywhere. It stands for the sun or the moon, and has a direction and a brightness but no position.
  • A point light shines from one point in all directions, like a bare bulb, and weakens with distance.
  • A spot light is a point light limited to a cone, like a torch or a stage spotlight.
  • An area light shines from a shape with real size, usually a rectangle such as a window or a light panel, sometimes a disc or a tube. Bigger sources give softer shadows and broader highlights.
  • A sky light adds soft light from the whole sky or the distant surroundings, usually from an image of the environment.
  • An emissive surface is a material that glows by itself, such as a screen or a neon sign. It always shows its glow, but it lights other surfaces only when the renderer computes global illumination.

Point and spot lights are also called punctual lights, because all their light leaves a single point. That makes them the cheapest local lights, and the least realistic up close.

The Lantern Courtyard below uses two of these: a low directional sun from beyond the wall, and a point light inside the lantern. Its direct-light view shows them alone.

2 Light falls off with distance

Light from a point spreads out over a sphere that grows with distance. Twice as far away, the same light covers four times the area, so each square metre gets a quarter as much. This is the inverse-square law: the light arriving equals the light's intensity divided by the distance squared. A lamp that puts 100 lux on a desk 1 m below it gives 25 lux at 2 m and about 6 lux at 4 m.

Light attenuation, or falloff, is the general name for how a light weakens with distance, and physically based engines use the inverse-square law for it. The law has one practical problem: it never reaches zero, so in principle every light touches every pixel in the world. Each local light therefore gets an attenuation radius, Unreal's Attenuation Radius and Unity's and Godot's Range, beyond which the renderer ignores it.

A radius that simply cuts the light off leaves a visible ring where it stops. Engines fade the light smoothly to zero instead. Epic's version, published in 2013, leaves most of the light's reach almost untouched, so an artist can shrink a radius for performance without the light looking dimmer.

Directional lights have no falloff and no radius. The sun is so far away that walking across a level changes the distance to it by nothing measurable.

Maths Inverse square, and Epic's window

light arriving (lux) = intensity (candelas) ÷ distance² (metres)

falloff = saturate(1 − (distance ÷ radius)⁴)² ÷ (distance² + 1)

The second formula is from Karis, "Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4" (2013). The saturate keeps the window between 0 and 1, and the + 1 stops the value exploding right next to the light. The courtyard's lantern uses the same window.

Simplified

The inverse-square law holds exactly only for light from a point. Close to a large lamp, or for a tightly focused searchlight beam, real light falls off differently, a point Frostbite's 2014 physically based rendering notes make explicit.

3 Spot light cones

A spot light has two angles. Inside the inner cone the light is at full strength; between the inner and outer cones it fades to nothing, which gives the pool of light its soft edge. Unreal calls them Inner Cone Angle and Outer Cone Angle, Unity Inner and Outer Spot Angle, and Godot uses Angle with Angle Attenuation for the softness.

Narrowing a cone changes its brightness in some setups and not in others. In Unreal, a spot light set in lumens squeezes those lumens into the cone, so a narrower cone makes a brighter pool. Godot's documentation says it treats the space outside the cone as if a perfect absorber surrounded the light, so the pool keeps its brightness as the cone changes. In Unreal, a spot light set in candelas, a measure of strength in one direction, keeps its brightness whatever the cone. Check how your engine measures the angles, too, before copying values between engines: Unreal measures from the centre line.

4 Area lights: soft shadows and broad highlights

Every real lamp has size. On a shiny surface its highlight is a small image of the lamp, and its shadows have a soft edge, the penumbra, where only part of the lamp is hidden. A point light has no size, so it gives a pin-prick highlight and razor-sharp shadows.

Area lights fix both, at a price. Unreal has Rect Lights, Unity HDRP has Rectangle, Tube and Disc area lights, and Godot 4.7 added AreaLight3D for rectangles. Shading a rectangle exactly is expensive, so real-time renderers approximate it; a widely used method is linearly transformed cosines (Heitz and colleagues, 2016). Epic's documentation says Rect Lights that are not baked are "typically much more expensive" than point or spot lights, and Godot's calls AreaLight3D its most expensive light to render in real time.

The cheaper middle ground is to give point and spot lights a size. Unreal's Source Radius, HDRP's Radius and Godot's Size make the highlight as broad as a lamp of that size would give and soften shadows, without the full cost of an area light. Directional lights have the same idea as an angle: Unreal's default Source Angle of 0.5357° is the size of the sun in the sky.

5 Sky light and glowing surfaces

Without a sky light, everything the sun does not reach directly is black, as in the courtyard's direct-light view. A sky light fills those areas with soft light from the environment around the scene. Unreal's Sky Light "captures the distant parts of your level and applies that to the scene as a light", either once or every frame with Real Time Capture. Unity takes it from the sky or a colour in the Lighting window (URP) or the sky in a Volume (HDRP), and Godot from the environment's sky or a colour.

A sky light adds the same light from the sky to every point that faces it, with nothing in the way. Where something does block it, such as under a table or in a crack, ambient occlusion darkens it (8.3 Ambient occlusion).

Emissive surfaces are set in the material: Unreal's Emissive Color, Unity's Emission, Godot's Emission. Values above 1 glow brighter than white and feed bloom. Whether they light anything else depends on global illumination. In Unreal, emissive materials light the scene through Lumen at no extra cost, though Epic warns that very small, very bright ones cause noise. In Unity they light other objects through baked or Enlighten real-time lightmaps, chosen per material. In Godot they need baked or screen-space indirect lighting.

6 Physical light units

For years engines measured lights in made-up numbers: intensity 1, intensity 5000. Physical light units replace them with the units lighting designers and light meters use, so values can be checked against the real world.

  • The lumen measures all the light a source gives out, in every direction. It is the number on a bulb's packaging.
  • The candela measures how strong a source is in one direction. A candle is about 1 candela. A point light of 1 candela gives 4π, about 12.6, lumens in total.
  • The lux measures light arriving on a surface: one lumen per square metre. It is what a light meter reads, and how engines set the sun.
  • The nit, a candela per square metre, measures how bright a surface looks: a screen, the sky, a glowing sign (3.2 Colour done right).

Unreal uses physical units by default: lux for the directional light, candelas per square metre for the sky light, and for point, spot and rect lights a choice of candelas (the default), lumens, EV or nits. EV is the photographer's exposure value, a scale on which each step doubles the light. HDRP also works in physical units, with EV100 as an option. URP's light intensity has no unit. Godot's physical light units are off until you switch them on in the project settings.

Units need scale and exposure

Physical units only work if the world is built to scale; HDRP's documentation asks for 1 Unity unit to equal 1 metre. And real light covers a huge range, so a scene lit with a 100,000 lux sun turns white until the camera's exposure is set for it, exactly as a real camera's would (9.1 HDR, exposure and tone mapping).

7 Colour temperature

Light sources are rarely pure white. A candle is orange, an overcast sky slightly blue. Lighting artists describe this tint with colour temperature, in kelvin: the temperature a glowing object would need to give off light of that colour. Heat a piece of metal and it glows dull red, then orange, then towards white and blue as it gets hotter, so low numbers are warm-looking and high numbers cool-looking.

All three engines offer a temperature setting next to the light colour: Unreal's Use Temperature and Temperature, Unity's Filter and Temperature, and Godot's Temperature once physical light units are on. Setting lights by temperature keeps a set of lamps consistent and makes it easy to match a real reference. The final look also depends on the camera's white balance, which shifts every colour at once (chapter 9.1).

8 IES profiles: the shape of a real fixture

A bare point light shines equally in every direction. Real fixtures do not: a lampshade, a reflector or a lens throws light in shaped fans. Manufacturers measure this and publish it as an IES light profile, a text file in the Illuminating Engineering Society's LM-63 format that lists the candela value at each angle around the fixture.

Unreal's point and spot lights take an IES Texture, and can use the file's own brightness in lumens. HDRP imports IES files as ready-made light prefabs for point, spot and rectangular area lights. URP and Godot have no IES import; textures projected through the light, called cookies in Unity and projectors in Godot, give a similar effect by hand.

9 Static and dynamic lights

A static light is baked: its effect is computed once, before the game runs, and stored in lightmaps. At runtime it costs almost nothing, but it cannot move or change. A dynamic light is computed every frame, so it can move, flicker, switch off and cast shadows from moving characters. Engines also offer a middle setting that computes direct light every frame and bakes only the bounce.

Which lights are baked shapes both the look and the budget of a game. Chapter 8.2 Baked lighting covers how baking works; the rest of this chapter is about the cost of the dynamic ones.

4 What it costs

The per-light cost of a dynamic light is roughly the number of pixels it reaches times the shading work for each, plus any shadow maps it has to render. Pixels inside a light's radius on screen run the BRDF from chapter 6.5 once more for that light; pixels outside it skip the light entirely, if the renderer can tell. How renderers find which lights touch which pixels is the subject of 7.3 Forward, deferred and clustered.

What makes the bill grow

  • OverlapEvery extra light on the same pixel repeats the shading work there. Rooms full of lamps hit this first.
  • RadiusA larger radius covers more pixels, and screen coverage is what costs, so keep each radius as small as the light's visible reach.
  • ShadowsEach shadow-casting light renders the scene again; a point light up to six times, into a cube map of 24 MB at 1024² and 32 bits.
  • ResolutionMore pixels means more work for every light, which is one reason upscaling helps lighting-heavy scenes.
  • Light typeArea lights and soft shadows cost more per pixel than point and spot lights.
  • Renderer limitsForward renderers cap lights per object: URP's Forward path allows one main and eight additional lights per object.

Budget lights the way you budget triangles

Within the frame budget, give each level a budget for dynamic lights that may overlap the same pixels and for shadow-casting lights in view, and make the light complexity view part of every lighting review. Ask whether each shadowed light needs its shadow and whether lights that never move can be baked. Those two questions settle most lighting performance problems before they reach the profiler.

5 How it breaks

Lighting bugs are usually visible from across the room. These six cover most of them.

A hard ring at the edge of the light

Cause
The light is cut off at its radius instead of fading smoothly to zero, or a non-physical falloff ends abruptly.
Ask for
A falloff that fades smoothly to zero at the radius, such as Epic's window, and a radius matched to the light's brightness.

White-out or black-out after a units change

Cause
Lights were switched to physical units, so the sun is now 100,000 lux, but exposure still expects the old values.
Ask for
Exposure settings chosen for the new range, and a check of every light against real references before anyone retunes them by eye.

Light shining through walls

Cause
A light without shadows lights everything within its radius, including rooms on the other side of a wall.
Ask for
Shadows on lights near thin walls, or radii that stay inside their room, and a list of which lights do neither.

Lights flickering on and off as you move

Cause
A forward renderer's per-object or per-view light limit is exceeded, so the engine drops different lights each frame. URP's Forward path allows eight additional lights per object; Godot's Mobile renderer eight omni and eight spot lights per mesh.
Ask for
A light complexity view of the spot, then the cheapest fix: smaller radii, split meshes, fewer lights, or a clustered path such as Forward+.

Pin-prick highlights and razor shadows

Cause
Point and spot lights with zero size. Artists then roughen materials to hide the tiny highlights, which breaks the materials everywhere else, as Epic's 2013 notes describe.
Ask for
Source sizes that match the real fixtures, and materials left at their plausible roughness.

Frame time jumps in one room

Cause
Many overlapping dynamic lights, several of them casting shadows, all in view at once.
Ask for
A capture of that view with the time per light and per shadow map, and a plan within the level's light budget: bake, merge, shrink or drop shadows.

Each engine can draw the overlap map directly: Unreal's Light Complexity view mode (Alt + 7), the Lighting Complexity material override in URP's Rendering Debugger, and Godot's Cluster Omni Lights and Cluster Spot Lights debug draw modes. A frame capture tool shows the time each shadow map takes.

7 Say it to your AI

Lighting briefs need the engine, render path and platform, the lights as numbers with their units, and the budget. Adapt these.

BriefUse when moving a project to physical light units
Engine: Unreal Engine 5.8. Our levels were lit with Unitless intensities. We are moving point, spot and rect lights to candelas and the sun to lux, so lighting matches between levels and artists.

1. For each level, list every light with its current intensity and the equivalent in its new unit, using Unreal's own conversion for each light type.
2. Flag values far from real references: a candle about 1 cd, household bulbs 600 to 1,200 lm, direct sun 32,000 to 100,000 lux.
3. Propose exposure settings that keep each level's current look, and mark the places that will need an artist's eye.
4. Do not change any light's colour, radius, mobility or shadow settings.
  • Real references give the AI a way to spot values that were wrong all along.
  • Asking for exposure up front avoids the white-out that follows a units change.
  • Fencing off the other settings keeps the change reviewable.
Bug reportUse when lights flicker or vanish as the camera moves
Unity 6.6, URP, Forward rendering path, PC. Torches along a castle corridor switch on and off as the player walks. Each torch is a Realtime point light with a 6 m Range; there are 14 in the corridor, and the floor is one large mesh.

Find the cause. Compare how many lights reach the floor mesh with the per-object limit of our rendering path, and show where the limit is hit with the Rendering Debugger's Lighting Complexity override. Then compare fixes (splitting the floor mesh, smaller ranges, baking some torches, switching to Forward+) with the GPU cost of each on our minimum spec.
  • Fourteen lights on one mesh, past an eight-light limit, is the likely cause; the numbers let the AI see it.
  • Naming the debug view asks for evidence before a fix.
  • Listing several fixes with costs turns it into a decision you can make.

Questions to ask when reviewing lighting work

  1. How many dynamic lights reach the busiest pixels, and where are they?Overlap is what costs; the light complexity view shows it in seconds.
  2. Which lights cast shadows, and does each one need to?A shadowed point light can render the scene six more times a frame.
  3. Are intensities in physical units, and do they match real references?Values tuned by eye drift apart between levels and artists.
  4. Is every attenuation radius as small as the light's visible reach?The radius sets how many pixels pay for the light.
  5. Which lights are baked, which dynamic, and why?A light that never moves rarely needs to cost GPU time every frame.
  6. Does each area light earn its extra cost over a point or spot light with a source size?The cheaper option often looks nearly the same.

8 Check yourself

  1. A lamp puts 100 lux on a desk 1 m below it. The desk is moved so it is 3 m from the lamp. Roughly how much light does it get now?

    Show answer

    About 11 lux. By the inverse-square law, three times the distance gives 1 ÷ 3² = one ninth of the light: 100 ÷ 9 ≈ 11.

  2. Why does a directional light have no attenuation radius?

    Show answer

    It stands for a source so far away, such as the sun, that its light arrives parallel and equally strong everywhere in the level. Moving across the level does not change the distance to it in any way that matters, so there is nothing to fade.

  3. In Unreal, a spot light set to 1,000 lumens has its outer cone narrowed from 44° to 15°. Does the pool of light get brighter, dimmer or stay the same? And if it were set in candelas?

    Show answer

    Brighter. In lumens, the same light is squeezed into a smaller solid angle: 2π(1 − cos θ) drops from 1.76 to 0.21 steradians, so the beam is about eight times as intense. Set in candelas, the strength per direction is fixed, so the pool keeps its brightness and only shrinks.
